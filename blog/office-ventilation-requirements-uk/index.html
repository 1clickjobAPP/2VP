Office Ventilation Requirements in the UK — A Fit-Out Guide

Office ventilation requirements in the UK come from Approved Document F: enough outdoor air per person, extract where pollutants arise, and commissioning proof.

By Vladimir Castravet 14 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Alexandra · N22 · whole-house renovation Shown illustratively · a 2VP home, not an office fit-out

Office ventilation requirements in the UK come from two sets of rules that often get mixed up. Part F of the Building Regulations 2010, with Approved Document F as its guidance, controls what new or altered ventilation must deliver when you do building work. The Workplace (Health, Safety and Welfare) Regulations 1992 require every enclosed workplace to be ventilated by a sufficient quantity of fresh or purified air, every day you occupy it. This guide gives you the rates, explains where meeting rooms and new partitions catch a fit-out out, sets out what your landlord’s base build does and does not cover, and lists what to ask for in writing before you sign. 2VP fits out small London offices, so it has an interest in how you plan this. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Two rulebooks. Part F of the Building Regulations 2010 governs the building work. Regulation 6 of the Workplace Regulations 1992 governs the office for as long as people work in it.
  • Outdoor air per person is the headline number. Approved Document F Volume 2 sets a minimum outdoor air supply for offices of 10 litres per second per person. Design for the number of people a room can hold, not the number on your org chart.
  • Extract where pollution is made. WCs, showers, tea points and print rooms each have their own extract rate, to outside.
  • Air conditioning is not ventilation. A split unit or cassette cools and recirculates room air. It adds no outdoor air on its own.
  • Partitions break open-plan systems. Every new meeting room needs its own supply and extract, rebalanced and recorded.
  • Get the base-build figures in writing. Ask your landlord for the design occupancy and the outdoor air rate per person the central plant was sized for.
  • Commissioning is the proof. Without measured flow rates on a commissioning record, you cannot show the system does what the drawing says.

What office ventilation requirements actually mean

Ventilation means replacing used indoor air with outdoor air. It dilutes the carbon dioxide people breathe out, along with moisture, odours and the pollutants given off by carpets, furniture, printers and cleaning products. Heating and cooling are different jobs, even when one machine does all three.

What does the law require for office ventilation?

Two layers apply. Requirement F1 of Schedule 1 to the Building Regulations 2010 requires adequate means of ventilation for people in the building. Approved Document F is the statutory guidance that shows one way of meeting it; the 2021 edition took effect in June 2022 and comes in two volumes. Volume 1 covers dwellings. Volume 2 covers buildings other than dwellings, and it is the one for your office.

Separately, regulation 6 of the Workplace (Health, Safety and Welfare) Regulations 1992 requires effective and suitable provision to ensure every enclosed workplace is ventilated by a sufficient quantity of fresh or purified air. The Building Regulations are judged when work is done and signed off. The Workplace Regulations apply every working day, and the duty sits with you as the employer or the person in control of the workplace. HSE’s Approved Code of Practice for those regulations (L24) gives a guide figure of 5 to 8 litres per second per occupant as a floor for an occupied workplace; the Approved Document F rate is the design figure for new work.

What is the difference between whole-building ventilation and extract?

  • Whole-building ventilation is the steady supply of outdoor air to every occupied space — desks, meeting rooms, reception.
  • Extract removes pollutants at their source and sends them to outside, never back into the office: WCs, showers, tea points, print rooms.
  • Purge ventilation clears a room quickly after a spill, a paint smell or an overheated afternoon, often through openable windows.

Does air conditioning count as ventilation?

Usually not on its own. Most office comfort cooling — wall-mounted split units, ceiling cassettes, many fan coil units — takes air from the room, cools or heats it, and blows it back. In a lot of London base builds, outdoor air is ducted to the floor from a central air handling unit and delivered next to the fan coils; the fan coil itself adds none. Ask which you have. If the outdoor air comes only from openable windows, that is natural ventilation, and it works only when someone opens them — which few people do in January or above a bus route.

The rates in Approved Document F Volume 2

For an office, Approved Document F Volume 2 sets a minimum outdoor air supply per person and minimum extract rates for the rooms that make pollution. These are the figures a fit-out most often turns on.

How much fresh air does an office need per person?

At least 10 litres per second per person of outdoor air under Approved Document F Volume 2. A 20-desk open-plan area needs at least 200 litres per second delivered into it. The number that catches tenants out is not the rate but the design occupancy: the number of people per square metre the landlord’s plant was sized for. If your layout puts people closer together than that, the system can meet its own specification and still feel stuffy by three in the afternoon. Ask for the base-build design occupancy and outdoor air rate per person, in writing, before you agree the layout. The open plan vs cellular office guide explains how layout choice changes the headcount a floor carries.

How do you size ventilation for a meeting room?

Size it on the seats, not on average use. An 8-seat room needs at least 8 × 10 = 80 litres per second of outdoor air, with extract to match so the room does not push air out under the door. Meeting rooms are where ventilation fails first: the door shuts, eight people sit for an hour, and the carbon dioxide climbs. A room carved out of an open floor often ends up under a single supply diffuser that was sized for two desks. Glass-fronted rooms gain heat from people, screens and sun as well, so cooling and ventilation have to be designed together.

Phone booths and pods are a smaller version of the same problem. Most have their own fans, but they draw from the office around them. Check the manufacturer’s stated air change rate, and that the space each booth draws from is itself ventilated.

Do offices need CO2 monitors?

The 2021 edition of Approved Document F Volume 2 added guidance on carbon dioxide monitoring in offices, so that occupants can see when a room is under-ventilated. Whether it applies to your project depends on the work you are doing; ask your building control body at the design stage. Either way, a monitor in each meeting room gives you evidence rather than complaints. HSE’s ventilation guidance treats carbon dioxide readings consistently above 1,500 parts per million as a sign that a space is poorly ventilated.

When office ventilation requirements apply in a London fit-out

Part F applies when you carry out building work that affects ventilation — installing or altering a mechanical ventilation system, or a change that leaves existing ventilation worse. The Workplace Regulations apply from the day your team moves in.

Does an office fit-out trigger Part F?

Often, at least in part. A typical London fit-out adds partitions, moves grilles, adds a tea point or a WC, and extends the landlord’s ductwork. Work like that is building work, notified to building control, and it must not leave the building less compliant with the ventilation requirement than it was before. Part L applies alongside it: new or replaced fans, controls and other fixed building services must meet the energy efficiency guidance in Approved Document L Volume 2, and the Building Regulations require those services to be commissioned, with notice given to building control. A purely decorative refresh — new carpet, paint, furniture — is not building work. The Workplace Regulations still apply to it.

What happens when you add partitions to an open-plan floor?

Each new room cuts off part of an air distribution system designed for an open floor. Three things go wrong:

  1. Grilles end up on the wrong side of the wall. A room can get supply with no extract, or extract with no supply.
  2. The return air path is blocked. Many base builds return air through the ceiling void. A partition built up to the slab for sound stops that, unless a transfer path is designed in.
  3. The system goes out of balance. Air takes the easiest route. Without rebalancing, open-plan desks get too much and the new rooms too little.

This is where ventilation and sound pull against each other. A transfer grille or a gap under the door lets air back to the open floor, but it lets speech through too. Acoustically lined transfer ducts solve both, and they need to be on the drawing before the partitions are ordered. The office acoustics design guide covers the sound side.

Who is responsible: landlord or tenant?

In a multi-let London building the split is usually this. The landlord’s base build provides the central plant, the risers and a set amount of outdoor air to your floor. Your fit-out provides the distribution on the floor — ductwork, diffusers, extract, controls — and any changes to it. Your lease and your licence to alter set out the detail, and most landlords require their own M&E consultant to approve your design. The licence to alter for an office fit out guide explains that approval route. Once you are in, the duty under regulation 6 of the Workplace Regulations sits with you as the employer, whatever the lease says about the plant.

What drives the cost of meeting the ventilation requirements

The cost is set by what the base build already gives you, and how far your layout moves away from it. 2VP does not publish prices in its guides; these are the drivers to price, and the questions to ask.

Base-build capacity. If the central plant has enough outdoor air for your headcount, the work is distribution on the floor. If it does not, you are into new plant, a landlord negotiation, or a smaller headcount.
Number of enclosed rooms. Each meeting room, office or booth adds supply, extract, controls and balancing. Ten small rooms cost more to ventilate than two large ones of the same total area.
Ceiling type. Ductwork above a suspended ceiling can be routed for function. An exposed soffit means every duct is on show, so it has to be coordinated, aligned and finished.
New extract routes. A new WC, shower or tea point needs a route to outside. A new louvre on the façade can need landlord consent and, in some buildings, planning permission.
Working in an occupied building. Tie-ins to the landlord’s system are often out of hours, and plant shutdowns have to be booked.
Commissioning and balancing. Measuring and adjusting every grille is a line on its own. It is the line most often left out of a low quote.

Ask for these in writing before you commit:

  • The base-build design occupancy and outdoor air rate per person, from the landlord or its agent.
  • An M&E design showing the outdoor air and extract rate for every room, against the Approved Document F figure.
  • Who pays the landlord’s M&E consultant to review your design.
  • The commissioning records, with measured flow rates, at handover.

Mechanical design and landlord approval take time as well as money. The office fit out timeline in London guide shows where they sit in the programme.

Frequently asked questions.

What are the minimum ventilation requirements for an office in the UK?

In England, requirement F1 of the Building Regulations 2010 requires adequate ventilation, and Approved Document F Volume 2 shows how: at least 10 litres per second per person of outdoor air, plus extract to outside from WCs, showers, tea points and busy print rooms. Separately, regulation 6 of the Workplace (Health, Safety and Welfare) Regulations 1992 requires every enclosed workplace to be ventilated by a sufficient quantity of fresh or purified air while it is in use.

How much fresh air per person does an office need?

Approved Document F Volume 2 sets a minimum of 10 litres per second per person of outdoor air for offices. Size each space on the maximum number of people it can hold: an 8-seat meeting room needs at least 80 litres per second. Check your layout against the occupancy the landlord’s plant was designed for.

Is air conditioning enough to ventilate an office?

Usually not on its own. Most split units, cassettes and fan coil units recirculate and cool room air without adding outdoor air. Outdoor air has to come from a separate supply — often a central air handling unit in the base build — or from openable windows. Ask your landlord which system your floor has.

Do meeting rooms need their own ventilation?

Yes. Each enclosed room needs its own outdoor air supply sized on its seats, extract to match, and a return air path that does not let sound through. Rooms carved out of an open-plan floor often end up under a single diffuser sized for two desks, which is why they go stuffy first.

Who is responsible for office ventilation, landlord or tenant?

In most multi-let London buildings the landlord provides the central plant and a set amount of outdoor air to your floor, and your fit-out provides and alters the distribution on the floor. The lease and licence to alter set out the split. Once you occupy the office, the duty under the Workplace Regulations 1992 sits with you as the employer.

Do I need building control for office ventilation work?

If your fit-out installs or alters a mechanical ventilation system, adds extract or changes ventilation in a way that could make it worse, it is building work and needs building control. A decorative refresh does not. Ask for the building control approval and the commissioning records, with measured flow rates, at handover.
