Loft Conversion vs Extension in London — which one to build

Loft conversion vs extension turns on the room you are missing: a loft adds bedrooms, an extension adds living space, then the house decides.

By Vladimir Castravet 17 October 2026 12 min read Updated October 2026
Moncrieff · SE15 · loft and extension Completed 2VP project · both routes on one house

A loft conversion vs extension decision is simpler than it looks: a loft adds bedrooms, an extension adds living space. Pick by the room you are missing. Then let the house decide — the head height under your roof, the depth of your garden, the planning status of your street, and where a new stair can land. This guide runs those tests in order, prices both routes on the same 2VP rate basis, and says plainly when the answer is neither. 2VP builds lofts and extensions and is paid on either. That is a commercial interest, declared here — and it is also why there is no reason for us to push you one way.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • The room decides first. Short of a bedroom and a bathroom: look up. Short of a kitchen the whole family can sit in: look back.
  • The roof decides second. The working rule of thumb is about 2.2m from the underside of the ridge to the top of the ceiling joists. Below that, a loft gets harder and dearer.
  • The garden and the street decide third. Permitted development allows a loft of 40m³ on a terrace (50m³ semi or detached) and a single-storey rear extension of 3m (6m under prior approval) on an attached house. A conservation area removes the loft allowance entirely.
  • Per square metre, the loft is cheaper. On 2VP’s estimator rate library, Balanced finish: £2,700/m² NET for a loft against £4,185/m² NET for a rear extension. For 30m² each, £81,000 against £125,550, VAT in addition.
  • The loft is quicker and easier to live through. 10–14 weeks on site, built largely from scaffold. A rear extension runs 14–20 weeks and takes your kitchen out.
  • Value is local. No national percentage tells you what your street will pay. A local agent’s comparables do.

What “loft conversion vs extension” actually means

The two are not interchangeable square metres. They put different rooms on different floors, and they change different parts of how you live.

A loft adds to the top of the house

Bedrooms, a shower room, a study. It leaves the ground floor as it is. On a typical London terrace a rear dormer gives one good double bedroom with an en-suite, sometimes two smaller rooms. An L-shaped dormer over the back addition gives more. A mansard gives the most, and needs planning permission to get it.

An extension adds to the bottom

Kitchen, dining, a family room, and the connection to the garden. It does not give you a bedroom unless you build two storeys, which 2VP’s house extension cost guide prices at £120k–£200k NET. On a Victorian terrace the extension is often a side return infill rather than a full-width rear box, and that changes the numbers again.

So the first question is not which is cheaper. It is which room you are short of. Write it as one sentence. “We need a third bedroom so the children stop sharing” points up. “We need a kitchen we can all sit in” points back. If you cannot write that sentence yet, you are not ready to price either.

Key takeaway A loft is the cheaper square metre, but only if the square metre you need is a bedroom. Buying the cheaper route to the wrong room is the expensive mistake.

When you need a loft conversion or an extension in London: the tests, in order

Run these in sequence. Each one can end the conversation for one option.

1. Head height under the roof

Stand in the loft and measure from the underside of the ridge timber to the top of the ceiling joists. About 2.2m is the figure most designers want before a loft is straightforward, because the new floor build-up and the insulation both eat into it. That is a rule of thumb, not a regulation — it is the same ridge-to-ceiling-joist test the Velux loft conversion guide uses.

What the regulations do fix is the stair. Approved Document K asks for 2.0m clear headroom over a stair. Where a loft conversion cannot achieve that, Approved Document K accepts 1.9m at the centreline of the stair and 1.8m at the side (Approved Document K, Diagram 1.3). Below the 2.2m rule of thumb the options are to lower the ceilings of the floor below, which costs you height in those bedrooms, or to rebuild the roof as a mansard. The dormer loft conversion guide takes each loft type through the headroom test.

2. Garden depth and permitted development

Under Class A of the GPDO 2015, a single-storey rear extension on an attached house can go 3m beyond the original rear wall (4m detached), or 6m (8m detached) under the prior approval process, with a maximum height of 4m and eaves no higher than 3m within 2m of a boundary. Extensions and outbuildings together may cover no more than half the land around the original house. Under Class B, a loft may add 40m³ on a terraced house and 50m³ on a semi or detached one. The permitted development rules guide has the full conditions.

Then look at the garden itself. On a 6m-deep London garden, a 3m extension takes half of it. A loft takes none.

3. Conservation area or Article 4 direction

Class B does not apply on Article 2(3) land, which includes conservation areas. A loft there needs planning permission, and many boroughs resist rear dormers that are visible from the street or the gardens behind. Class A loses side extensions and two-storey extensions on the same land, but a rear single-storey extension can still fall within it. An Article 4 direction can remove further rights street by street. Check your borough’s list before you assume anything; the conservation area loft conversion guide covers what usually gets approved.

4. Where the new stair lands

A loft needs a full stair, and it lands on the floor below. On most terraces it sits over the existing stair and takes roughly a box room’s worth of space, or a slice of the largest bedroom. Count honestly: a three-bed that loses its box room to the stair and gains a double with an en-suite has a better bedroom, not an extra one. An extension has no equivalent loss.

5. Party walls

A loft on a terrace usually puts steel beams into both party walls. That is work to a party structure under section 2 of the Party Wall etc. Act 1996, with at least two months’ notice. An extension usually means foundations within 3 metres of a neighbour’s building and deeper than theirs, which is section 6, with at least one month’s notice. On 2VP’s published figures, a party wall agreement costs £800–£1,500 per neighbour where one Agreed Surveyor acts for both sides.

6. Living through it

A loft is built largely from the outside. Scaffold goes up, materials go up it, and the house below stays usable until the stair is cut through near the end. A rear extension opens the back of the house and takes the kitchen out for most of the programme. Plan a temporary kitchen, or plan to move out.

7. The timeline

On 2VP’s estimator, a loft conversion runs 10–14 weeks on site and a single-storey rear extension 14–20 weeks. Design, any planning application and party wall notice periods come before either.

Same floor area, same finish: the extension costs £44,550 more NET, about 55% above the loft. The reason is physical. An extension needs new foundations, a new slab, new drainage, new walls, a new roof and usually a wall of glazing. Does a loft conversion add value?

You will find percentages online. This page does not quote one, because no national figure knows your street. What decides value is local and checkable.

  • The street ceiling. Every street has a top price — the most a house on it has sold for. Spend that takes your house past that ceiling is spend for living in, not for resale.
  • Bedrooms against reception space. Agents list and compare houses by bedroom count. A loft that turns a three-bed into a four-bed with a second bathroom moves the house into a different comparison set. An extension that creates a family kitchen makes a house more attractive to buy, but it does not change the bedroom count on the listing.
  • The stair cost. If the loft stair eats the box room, the bedroom count may not change at all. Check the drawing before you check the comparables.

The practical test: ask a local agent for two sets of comparables — recent sales on or near your street with the extra bedroom, and with the extended kitchen. The gap between those prices and your house as it stands is one side of the sum. The NET build cost, plus VAT and fees, is the other. Do not take a builder’s word for the value side, including ours.

Doing both: the order that saves money

If you expect to do both within a few years, do them together, or do the loft first. Three things are shared when they run as one job:

  1. Scaffold and site set-up. Preliminaries — project management, scaffold, skips, welfare — are 18% of net build on a loft and 12% on a rear extension in 2VP’s estimator stage split. Run together, part of that is paid once.
  2. One round of party wall notices. One set of surveyors, one schedule of condition per neighbour.
  3. One disruption window. You live through one programme, not two.

If you have to stage it, the loft first is usually the cleaner order. It is built from scaffold and keeps the ground floor working; the extension later does not disturb a finished loft. The reverse order means a second scaffold and a loft stair landing in a newly decorated hallway.

The published figure is £140k–£175k whole-house, ex VAT · 14 weeks on site. The loft and the extension ran inside one 14-week programme, not end to end.

When neither is the answer

The case against building at all deserves a fair hearing.

  • The missing room may be a layout problem. A dining room nobody eats in, a back reception used as a corridor, a kitchen in the wrong half of the house. Taking out a wall and moving the kitchen costs less than either new structure. 2VP’s estimator prices whole-house renovation at £2,565/m² NET Balanced — less than either the loft or the extension rate — and the full house renovation cost guide breaks it down room by room.
  • You plan to move within two or three years. You carry the disruption and the fees, and the next owner gets the room.
  • The roof fails the head-height test and the street is a conservation area. The loft becomes a mansard through planning, at £110k–£175k NET, and the garden may be too small to extend into. That is the point to ask whether the house is the right house.

If the honest answer is reconfiguration, we will say so. It is a smaller job, and it is the right one.

Frequently asked questions.

Is a loft conversion cheaper than an extension?

Per square metre, yes. On 2VP’s own estimator rate library, Balanced finish in outer London, a loft conversion is £2,700 per m² NET and a single-storey rear extension is £4,185 per m² NET. For 30m² of each that is £81,000 against £125,550, VAT in addition. The extension needs new foundations, a slab, drainage, walls and a roof; the loft reuses most of what is already there. Cheaper only matters if the loft gives you the room you are actually missing.

Does a loft conversion add value?

It can, but no national percentage will tell you how much on your street. Value is capped by the street ceiling — the highest price a comparable house nearby has sold for. A loft that adds a bedroom and a bathroom moves the house into a different comparison set; one whose stair swallows the box room may not change the bedroom count at all. Ask a local agent for recent comparables with and without the extra bedroom, and set that gap against the NET build cost plus VAT and fees.

How much head height do I need for a loft conversion?

The working rule of thumb is about 2.2m from the underside of the ridge to the top of the ceiling joists, because the new floor and insulation take height. That is not a regulation. What the regulations fix is the stair: Approved Document K asks for 2.0m clear headroom, and where a loft conversion cannot achieve it accepts 1.9m at the centreline of the stair and 1.8m at the side (Diagram 1.3). Below the rule of thumb, the options are lowering the ceilings below or a mansard.

Can I do a loft conversion and an extension under permitted development?

Often, because they fall under separate classes of the GPDO 2015, each with its own limits. Class B allows a loft of 40m³ on a terraced house and 50m³ on a semi or detached house. Class A allows a single-storey rear extension of 3m on an attached house, or 6m under prior approval. Neither the Class B loft nor side and two-storey extensions are available on Article 2(3) land such as a conservation area, and an Article 4 direction can remove more. A lawful development certificate confirms your position before you build.

Which is more disruptive to live through, a loft or an extension?

The extension. A loft is built largely from scaffold, and the house below stays usable until the stair is cut through near the end. A rear extension opens the back of the house and takes the kitchen out for most of the programme, so you need a temporary kitchen or somewhere else to live.

How long does a loft conversion take compared with an extension?

On 2VP’s estimator, a loft conversion runs 10–14 weeks on site and a single-storey rear extension 14–20 weeks. Design, any planning application and party wall notice periods — two months for work to a party wall, one month for excavation near a neighbour — come before either. Done together as one job, they can share a programme: Amott Road in Peckham, with a dormer loft and a rear and side infill extension, was 14 weeks on site.
