Hidden Renovation Costs London Homeowners Miss

Hidden renovation costs are the items outside the build price — fees, surveys, permits, VAT, contingency and living costs — that London homeowners most often miss.

By Vladimir Castravet 17 November 2026 12 min read Updated November 2026
Alexandra · N22 · whole-house renovation Completed · 2VP project

Hidden renovation costs are rarely hidden on purpose. They sit outside the builder’s price because they are paid to someone else — a surveyor, the council, a utility company, HMRC — or because nobody can see them until the floor is up. Add them up on a London house and they can change the budget decision. This guide lists the costs homeowners most often miss, what drives each one, and what to ask for in writing so you can put a number on it before you sign. It gives no price figures: the right number depends on your house, and a figure that is wrong for you is worse than none.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Professional fees. Measured survey, design, structural engineer, and possibly a party wall surveyor for each neighbour.
  • Statutory fees. Planning, building control and, on some extensions, the Community Infrastructure Levy.
  • Surveys before work. Asbestos on older homes, and a drainage CCTV survey where you build near drains.
  • What’s behind the walls. Rotten joists, failed lintels, damp, old wiring and lead pipes. That is what contingency is for.
  • Utilities and permits. Supply upgrades, meter moves, skip and scaffold licences, parking suspensions.
  • VAT. At the standard rate of 20% (HMRC), on a quote shown NET it is the single largest addition.
  • Living costs. Somewhere else to live, storage, eating out while the kitchen is gone.

What hidden renovation costs actually mean

They are every cost of the project that is not in the builder’s price. Some are genuinely unknowable until work starts. Most are perfectly knowable, just paid to a different person, so they drop off a budget built from one quote.

Why are they missing from the quote?

Three reasons. First, some are not the builder’s to charge: party wall surveyors, council fees and utility connections are paid by you to third parties. Second, some are excluded because they cannot be priced until the house is opened up. Third, some are left out to make a quote look lower — which is why two quotes for the same job can differ so much. The two quotes, same job guide shows how to compare what is inside each price.

Known unknowns and unknown unknowns

Treat the list in two halves. Known costs — fees, permits, VAT, surveys — can be priced before you sign; get a written figure for each. Unknown costs — what is behind the plaster — cannot be priced, but they can be budgeted for, and the tool for that is contingency.

When hidden costs hit on a London project

They arrive in three waves. Knowing when each lands helps you hold the cash for it.

  1. Before work starts. Survey, design and engineer’s fees; planning and building control; party wall surveyors; asbestos and drainage surveys. Most must be paid before a builder can start.
  2. In the first weeks on site. Strip-out and opening-up reveal the condition of joists, lintels, walls, wiring and pipes. This is when contingency gets spent.
  3. Near the end. Utility connections, finishes upgrades chosen late, decorating rooms you did not plan to touch, furniture, and the move back in.

A payment schedule that matches the work as it is done keeps you from paying for the second and third waves twice. See builder payment schedules and front-loaded pricing.

The list: hidden renovation costs, and what drives each one

Here is every item that most often goes missing from a London renovation budget, with what makes it bigger or smaller and how to pin it down.

1. Party wall surveyors

If your work touches a party wall — a steel beam bearing into it, a loft, digging new foundations close to a neighbour’s — the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 applies. If a neighbour does not consent, surveyors are appointed, and the building owner normally pays the reasonable costs of the neighbour’s surveyor as well as their own. What drives it: the number of neighbours affected, whether they agree to one surveyor or each appoint their own, and how complex the work is. The party wall agreement cost guide goes through it.

2. Building control fees

Most renovation work that changes structure, drainage, fire safety or insulation needs building regulations approval. What drives it: each council and each registered building control approver sets its own charges, by type and size of work. The route matters too — full plans, building notice or a private approver. Ask who is applying and whether the fee is in the quote or paid by you.

3. Structural engineer

Any wall removed, opening widened, loft floor added or foundation dug needs calculations. What drives it: the number of steels and openings, the condition of the existing structure, and whether site visits during the build are included. A design that changes after the engineer has finished means paying for revised calculations.

4. Survey and design fees

A measured survey, the design drawings, planning drawings and, on bigger jobs, a building regulations package. What drives it: the size of the house, how much of it changes, and whether the designer stays involved during the build. In a design & build contract these may sit inside the price; in a traditional route they are separate.

5. Planning fees and the Community Infrastructure Levy

A householder planning application carries a fee published nationally; check the current figure on the Planning Portal. Larger extensions can also attract the Community Infrastructure Levy. Exemptions exist for some self-build residential extensions, but generally they must be claimed before work starts. What drives it: whether you need permission at all, the size of the extension, and your borough.

6. Asbestos survey and removal

Homes built or refurbished before 2000 may contain asbestos — in textured ceiling coatings, floor tiles, boiler cupboards, pipe lagging and old roof sheets. The Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012 apply to work that could disturb it, and a refurbishment survey before strip-out is how you find out. What drives it: the age of the house and its past alterations, how much of it you are opening up, and what is found. Some materials can be removed by a trained contractor; others need a licensed one.

7. Drainage surveys and build-over approval

Victorian drains run under back additions and gardens, often shared with neighbours. A CCTV survey before you design shows the route and condition. If you build over or close to a public sewer, Thames Water’s approval is needed. What drives it: where the drains run, their condition, whether they are shared, and whether the extension sits on top of them. A collapsed or root-filled drain found mid-build costs more than one found by survey.

8. What’s behind the walls and under the floors

This is the cost no survey sees: rotten joist ends, a cracked lintel, damp in a solid wall, wiring that has to be replaced once exposed, a lead water pipe under the hall. Cracks over openings are often the clue to a lintel problem; see cracks above windows and doors. What drives it: the age of the house, past alterations, and how much of it you open up. A house that has been well maintained hides less.

9. Utility upgrades

A bigger kitchen, an induction hob, a heat pump or an EV charger can need more power than the house has. What drives it: the size of the incoming electricity supply and whether it is shared with next door, which in some older terraces means the network operator has to split it first; a gas meter that has to move; a lead or undersized water main. Low pressure at the top of the house is another; see check water pressure before you renovate. Utility companies set their own lead times, so ask early.

10. Skip, scaffold and parking permits

A skip on the road, scaffolding over the pavement and a suspended parking bay for deliveries each need a licence from the council. What drives it: your borough’s charges, how long each is needed, whether you are on a red route or a busy high street, and whether there is room off the road. Ask whether permits are in the quote and for how many weeks.

11. Temporary accommodation and storage

Living through a full renovation is possible for some jobs and impossible for others. What drives it: whether the kitchen, the only bathroom or the heating will be out of action, dust and noise, any asbestos removal, how long the work runs, and whether you have children or pets. Add storage for furniture, and the cost of eating out while the kitchen is gone.

12. Making good, decoration and finishes

New work next to old work shows. Rooms you did not plan to touch get cables chased through them, or need redecorating to match. Flooring continuity across a new opening is a classic late addition. What drives it: how far the work spreads beyond the rooms in scope, and whether finishes were specified or left as allowances.

13. Allowances that turn out too small

A quote may carry a provisional sum or an allowance for items not yet chosen — a kitchen, tiles, sanitaryware, ironmongery. If what you choose costs more than the allowance, you pay the difference. What drives it: how realistic the allowance was, and how many items were left unchosen at contract. Choose the big items before you sign where you can.

14. Changes you make during the build

Moving a socket is cheap before the walls are plastered and expensive after. What drives it: how late the change is made, and what it affects downstream. Agree every change in writing, with its cost and its effect on the programme, before the work is done.

15. VAT

Most building work on an existing home is charged at the standard rate of 20% (HMRC). If your quote is shown NET, VAT is added on top, and on a large project it is the biggest single item people forget. A few kinds of work qualify for a reduced or zero rate — for example some renovations of homes that have been empty for a long time — but check HMRC’s guidance before you assume your job does. Ask every quote to show VAT as its own line.

16. Insurance and the furniture budget

Tell your home insurer before work starts; some policies change when a house is unoccupied or under major works. And remember the items outside any build: curtains, blinds, lighting fittings you chose yourself, furniture for new rooms, and the garden after the scaffold comes down.

How much contingency should you hold?

Enough to cover what nobody can see, and no more. The 2VP estimator carries 10% contingency by default, for the risk on a defined, measured scope. For pre-1940 housing, 2VP’s own research puts it at 15–20%, because period houses hide defects that only show once plaster and floors come up. Contingency is not a fund for upgrades; if it is spent on a better kitchen, it is not there when the joists turn out to be rotten.

The counter-case: a quote with everything in it is not always the better quote

A quote that includes every fee and a large contingency will look higher than one that leaves them out. That does not make it more expensive; it makes it more honest. Equally, a quote padded with vague allowances is not safer. The quote to prefer is the one where you can see every line, know who pays what, and see how contingency is held and reported.

Frequently asked questions.

What are the hidden costs of renovating a house?

The costs outside the builder’s price: party wall surveyors, building control and planning fees, the structural engineer, asbestos and drainage surveys, utility upgrades, skip and scaffold permits, temporary accommodation and storage, decoration beyond the rooms in scope, allowances that prove too small, changes during the build, VAT and contingency for what is found behind walls and floors.

How much contingency should I add to a renovation?

The 2VP estimator carries 10% contingency by default for a defined, measured scope. For pre-1940 London housing, 2VP’s own research puts it at 15–20%, because period houses hide defects that only show once plaster and floors come up. Keep it for the unknown, not for upgrades.

Is VAT included in a builder’s quote?

Not always. Most building work on an existing home is charged at the standard rate of 20% (HMRC). 2VP shows every price NET with VAT in addition. Ask every builder to show VAT as its own line so you compare like with like.

Who pays the neighbour’s party wall surveyor?

Under the Party Wall etc. Act 1996, the building owner — the person doing the work — normally pays the reasonable costs of the adjoining owner’s surveyor as well as their own. The number of neighbours and whether they agree to one surveyor drive the total.

Do I need an asbestos survey before renovating?

If your home was built or refurbished before 2000, a refurbishment survey before strip-out is how you find out whether work will disturb asbestos. The Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012 apply to that work, and what is found decides how it must be removed.

Do I need to move out during a renovation?

It depends on the scope and the programme. If the kitchen, the only bathroom or the heating will be out of action, or asbestos is being removed, moving out is often the practical choice. Ask for the programme dates of those stages before you decide.
