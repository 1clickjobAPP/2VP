How to Vet a Builder in London: The Eight Checks That Matter

How to vet a builder in London: check the company, all five insurances, recent references, a live site and an itemised quote, in writing.

By Vladimir Castravet 3 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Amott Road · Peckham SE15 · dormer loft and rear and side infill Completed · 2VP project

Knowing how to vet a builder in London comes down to checking, in writing, that the company is real, insured, competent for your job and willing to put its price and payment terms on paper before you pay anything. None of it needs a professional. Most of the checks are free public registers and a handful of direct questions, and together they take an evening. This guide gives you eight checks in the order to make them, the red flags that should end the conversation, and what a properly vetted builder’s quote should contain. 2VP is a builder you might be vetting, so it has an interest here. Use every check below on 2VP too.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Check the company. Look it up on Companies House: name, number, age, filed accounts, directors. A sole trader should give you a trading address and, if VAT-registered, a VAT number you can check.
  • See all five insurance certificates. Public liability, employer’s liability, contractors all-risk, professional indemnity and product liability, each in the company’s name and in date.
  • Check the registers. Gas work by a Gas Safe registered engineer; electrical work notified through a competent person scheme or building control.
  • Talk to recent clients and visit a live site. A tidy, protected site tells you more than a portfolio.
  • Get an itemised quote on your drawings. With exclusions, provisional sums and VAT spelled out.
  • Read the payment terms and the contract before you pay a deposit. Payments should follow completed work, with retention held back at the end.

What vetting a builder actually means

Vetting answers three questions, in this order. Is this company who it says it is? Can it do your job? And will the paperwork protect you if something goes wrong? A builder who passes the first two but will not commit the third to writing has not passed.

Is vetting the same as comparing quotes?

No. Comparing quotes tells you about price; vetting tells you whether the price will hold and the work will be done properly. Vet first, so you only spend time comparing quotes from builders you would actually hire. Then compare them line by line — why two quotes for the same job differ shows what to look for.

Are trade association logos proof a builder is good?

They are a starting point, not proof. Schemes such as TrustMark and trade bodies such as the Federation of Master Builders carry out checks before they admit a member, and some offer dispute routes. But a logo on a van or a website can be out of date or simply copied. Check membership on the scheme’s own website, and ask what the scheme does for you if there is a dispute.

How do I choose a construction company in London rather than a sole trader?

Match the firm to the job. A single trade — a decorator, a plumber — can be a sole trader. A loft, an extension or a structural refurbishment involves several trades, an engineer, building control and often a party wall award, and needs someone who manages all of them. On a domestic project with more than one contractor, the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 put the client’s duties onto the principal contractor; the client dutyholder guide explains what that means for you.

When you need to vet a builder in London

Before the first site visit for the basic checks, and before you sign or pay for everything else. The order matters, because each check is cheaper to make than the mistake it prevents.

  1. Before the site visit: Companies House, the website’s address and phone number, and recent reviews on platforms where reviewers are verified.
  2. At the site visit: who comes, what they ask about, whether they mention party walls, building control and access without being prompted.
  3. With the quote: insurance certificates, registrations, references and a live site you can visit.
  4. Before you sign: the contract, the payment schedule and the programme, read in full.

London adds its own checks. Ask how the builder will handle parking suspensions and skip or scaffold licences from your council, how materials get in on a terraced street with no side access, and how neighbours will be kept informed. A builder who has worked in London streets has an answer to each.

How many builders should I shortlist?

Three is a common choice: enough to see a spread, few enough to compare properly. What matters more is that all three price the same drawings and specification, otherwise you are comparing three different projects.

The eight checks, in order

Make them in this order. If a builder fails one, you can stop there.

1. Is the company real, and how long has it traded?

Search the company name on Companies House, which is free. Check that the company number, registered office and directors match what the builder gave you, how long the company has existed, and whether its accounts and confirmation statements are filed on time. Look at the directors’ other appointments: a run of dissolved companies is worth a question. If the builder trades as a sole trader, there will be no record, so ask for a trading address and, if they charge VAT, check the VAT number on HMRC’s online checker.

2. Are all five insurances in place?

Ask for the certificates, not a sentence saying “we’re insured”. You want public liability (damage to your property or neighbours’), employer’s liability (compulsory for a business with employees), contractors all-risk (the works themselves while they are being built), professional indemnity (where the builder designs, as on design and build) and product liability. Check each certificate is in the company’s name, covers the dates of your job and states the cover limit.

3. Are the right people registered for the regulated work?

Gas work must be done by a Gas Safe registered engineer: check the engineer’s card and the online register. Notifiable electrical work should be done by an electrician in a competent person scheme, who self-certifies it, or else checked by building control. Replacement windows are often notified through a competent person scheme too. Ask which subcontractors will do this work, and check them, not just the main contractor.

4. What do recent clients say?

Ask for two or three clients from the last year or two, on projects like yours, and speak to them. Ask specific questions: Did the job finish when planned, and if not, why? How were changes priced? Who ran the site day to day? How were snags dealt with? Would you hire them again for the next project? Specific answers are worth more than a general “they were lovely”.

5. What does a live site look like?

Visit a job in progress if you can. Look for protection on floors and stairs, a tidy and secure site, hoarding or barriers on the street, welfare facilities for the workers, and a site manager who knows what is happening that day. You are looking at what your house and your street will look like for months.

6. Is the quote itemised, on your drawings?

A quote you can vet has line items with quantities, refers to your drawings and specification by revision, lists exclusions, marks provisional sums, and states whether VAT is included or added. A one-line total is not a quote you can check. Compare what is excluded as carefully as what is included: the gap between two prices is usually in what one of them leaves out.

7. Do the payment terms follow the work?

Payments should be tied to stages that are complete, not to dates, and a small share should be retained until after the defects period. Be careful with a large up-front deposit that is not linked to materials ordered for your job. The guide to builder payment schedules and front-loaded pricing shows what a fair schedule looks like.

8. Will they sign a written contract with a named person in charge?

The contract should name the parties, the drawings, the price, the payment schedule, the programme, how changes are instructed and priced, the defects period and the warranty. It should also name who runs your project. If the builder visits your home and you sign there, consumer law may give you a cancellation period; ask the builder to explain it in writing before you sign.

What drives the cost of a vetted builder

Vetting itself costs you time, not money: Companies House and the Gas Safe register are free to search. What it changes is which quotes you compare, and the builders who pass tend to price things that the ones who fail leave out. Expect these lines in a vetted builder’s quote:

  • Insurance. Five policies cost money, and that cost sits in the price.
  • Site management. A named project manager and a site supervisor are real time on your job.
  • Preliminaries. Scaffold, protection, skips, welfare, licences and parking suspensions. A quote that has none of these lines has either hidden them or not allowed for them.
  • Certification. Gas, electrical and building control sign-off, and the paperwork at handover.
  • Provisional sums. Honest allowances for what nobody can price until the walls are open. Ask what each is based on.

Frequently asked questions.

How do I check a builder is legitimate?

Look the company up on Companies House and check the number, registered office, directors, age and filed accounts match what you were given. Ask for all five insurance certificates in the company’s name, check Gas Safe and electrical registrations for the people doing that work, speak to recent clients, and visit a live site. A sole trader should give you a trading address and a VAT number you can check if they charge VAT.

What insurance should a builder have?

Five policies: public liability, employer’s liability, contractors all-risk, professional indemnity and product liability. Employer’s liability is compulsory for a business with employees. Ask for the certificates, and check each is in the company’s name, covers the dates of your job and states the cover limit.

How many quotes should I get for building work?

Three is a common choice: enough to see a spread, few enough to compare properly. All three must price the same drawings and specification, or you are comparing different projects. Vet the builders first so you only compare quotes from firms you would actually hire.

What questions should I ask a builder before hiring?

Who will run the job day to day? Who serves the party wall notices and who is the building control body? What does the deposit pay for, and when is each stage payment due? How are changes instructed and priced? What is excluded from the quote? How long is the quote valid? Can I speak to two recent clients and see a live site?

How much deposit should I pay a builder?

Enough to cover what the builder has to commit to your job before work starts, and no more. Be careful with a large deposit not linked to materials ordered for you. On a 2VP contract the mobilisation deposit is 10%, stage payments follow a quality check, and 5% is retained until after the 12-month defects period.

How do I choose a construction company in London?

Match the firm to the job: a single trade can be a sole trader, but a loft, extension or structural refurbishment needs a company that manages several trades, the engineer, building control and the party wall process. Then run the same checks on each: company record, five insurances, registrations, references, a live site, an itemised quote, payment terms and a written contract with a named project manager.
