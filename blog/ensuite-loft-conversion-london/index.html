Ensuite Loft Conversion in London: Drainage, Headroom and Extract

An ensuite loft conversion in London works when the soil pipe can reach the stack, the shower has standing headroom and the fan vents outside.

By Vladimir Castravet 21 November 2026 9 min read Updated November 2026
Moncrieff · SE15 · loft conversion and extension Completed · 2VP project

An ensuite loft conversion turns a loft bedroom into a top-floor suite, and in most London houses it can be done. Whether it works well is decided by four things you cannot see on a mood board: where the waste goes, how much height you have where you stand to shower, where the steam goes, and whether your water supply can push a decent shower to the top of the house. This guide takes each one in turn, with the Approved Document behind it, what drives the cost, and what to ask your builder to show you in writing. 2VP builds loft conversions and bathrooms, so it has an interest in you building one. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Drainage decides the layout. The waste has to fall to the soil stack, and Approved Document H limits how long an unventilated branch pipe can be. Put the bathroom near the stack.
  • Shower where the height is. In a dormer that is under the flat roof. The WC and basin can sit lower under the slope.
  • Extract to outside. Approved Document F Volume 1, Table 1.1, sets a bathroom’s intermittent extract at 15 litres per second. Duct it outside, insulated where it crosses a cold void.
  • Test the water before you choose the shower. Combi and unvented systems run at mains pressure; a gravity system usually needs the tank moved and a pump.
  • Electrics are notifiable. Work in a room with a shower is covered by Approved Document P and BS 7671 Section 701.
  • Fix the bathroom before the steel. Its position changes the floor structure, the stair and the dormer. Late changes cost the most.

What an ensuite loft conversion actually involves

An en-suite in a loft is a bathroom built at the end of every service run in the house: the furthest point from the water main, the highest point above the drain, and usually under a sloping roof. That is why it needs planning before the rest of the loft is drawn, not after.

It involves four systems, each with its own Approved Document:

The four systems behind a loft en-suite
SystemThe questionWhere the rule sits
DrainageCan every fitting fall to the stack?Approved Document H
Headroom and layoutCan you stand to shower, and reach the room by a compliant stair?Approved Document K (stair); layout is design, not regulation
VentilationWhere does the moisture go?Approved Document F Volume 1
Water and hot waterIs there enough pressure, flow and hot water at the top?Approved Document G; the supply itself is a site test

On top of those sit the electrics — Approved Document P and BS 7671 — and the fire safety of the whole loft, which affects the bedroom door and the stair rather than the bathroom itself.

When an ensuite works in a London loft

An en-suite works in most London lofts that give a full-height bedroom: rear dormers, L-shaped dormers, hip-to-gable conversions and mansards. Roof-light-only lofts are harder, because the full-height strip is narrow.

Can you put an ensuite in a loft conversion?

Usually, yes. On a typical Victorian terrace the soil stack runs up the rear wall near the existing bathroom, and the loft bathroom can sit almost directly above it. An L-shaped dormer loft conversion adds a second dormer over the rear projection, which often gives the en-suite its own full-height space without taking from the bedroom.

Where does the waste go from a loft bathroom?

Into the existing soil and vent stack, by branch pipes that fall the whole way. Approved Document H limits how far an unventilated branch can run from each fitting before it needs extra ventilation — the basin’s branch is the shortest, the WC’s the longest. So the closer each fitting is to the stack, the simpler the job. Where the stack is on the other side of the house, the options are a ventilated branch, a new stack, or moving the bathroom.

Is a macerator toilet allowed?

Approved Document H accepts a WC with a macerator and pump only where there is also a WC in the building that drains by gravity — which your existing bathroom normally is. A macerator is a fallback when the stack is out of reach. It is noisier, needs a power supply, and needs servicing. It is not a reason to put the bathroom anywhere you like.

How much headroom do you need for a shower?

The Building Regulations do not set a headroom figure over a shower. A common rule of thumb is to plan for about 2.0 to 2.1m of clear height where you stand, and to use the lower part of the slope for the WC cistern, the basin, a niche or storage. In a dormer, the shower goes under the flat dormer roof. The stair is different: Approved Document K, Diagram 1.3, allows a loft conversion stair a headroom of 2.0m, or 1.9m at the centre line reducing to 1.8m at the side.

The four checks, in order

Do these four checks before the layout is drawn. Each one can move the bathroom, and the bathroom’s position changes the floor joists, the steels and the dormer.

1. The drainage route

  • Find the stack. Outside on the rear wall, or boxed in inside. Note where it is relative to the new floor.
  • Plan the falls. Every branch has to fall continuously to the stack. A shower tray or a wet-room floor needs depth below it for the trap and the fall — which the new floor build-up has to allow for.
  • Check the branch lengths. Against Approved Document H, fitting by fitting.
  • Keep the traps sealed. Long or badly vented branches can pull the water out of a trap and let drain air into the room. If a branch is long, it needs ventilating, not just extending.
  • Avoid cutting joists to fit pipes. Pipe routes through joists need the structural engineer’s say, not the plumber’s.

2. Headroom and layout

  • Draw a section, not just a plan. The plan shows floor; the section shows where you can stand.
  • Shower at full height, WC and basin lower. Doors where the ceiling is high enough to walk through.
  • Leave room for the boxing. The soil pipe, the extract duct and any pump take space.
  • Think about the bedroom too. An en-suite that takes the bedroom below a usable double size is a poor trade.

3. Extract and condensation

  • Size the fan. Approved Document F Volume 1, Table 1.1: 15 litres per second intermittent extract for a bathroom.
  • Duct it to outside. Through a dormer cheek, a tile vent or a soffit — never into the roof void.
  • Insulate the duct where it passes through a cold space, or the steam condenses inside it and runs back into the fan.
  • Mind the vapour. A loft bathroom sits right against the roof insulation. The vapour control layer behind the board has to be continuous, or moisture gets into the roof build-up.

4. Water pressure and hot water

The en-suite is the highest outlet in the house, so it gets the lowest pressure. Test before you choose a shower — the check water pressure before you renovate guide shows how to measure both pressure and flow.

Combi boilerMains pressure, so usually no pump. But a combi heats one outlet’s worth of water well; two showers at once may run short. Check its flow rate against what you plan to use.
Unvented cylinderMains pressure, stored hot water, good for more than one bathroom — if the incoming main can feed it. Approved Document G requires an unvented system to be installed by a competent person.
Gravity system (cold tank in the loft)The tank usually has to move, because the loft is now a room. A shower at the top of the house will usually need a pump, or the system needs replacing.

Electrics and fire

Lights, the fan and any shaver socket or towel rail sit in the bathroom zones set by BS 7671 Section 701, and work in a room with a bath or shower is notifiable under Approved Document P. On fire safety, the loft makes your house three storeys, so the stair has to be protected and the bedroom door fire-resisting; the en-suite opens off the bedroom, so it does not change the escape route. Where pipes and ducts pass through fire-resisting floors and walls, they need fire-stopping, photographed before it is closed in — see fire-stopping before the walls are closed.

What to ask your builder for in writing

Before you approve the layout, ask for these six things. A builder who has done the checks can answer each one in a line; one who has not will need to go back to site.

  1. The stack position marked on the loft plan, with the route and length of each branch pipe.
  2. A section through the shower showing the clear height where you stand and the floor build-up under the tray or wet-room former.
  3. The extract fan’s rating and duct route to outside, and where the duct is insulated.
  4. The pressure and flow readings taken at your house, and which shower and hot water system they support.
  5. Where the cold tank goes, if you have one, and whether the system changes.
  6. The certificates you will receive at handover: building control completion, Electrical Installation Certificate and, for any new boiler or cylinder, the commissioning record.

What drives the cost of a loft en-suite

A loft en-suite costs more than the same bathroom on the first floor, because of what it takes to get services up there. What moves the cost, roughly in order of size:

  • Distance from the stack. Near it: short branches. Far from it: a ventilated branch, a new stack or a macerator.
  • The hot water system. Keeping it, adding a pump, or replacing a gravity system with an unvented cylinder.
  • Shower type. A tray is simpler than a level-access wet room, which needs a deeper floor build-up and full tanking.
  • The finish. Tiles, or a jointless finish such as microcement — the microcement vs resin guide compares them.
  • Heating. A towel rail on the heating circuit or an electric one; electric underfloor heating under the tiles.
  • Sanitaryware and brassware. Wall-hung WCs need a frame and a stud wall deep enough to hold it.

The finish lines are the same as any bathroom, and the bathroom renovation cost in London guide covers what drives them. The loft-specific lines are the drainage and the water. Ask for those priced as separate lines in writing, so you can see what the en-suite adds to the loft.

Frequently asked questions.

Can you put an ensuite in a loft conversion?

Usually, yes. Most London dormer, L-shaped dormer, hip-to-gable and mansard lofts can take a shower room. Four things decide it: whether the waste can fall to the soil stack within the pipe lengths Approved Document H allows, whether there is standing headroom where you shower, whether the extract fan can vent outside, and whether your water supply gives a usable shower at the top of the house. Check all four before the layout is fixed.

How much headroom do you need for a shower in a loft?

There is no single Building Regulations figure for headroom over a shower. As a rule of thumb, plan for about 2.0 to 2.1m of clear height where you stand, and use the lower part of the slope for the WC, basin or storage. In a dormer, put the shower under the flat dormer roof, where the height is full.

Where does the waste go from a loft bathroom?

Normally into the existing soil and vent stack, by a branch pipe that falls all the way. Approved Document H limits how long an unventilated branch can be for each fitting, so the closer the bathroom sits to the stack, the simpler it is. If the stack is on the wrong side of the house, the options are a longer ventilated branch, a new stack, or rearranging the layout.

Do I need a pump for a loft shower?

It depends on your system. With a combi boiler or an unvented cylinder, the shower runs at mains pressure and a pump is usually not needed, provided the incoming pressure and flow are good enough at the top floor. With a gravity system and a cold tank in the loft, the tank often has to move, and the shower usually needs a pump. Test the pressure and flow before you choose the shower.

Is a macerator toilet allowed in a loft conversion?

Approved Document H accepts WCs with macerators and pumps only where there is also a WC in the building that drains by gravity. Most houses meet that through the existing bathroom. A macerator is noisier than a gravity WC and needs a power supply and servicing, so treat it as a fallback when the stack is out of reach, not the first choice.

Does a loft ensuite need building regulations approval?

The loft conversion as a whole needs building regulations approval, and the en-suite is part of it: drainage under Approved Document H, ventilation under Approved Document F, hot water safety under Approved Document G, and the electrics under Approved Document P and BS 7671. New circuits and work in a room with a bath or shower are notifiable, so collect the Electrical Installation Certificate and the compliance certificate at handover.
