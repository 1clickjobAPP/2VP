Basement Waterproofing BS 8102: How to Check the Design

Basement waterproofing, BS 8102 says, is a designed system: choose the grade of dry, combine protection types to reach it, then maintain it for life.

By Vladimir Castravet 17 October 2026 11 min read Updated October 2026
Amott Road · Peckham, SE15 · scope included cellar tanking · shown illustratively Architect Nick Elias · completed 2VP project

Basement waterproofing to BS 8102 is not a product you buy. It is a design: decide how dry the room has to be, assume the water will reach it, and combine protections so that one failure does not flood the floor. That is the short version of BS 8102:2022, the British Standard code of practice for protecting below-ground structures against water ingress. This page is the deep-dive behind the waterproofing section of our basement conversion in London guide, which covers the ground, the escape route and the party walls. Here you get the three types, the three grades, the pump in your floor, and eight steps to brief and check a design before you sign. 2VP builds basement conversions and the waterproofing inside them — declared, because some of the advice below is about spending less.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • BS 8102:2022 is a code of practice. Guidance, not law. It replaced the 2009 edition. Approved Document C points to it for floors and walls subject to groundwater pressure, at paragraphs 4.12 and 5.6.
  • Three types of protection. Type A, a barrier. Type B, a structure that resists water itself. Type C, a drained cavity that collects water and pumps it away.
  • Three grades of dry. Grade 1 tolerates some seepage. Grade 2 allows no water but tolerates damp. Grade 3 is a dry, habitable room.
  • Habitable means combined. A bedroom below ground normally gets two types working together, so that one can fail without the room flooding.
  • Type C is a machine. Pumps, an alarm, a battery backup, access points and an annual service.
  • The guarantee is part of the specification. Ask who stands behind it and whether it is insurance-backed — before the first membrane goes on.

What BS 8102 waterproofing actually means

BS 8102:2022 is titled Protection of below ground structures against water ingress — Code of practice. It is written as recommendations. Your building control body judges the work against Requirement C2 of the Building Regulations 2010 — resistance to moisture — and Approved Document C sends below-ground floors and walls under water pressure to BS 8102 rather than to its own standard damp-proofing provisions.

The standard asks two separate questions. Keep them separate and most of the confusion goes away.

Question one: how dry does the room have to be? The grade

BS 8102 grades of waterproofing protection — summarised
GradeWhat it allowsTypical use
Grade 1Some seepage and damp areas are tolerable, depending on the useCar parks, plant rooms without electrical equipment
Grade 2No water penetration; damp and moisture vapour tolerable; ventilation may be neededPlant rooms and workshops needing a drier environment, storage
Grade 3No water penetration; a dry environment, with ventilation, dehumidification or air conditioning to suit the useHabitable rooms — bedrooms, living space, a home office

This table summarises the grade descriptions. Read the grade table in BS 8102:2022 itself for the full wording, and let your waterproofing designer set the grade against your intended use.

Question two: how is that dryness delivered? The type

Type A — barrier protectionA membrane, coating or cementitious render applied to the structure, either on the outside face or the inside. It works only if it is continuous: every lap, corner and service penetration has to be right. An internal barrier on an old cellar wall is pushed by water pressure from behind, so it depends on a sound, well-prepared surface to hold onto.
Type B — structurally integral protectionThe concrete itself resists water: water-resisting concrete, waterbars at the joints, and crack widths controlled by design. It suits a new concrete box in a dig or a lowered floor. It is rarely an option for a converted Victorian cellar, because the walls are already there.
Type C — drained protectionAccepts that water will pass the structure and manages it. A studded cavity drain membrane lines the walls and floor, water runs behind it to channels at the base, and the channels drain to a sump where pumps lift it out. It tolerates old, uneven walls and slight movement, which is why it is so common in London conversions. It also needs maintaining.
Key takeaway The grade is about the room. The type is about the method. A quote that names a product without naming a grade has answered the wrong question first.

When you need BS 8102 waterproofing in London

Any room you intend to use below ground: a converted cellar, a lowered floor, a new basement, a lower-ground floor cut into a slope, the walls of a light well. If the space is habitable, the design is Grade 3 and the method follows from the ground.

Start from the ground investigation, not the product

The ground investigation tells the designer what the water is doing: the level, how it moves over the seasons, and what the soil is. Much of London sits on clay, which drains slowly. Water can collect in looser backfill against a basement wall and stand there even where the natural water table is deep. A monitoring standpipe read over weeks tells you more than a single reading on the day of the borehole.

Where the information is uncertain, good practice is to design for water to the full height of the retained ground. It is the conservative assumption, it covers a burst main or a blocked drain as well as groundwater, and it is the one we prefer. Ask your designer what water level they have assumed, and get the answer in writing.

Why habitable basements usually get two types

BS 8102 is built around risk: how likely water is to arrive, and what it costs if it gets in. A wet plant room is an inconvenience. A wet bedroom is a strip-out. For higher-risk, higher-consequence spaces, designers commonly combine two types:

  • Type A + Type C. A barrier on the structure and a drained cavity inside it. Common in cellar conversions: the barrier limits how much water reaches the cavity, and the cavity deals with what does.
  • Type B + Type C. A water-resisting concrete box with a cavity drain lining. Common where a new structure is cast.

Two types cost more than one. They also mean a single defect is not a flood.

The radon barrier and the waterproofing have to be designed together. A sump, a floor cavity and every pipe through the slab are possible paths for ground gas as well as water. The drainage answer matters twice: once for the toilet and shower, and once for where the pumped groundwater from a Type C system goes.

Approved Documents are statutory guidance to the Building Regulations 2010 for England. Compliance is judged against the Requirements in Schedule 1; the Approved Document shows one way of meeting them. Your building control body has the final word on your building.

Type C: living with a pump in your floor

A drained system is the most forgiving to install and the least forgiving to neglect. Before you accept one, check the design answers each of these:

  • Two pumps, not one. A duty pump and a standby, each able to handle the design inflow on its own.
  • A high-level alarm. One that tells you, audibly or by phone, when the sump is filling faster than it empties.
  • A battery backup. Storms bring both water and power cuts. A pump without backup fails exactly when it is needed.
  • Access points. Flushing or rodding points at the ends and corners of the perimeter channel, and a sump lid you can lift. Not tiled over, not under a fitted wardrobe.
  • A service schedule. Typically annual, to the manufacturer’s schedule. Channels can silt up with lime from new concrete or iron deposits from the ground, and the flushing points are how they are cleared.
  • A record. Keep every service sheet. Guarantees are commonly conditional on servicing, and a buyer’s surveyor will ask.

What basement waterproofing costs

2VP publishes rates it has billed. It publishes no basement rate, as the basement conversion guide explains, and no waterproofing rate either. A number invented for this page would be worth nothing to you. What can be stated is what moves the price, in order of weight:

  1. The types, and whether they are combined. Two systems cost more than one.
  2. The grade. Grade 3 brings ventilation or dehumidification with it.
  3. The area — walls as well as floor. A 5m × 8m cellar has a 40m² floor, but with 2.4m walls round a 26m perimeter it has another 62.4m² of wall: 102.4m² of surface to waterproof, two and a half times the floor area.
  4. Pumps and controls. Twin pumps, alarm, battery backup and the discharge route.
  5. Access. Materials in and spoil out through a terraced hallway take time.
  6. The condition of what is there. Old brick that needs preparation before any barrier will bond.
  7. The guarantee and the service contract. Both are part of the price, and both are worth paying for.

How to brief and check a BS 8102 waterproofing design

Eight steps. Use them on any designer and any contractor, including us.

  1. Write down the use and the grade. Bedroom, gym, wine store. The use sets the grade; the grade sets the system. Put it in the brief.
  2. Get the ground investigation and the water level in writing. Ask what groundwater level the design assumes. If the answer is uncertain, ask whether the design takes water to the full retained height.
  3. Put a waterproofing specialist in the design team early. At concept stage, not after the structure is drawn. Ask about qualifications — the Property Care Association’s Certified Surveyor in Structural Waterproofing (CSSW) is one to look for.
  4. Ask for the risk assessment and the reason for each type. For a habitable room, expect combined protection or a clear written reason why not.
  5. Check the junctions on the drawings. Wall to floor, wall to party wall, light well, door thresholds, every service entry, and where the radon membrane meets the waterproofing.
  6. Check the Type C maintenance design. Two pumps, alarm, battery backup, flushing points, the discharge route, and a service contract from day one.
  7. Agree the hold points before work starts. Each layer is inspected and photographed before it is covered: substrate, membrane laps and fixings, channels, sump and pumps tested with water.
  8. Get the guarantee in writing. Who issues it, what it covers — design, materials and workmanship — how long it runs, whether it is insurance-backed, and what voids it.

When Grade 3 is the wrong answer

The case for spending less is real.

  • A store is not a bedroom. A wine store, a bike store or a plant room may need only Grade 1 or Grade 2. Do not buy Grade 3 for a wine store. Record the use on the drawings, because changing it later means upgrading the system.
  • Damp in a dry cellar is not always groundwater. A bridged damp-proof course, garden levels raised against the wall, or a leaking gutter can all look like rising water. Fix the cause outside first; the damp-proof course and weep holes guide explains the 150mm rule.
  • A loft may be the better room. If you need a bedroom, a loft adds it above ground with no pumps to service. The basement guide makes that comparison on 2VP’s delivered loft rates, and the loft conversion vs extension guide runs the next decision.

Frequently asked questions.

What is BS 8102?

BS 8102:2022, Protection of below ground structures against water ingress — Code of practice, is the British Standard for keeping water out of basements and other below-ground structures. It replaced the 2009 edition. It is a code of practice — guidance, not law — but Approved Document C sends floors and walls subject to groundwater pressure to it, at paragraphs 4.12 and 5.6. It asks two questions: what grade of dry the space needs, and which types of protection will deliver it.

What are Type A, B and C waterproofing?

They are the three types of protection in BS 8102. Type A is barrier protection: a membrane, coating or tanking render applied to the structure. Type B is structurally integral protection: the concrete itself resists water, through water-resisting concrete and sealed joints. Type C is drained protection: a cavity drain membrane lets water pass the structure, collects it in channels and pumps it out from a sump. Habitable basements commonly combine two types.

What grade of waterproofing does a basement bedroom need?

Grade 3. BS 8102 describes Grade 3 as no water penetration and a dry environment, with ventilation, dehumidification or air conditioning to suit the use. Grade 2 allows no water but tolerates damp, which suits storage and plant rooms. Grade 1 tolerates some seepage. Set the grade from the use of the room, then choose the system.

Is a cavity drain membrane better than tanking?

Neither is better in general; they work differently. Tanking is a Type A barrier that must be continuous and well bonded to hold water back. A cavity drain membrane is Type C: it accepts water through the wall and manages it, which tolerates old, uneven London cellar walls. It also needs pumps, an alarm, a battery backup and an annual service. For a habitable room, designers commonly use both together so that one can fail without the room flooding.

Does a cavity drain system need maintenance?

Yes. A Type C system is a machine in your floor. Expect a service typically once a year to the manufacturer’s schedule: pumps tested, the sump cleaned, the alarm and battery backup checked, and the perimeter channels flushed through their access points. Guarantees are commonly conditional on that servicing, so keep every record.

How much does basement waterproofing cost in London?

2VP publishes rates it has billed, and it publishes no basement or waterproofing rate, so there is no 2VP figure on this page. The price is set by the types and whether they are combined, the grade, the area of wall as well as floor, the pumps and controls, access, the condition of the existing structure, and the guarantee and service contract. Wall area is easy to underestimate: a 5m × 8m cellar with 2.4m walls has 102.4m² of surface to waterproof, not 40m².

What should a basement waterproofing guarantee cover?

Design, materials and workmanship, in writing, with the name of who stands behind it. Ask how long it runs, whether it is insurance-backed so that it survives the installer ceasing to trade, and exactly what voids it — a missed annual service usually does. A product warranty from the membrane manufacturer covers the product, not the way it was installed.
