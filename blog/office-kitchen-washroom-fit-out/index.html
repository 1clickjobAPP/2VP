Office Kitchen Fit-Out and Washrooms — A London Tenant’s Guide

An office kitchen fit out is decided by drainage: site the tea point near a soil stack, then check toilet numbers against the HSE ratios.

By Vladimir Castravet 22 November 2026 10 min read Updated November 2026
Moncrieff · SE15 · completed 2VP residential project Photo · office fit-out photo to follow

An office kitchen fit out looks like the easy part of a new office — some units, a boiling-water tap, a fridge. It is usually the hardest part to put where you want it. Water has to get in, waste has to fall back to a soil stack, and both have to cross a floor you lease but do not own. Washrooms add a second layer: the law sets how many toilets and washbasins you need for the number of people on the floor, and your landlord’s core may or may not already provide them. This guide covers the tea point, the toilet numbers, the drainage, and the landlord’s consent, in the order they need deciding. 2VP fits out offices, including kitchens and washrooms, so it has an interest here. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Drainage decides where the kitchen goes. Put the tea point close to an existing soil stack or drainage point. Every metre away adds pipe, fall, risk and cost.
  • Toilet numbers are set by law. The HSE Approved Code of Practice to the Workplace Regulations 1992 sets minimum toilets and washbasins by headcount.
  • Check the core first. Most multi-let London buildings provide washrooms in the landlord’s core. Your job may be to check they are enough, not to build new ones.
  • An accessible toilet is expected. Approved Document M expects at least one wheelchair-accessible toilet where sanitary accommodation is provided.
  • Nothing gets connected without consent. New water, waste and extract connections almost always need the landlord’s written approval, usually a licence to alter.
  • Plan for the end of the lease. A kitchen you add may have to come out again. Find out now.

What an office kitchen fit-out actually means

An office kitchen fit-out is any work that brings water, waste and power together on your floor so people can make drinks and food. It ranges from a single sink to a full breakout space, and the scale changes what the building has to give you.

Tea point, kitchenette or breakout kitchen?

Tea point. A short run of units with a sink, a boiling or chilled water tap, an under-counter fridge and perhaps a dishwasher. One waste, one water feed, a few dedicated circuits.
Kitchenette. A longer run with a full-height fridge, a dishwasher, microwaves and storage, often with a table. More power, more waste load, and a case for its own extract.
Breakout kitchen. A social space with an island, seating and sometimes an oven or hob. Cooking brings in heat detection, fire risk, extract and the landlord’s insurer.

Is a kitchen part of Cat A or Cat B?

A kitchen is normally Cat B — your fit-out, not the landlord’s. A Cat A floor typically gives you the core washrooms, a capped water supply and perhaps a drainage connection point; some “Cat A+” floors arrive with a tea point already built. The guide to Cat A vs Cat B office fit-out explains where the line usually falls.

What the law asks for: toilets, washing and drinking water

The Workplace (Health, Safety and Welfare) Regulations 1992 require an employer to provide suitable and sufficient toilets (regulation 20), washing facilities (regulation 21) and drinking water (regulation 22), and suitable facilities for rest and to eat meals (regulation 25). The duty sits with you as the employer, even when the toilets are in the landlord’s core.

How many toilets does an office need by law?

The HSE’s Approved Code of Practice, Workplace health, safety and welfare (L24), sets the minimum. For a mixed workforce, or toilets used by women, it is:

Toilets used only by men can be counted with urinals under a separate table in the same Code. Use the number of people on the floor at your busiest, not your average hybrid day: the 30 people in on a Tuesday are the ones who queue.

What do washing facilities have to include?

Washstations need hot and cold, or warm, running water, soap and a way to dry hands, and they should be next to the toilets. Toilets and washrooms must be ventilated, lit, kept clean, and separate for men and women unless each toilet is in its own room with a door that locks from inside.

Does an office need an accessible toilet?

Approved Document M, Volume 2, expects at least one wheelchair-accessible unisex toilet where sanitary accommodation is provided, and the Equality Act 2010 places its own duties on you as an employer and service provider. In a multi-let building the accessible toilet is often in the core. If it is not, or your fit-out alters the washrooms, building control will expect it to be addressed. Newer guidance on toilet provision in non-domestic buildings, Approved Document T, may also apply to some work; ask your building control body.

Does an office have to provide drinking water?

Yes. Regulation 22 requires an adequate supply of wholesome drinking water, easily accessible and clearly marked where needed. A tea point with a mains-fed tap meets it. Bottled-water coolers are acceptable where a mains supply is not practical.

Drainage on a lettable floor — and the landlord’s consent

On a let floor, waste water has to reach the building’s soil stacks, which almost always run through the core. That single fact decides most kitchen layouts.

Can I put a kitchen anywhere on an office floor?

Only where the waste can fall back to a stack. Waste pipes need a continuous fall, so the further the sink is from the stack, the deeper the pipe has to be at its far end. On a raised access floor, that depth may fit in the floor void. On a solid screed, the pipe ends up in the ceiling void of the floor below — which belongs to your landlord, and may sit above another tenant’s office.

  • Close to the core: the simplest option. Short runs, gravity drainage, easy access for rodding.
  • Across a raised floor: workable if the void is deep enough for the fall. Check the depth on site, not on the marketing plan.
  • Through the ceiling below: needs the landlord’s consent, access to another tenant’s space, and fire-stopping where the pipe passes through the slab.
  • Pumped waste: a small pump unit lifts waste to a high-level pipe. It solves distance, but adds noise, a power supply, maintenance and a point of failure. Building regulations guidance treats pumping as the fallback where gravity is not practical.

What about water supply, hot water and leaks?

The water feed comes off the landlord’s riser, usually through a capped and valved connection on your floor. Boiling-water and filtered taps have to comply with the Water Supply (Water Fittings) Regulations 1999, including backflow protection. Hot water is normally point-of-use; any stored hot water brings Legionella control under the HSE’s Approved Code of Practice L8 into your maintenance plan. Fit an isolation valve you can reach, and consider leak detection under the units — a slow drip on a let floor can run for a weekend into the office below.

Extract, power and fire

A tea point with a kettle and a microwave is a heat and moisture source; a hob is a fire risk. Approved Document F, Volume 2, covers extract for kitchens and sanitary spaces in non-domestic buildings. Detection over a kitchen is normally a heat detector, not a smoke detector, so the toaster does not evacuate the building — but changing a head on the landlord’s fire alarm is the landlord’s system and needs their engineer. Boiling-water units, dishwashers and fridges each want their own circuit.

Who has to agree to the connections?

Assume every new connection to water, waste, extract or the fire alarm needs the landlord’s written consent. The lease will say how it is given; on most London office leases it is a formal licence to alter for an office fit-out.

Do I need landlord consent for an office kitchen?

Almost certainly. The landlord will want drawings showing the drainage route and connection point, product data for anything plumbed in, the method of fire-stopping, the fire alarm changes, and who will maintain it all. Expect the landlord’s surveyor or M&E consultant to review it, and allow time for that review in the programme — the office fit-out timeline in London shows where it sits.

Will I have to take the kitchen out at the end of the lease?

Possibly. Many leases require you to reinstate the floor to its original state, unless the landlord agrees in writing to keep your works. A kitchen with drainage through the ceiling below is a costly item to strip out. Ask the landlord, as part of the licence, whether they will accept the kitchen staying, and read the guide to dilapidations on an office lease before you sign.

What drives the cost of an office kitchen and washroom fit-out

The cost is driven far more by where the kitchen goes than by what it is made of. 2VP does not publish a rate for office kitchens; the same units can cost very differently on two floors of the same building. Ask any contractor to price these as separate lines:

  1. Distance to the stack. Short gravity runs are the simplest drainage on any floor. Long runs, ceiling-void routes and pumped units are where budgets go.
  2. Floor build-up. A raised floor with a deep void is easier to route through than a screed you have to core or chase.
  3. Work below your floor. Out-of-hours access to another tenant’s ceiling, fire-stopping through the slab, and making good their finishes.
  4. Appliances and taps. Boiling, chilled and sparkling water units, dishwashers and full-height fridges each add supply, waste or power.
  5. Washroom scope. Re-using the core washrooms costs little. Adding toilets, showers or an accessible WC on your floor is a different project.
  6. Landlord’s requirements. Their consultants’ review, their approved fire alarm engineer, and any specified products.
  7. Reinstatement. If the kitchen comes out at lease end, that is a future cost that belongs in today’s decision. The guide to office fit-out cost in London shows how these items sit in the wider budget.

Frequently asked questions.

How many toilets does an office need by law?

The HSE Approved Code of Practice to the Workplace (Health, Safety and Welfare) Regulations 1992 sets the minimum. For a mixed workforce it is one toilet and one washstation for up to 5 people, two for 6 to 25, three for 26 to 50, four for 51 to 75 and five for 76 to 100, then one more of each for every 25 people. Count your busiest day, not your average.

Can I put a kitchen anywhere on an office floor?

Only where the waste can fall back to a soil stack, which on most let floors runs through the core. A raised floor may give room for the pipe; on a solid floor the pipe may have to run through the ceiling of the floor below, which needs the landlord’s consent. A pumped waste unit can solve distance but adds noise, power and maintenance.

Do I need landlord consent for an office kitchen?

Almost always. New water, waste, extract and fire alarm connections touch the landlord’s services. Most London office leases require a formal licence to alter, and the landlord will want drawings, product data, fire-stopping details and a maintenance plan. Ask at the same time whether the kitchen can stay at the end of the lease.

Does an office need an accessible toilet?

Approved Document M, Volume 2, expects at least one wheelchair-accessible unisex toilet where sanitary accommodation is provided in buildings other than dwellings, and the Equality Act 2010 adds duties to employers. In multi-let buildings it is often in the landlord’s core. If your fit-out alters the washrooms, building control will expect accessible provision to be addressed.

Does an office have to provide drinking water?

Yes. Regulation 22 of the Workplace (Health, Safety and Welfare) Regulations 1992 requires an adequate supply of wholesome drinking water that is easily accessible. A mains-fed tap at a tea point meets it. Where a mains supply is not practical, bottled-water dispensers are acceptable.

Will I have to remove the office kitchen when the lease ends?

It depends on your lease and licence to alter. Many leases require the floor to be reinstated unless the landlord agrees in writing to keep the works. Ask the landlord, before you build, whether the kitchen can stay, and record the answer in the licence. Otherwise budget for stripping it out as part of your dilapidations.
