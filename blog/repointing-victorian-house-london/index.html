Repointing a Victorian House in London: Lime, Timing and Cost

Repointing a Victorian house in London means replacing worn joints with lime mortar softer than the brick, so the mortar weathers and the bricks survive.

By Vladimir Castravet 7 November 2026 12 min read Updated November 2026
Chester Square · Belgravia, SW1W · period street elevation 2VP project · shown for context, not a repointing job

Repointing a Victorian house in London is the job of cutting out mortar that has failed between the bricks and replacing it with new mortar, by hand, joint by joint. On a house built between the 1840s and 1900, the mortar that goes back in should almost always be lime, not cement, because the bricks are soft and the walls are solid. Get the mortar wrong and the wall looks smart for a few years, then the faces of the bricks start to flake away. Below: lime against cement, when to repoint, render removal, and what decides the price. The photo above is a 2VP project in Chester Square, SW1W, shown for context; it is not a repointing case study. 2VP sells this work, so weigh the advice knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Use lime, not cement. Victorian London stock bricks are soft and porous. The mortar must be softer and more breathable than the brick, so that it wears and the brick does not.
  • Repoint when the joints have failed, not on a schedule. Mortar you can scrape out with a key, joints eroded well back from the brick face, damp inside, plants in the wall: those are the signs.
  • Cement pointing is the most common cause of damage. It traps water behind the brick face, which then freezes and spalls.
  • Render removal is a decision, not a default. Some fronts were always rendered. Test a small panel first.
  • The cost is mostly time and access. Scaffold, how hard the old mortar is to remove, the joint finish, and brick repairs decide the price — the mortar itself is a small part of it.
  • Check permissions on the front. A conservation area, an Article 4 direction or a listed building changes what you can change.

What repointing a Victorian house actually means

A brick wall is two materials: the bricks and the mortar that beds them. In a Victorian house the wall is usually solid, often 9 inches thick, with no cavity. It stays dry by taking rain into its outer face and letting it evaporate out again. The mortar is the part designed to wear. When it erodes, you rake out the worn outer part of each joint and press in new mortar. That is repointing.

Why lime mortar, not cement?

Because the mortar has to be the weak link. Victorian London stock bricks — the yellow-brown bricks you see across south and west London — are relatively soft and porous. Lime mortar is softer still, slightly flexible and lets moisture pass through it. Water in the wall leaves through the joints, and if anything has to be sacrificed to frost and salts, it is the mortar, which is cheap to renew.

Cement mortar reverses that. It is harder and much less permeable than the brick. Water can no longer leave through the joints, so it leaves through the brick face instead, carrying salts with it. In winter that wet face freezes, and the outer few millimetres of the brick break away. This is called spalling, and once a brick has spalled it cannot be put back: it has to be cut out and replaced, or turned round if the back face is sound.

Which lime?

Most repointing in London uses a natural hydraulic lime, sold as NHL with a strength grade — NHL 2, NHL 3.5 or NHL 5 under BS EN 459-1 — or a non-hydraulic lime putty for sheltered or fine work. The right grade depends on the brick, how exposed the wall is (a chimney stack or a parapet takes more weather than a sheltered back wall), and what was there before. The sand matters as much as the lime: its colour and grain decide whether new joints match the old ones. A good contractor analyses or at least matches a sample of the original mortar, and builds a small test panel for you to approve before the wall is started.

What does tuck pointing mean?

Tuck pointing is a decorative Victorian finish, common on London front elevations. The joints are filled with a mortar coloured to match the brick, then a fine, crisp ribbon of white lime putty is “tucked” into a groove along the centre, so the brickwork reads as perfectly regular. It is skilled, slow work. Putting it back is a conservation choice and a cost driver.

When you need repointing in London

You need it when the joints have failed, which on lime-pointed walls can be many decades after the last time. Age alone is not a reason. Walk round the house with a key and a torch and look for these signs:

  1. Mortar you can scrape out. If a key drags sand out of the joint easily, the mortar has lost its binder.
  2. Joints eroded back. When the mortar sits well behind the brick face, rain runs into the recess and soaks the brick edges.
  3. Damp patches inside. Especially on chimney breasts, gable walls and below parapets. Damp low down near the floor is often a different problem: bridged or failed damp protection. The damp-proof course and weep holes guide explains how to tell the two apart.
  4. Plants and moss growing from the joints. Roots open the joint and keep it wet.
  5. Spalled bricks next to hard, grey joints. That pattern usually means an earlier cement repoint is doing damage now.
  6. Chimney stacks and parapets. They take the most weather and are usually the first to fail. Look at them from across the street, or with binoculars.

When is it not a pointing problem?

Stepped cracks running diagonally through the joints, cracks above windows and doors, or bricks bulging out of line are signs of movement, not worn mortar. Repointing over them hides the symptom for a season and leaves the cause. Read cracks above windows and doors before you book a pointer, and get the cause diagnosed — sometimes by a structural engineer — first.

How a Victorian wall should be repointed

  1. Survey and sample. Identify the brick, the existing mortar and any earlier cement repairs. Take a sample of the original mortar to match.
  2. Test panel. A small area raked and repointed in the proposed mix and joint finish, left to cure, and approved by you before the wall is started.
  3. Rake out by hand, or with great care. Remove mortar to a consistent depth — commonly guided at at least twice the height of the joint, and never less than about 15mm — without chipping the brick edges. Angle grinders on narrow joints widen them and damage the arrises; that damage is permanent.
  4. Clean out and dampen. Brush out the dust and wet the joint so the dry brick does not suck the water out of the new mortar.
  5. Fill in layers. Deep joints are packed in stages, each firmed up before the next.
  6. Finish the joint. To the approved profile, usually flush or very slightly recessed, or tuck pointed where that was the original. Never a raised “ribbon” joint that stands proud of the brick and sheds water onto it.
  7. Protect while it cures. Lime cures slowly. Cover the wall against sun, wind, driving rain and frost, and mist it where the supplier says to. Lime work should not go on when frost is forecast, and generally not below about 5°C.

Repointing on a like-for-like basis is a repair. It does not normally need building control or planning permission. A change of appearance can: see the permissions section below. Your local planning authority has the final word on your house.

Render removal and brick restoration

Removing cement render from Victorian brick can be the right decision, but it is a decision to test, not to assume. Many London fronts were rendered later to hide damp or tired brick. Hard cement render traps moisture in exactly the way cement pointing does, and taking it off can let a wall dry out. But it can also expose bricks that were never meant to be seen, damaged keying marks from when the render went on, and patches of later, mismatched brick.

Can you remove cement render from Victorian brick?

Often, yes — but only after a trial patch. Cement render bonded hard to soft brick can pull the brick face off with it. Open up a small, discreet area first and see what is underneath and how cleanly it comes away. If the face comes away with the render, you are choosing between replacing a large number of bricks, re-rendering in a breathable lime render, or leaving well alone. On a stucco-fronted house where the render is original, removing it is usually wrong, and in a conservation area it may need consent.

Brick cleaning and replacement

  • Cleaning. Use the gentlest method that works: low-pressure water, steam or a poultice. Abrasive blasting strips the fired outer skin off soft bricks and leaves them more porous than before.
  • Replacing spalled bricks. Cut out the damaged brick and replace it with a match — often a reclaimed London stock — or turn it round if the back is sound.
  • Matching. Colour, size and texture matter. Imperial bricks are a different size from modern metric ones, so a straight swap rarely sits right.

Do I need permission to repoint my house in London?

For like-for-like work on an ordinary house, usually not. Three situations change that:

  • A listed building. Any works that affect its character, which can include repointing in a different mortar or style, need listed building consent under the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Act 1990. Carrying out such work without consent is a criminal offence.
  • A conservation area with an Article 4 direction. Some boroughs remove the right to change the front of a house without planning permission. Removing render, painting brick or changing the pointing style can then need an application.
  • The pavement. Scaffold on the public highway needs a licence from the council. Your contractor should apply and allow for it in the programme.

If you live in a conservation area, the rules that govern a front elevation are the same rules covered in the conservation area loft conversion guide: check your borough’s appraisal and any Article 4 direction before the scaffold goes up.

What drives the cost of repointing

Is repointing expensive? It is labour-heavy, and the price is mostly time and access, not mortar. 2VP does not publish a per-square-metre rate for repointing on this page, because the spread between two walls of the same size is wider than any single figure would suggest. What you can do is understand the drivers, and make sure your quote shows each one as its own line.

How to keep the cost down without cutting corners

  • Share the scaffold. If you are planning roof repairs, new windows, gutters or a loft, do the pointing while the scaffold is up for them.
  • Repoint only what has failed. Sound lime joints can be left. Wholesale raking out of good mortar is wasted money and risks the bricks.
  • Compare like with like. A low quote often means a grinder, a cement mix or no test panel. Two quotes for the same job shows how to read the difference line by line.
  • Fold it into a wider project. If you are renovating inside as well, the full house renovation cost guide for London explains how external fabric repairs sit in a whole-house budget.

Frequently asked questions.

Is repointing expensive?

It is labour-heavy rather than material-heavy. Most of the price is scaffolding and access, the time taken to rake out the old mortar — much longer if it is hard cement — the joint finish and any brick replacement. Ask for each of those as its own line, with scaffold hire priced separately, so you can compare quotes properly and see where the money goes.

Should I use lime or cement mortar on a Victorian house?

Lime. Victorian London stock bricks are soft and porous, and solid walls need to dry out through their face. Lime mortar is softer and more breathable than the brick, so it wears instead of the brick. Cement mortar is harder and less permeable, so water and salts leave through the brick face instead, which then spalls in frost.

How do I know if my house needs repointing?

Scrape a joint with a key. If sand comes out easily, or the mortar sits well back from the brick face, the joints have failed. Other signs are damp patches inside on chimney breasts and gables, plants growing from the wall and spalled bricks next to hard grey joints. Diagonal stepped cracks are a movement problem, not a pointing one — get that diagnosed first.

Can you remove cement render from Victorian brick?

Often, but only after a trial patch. Cement render bonded to soft brick can pull the brick face off as it comes away. Open a small area first and see what is underneath. If the brick is damaged, the options are replacing bricks, re-rendering in breathable lime, or leaving the render in place. Where render is original, as on many stucco fronts, removing it is usually wrong.

Do I need permission to repoint my house in London?

Like-for-like repointing on an ordinary house does not normally need planning permission or building control. A listed building needs listed building consent for works that affect its character. In a conservation area with an Article 4 direction, changing the appearance of the front — removing render, painting brick or changing the pointing style — can need planning permission. Scaffold on the pavement needs a council licence.

How long does repointing last?

Well-made lime pointing on a sheltered wall can last many decades; exposed chimney stacks and parapets need attention sooner. The mortar is designed to be the part that wears, so it can be renewed without harming the brick. Cement pointing may look sound for longer while the bricks behind it deteriorate.
