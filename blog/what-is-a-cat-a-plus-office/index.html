What Is a Cat A+ Office? Plug-and-Play Space Explained

A Cat A+ office is Cat A space the landlord has already fitted with meeting rooms, kitchen, furniture and data, so you can move in.

By Vladimir Castravet 6 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Moncrieff · SE15 · loft and extension Shown for context · residential, not an office

A Cat A+ office is a floor the landlord has finished beyond the usual Cat A shell — meeting rooms built, a kitchen or tea point in, furniture on the floor and data live — so you can sign a lease and start working within days rather than months. London landlords are pre-fitting more floors this way, particularly smaller ones, because tenants downsizing into better space want to move fast and spend less up front; the fit-out market reports from Glenigan and GXI for 2026 both point to it. It can be a very good deal. It can also be someone else’s layout that you pay for in the rent and have to put back at the end. This guide explains what the term covers, when it suits you, and the questions to ask before you sign. 2VP fits out offices, including tenant changes to Cat A+ floors, so it has an interest here. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Cat A+ = Cat A plus the basics of a working office. Typically meeting rooms, a tea point, furniture, data cabling and Wi-Fi, all fitted by the landlord.
  • There is no official definition. One landlord’s Cat A+ is another’s “fitted” or “plug-and-play”. The only definition that counts is the fit-out schedule attached to your lease.
  • It is still a lease. Unlike a serviced office, you usually pay rent, rates and service charge separately and take on the lease obligations.
  • You trade control for speed. The layout is the landlord’s. Changing it needs consent, and you may have to reinstate it at the end.
  • Read three clauses before you sign: who owns the fit-out, who repairs it, and what you must hand back.

What a Cat A+ office actually means

The “categories” describe how far a floor has been finished, and who paid for it. Cat A is the landlord’s base; Cat B is the tenant’s fit-out on top. Cat A+ sits between them: the landlord goes some or all of the way into Cat B before the tenant arrives. The Cat A vs Cat B office fit out guide covers the two ends in detail.

What is the difference between Cat A and Cat A+?

Cat A gives you a clean, lit, heated and cooled floor with nothing to work at. Cat A+ adds what you need to work: rooms, a kitchen point, desks and chairs, and a live network. On a Cat A floor you hire a designer and a fit-out contractor before you move in. On a Cat A+ floor the landlord has already done that, to its own brief.

Is a Cat A+ office the same as a serviced office?

No. A serviced office is usually a licence: you pay one fee and the operator provides the space, the services and often the reception, and you can leave on short notice. A Cat A+ office is usually a conventional lease of a fitted floor: rent, business rates and service charge are separate, you insure your own contents, and the lease covenants — repair, alterations, reinstatement — apply to you.

Why landlords are pre-fitting more space

Three reasons. Smaller occupiers want to move quickly and keep cash for the business, not a fit-out. A fitted floor lets faster, which shortens the void. And a landlord-designed layout can be reused for the next tenant instead of being stripped out and rebuilt. For you, that means more choice of fitted floors than there was a few years ago — and more reason to read what “fitted” actually includes.

When a Cat A+ office makes sense in London

A Cat A+ office suits you when speed and low up-front cost matter more than a layout designed around your team.

  • You need to move fast. A lease event, a hire wave or a sublease ending can leave no time for design, licence and build. A fitted floor removes most of that — compare the office fit out timeline in London for a Cat A floor.
  • You are a small team. A standard layout of desks, a couple of meeting rooms and a tea point fits most small teams well enough.
  • The lease is short. Paying for your own fit-out makes less sense when you will be leaving in a few years and may have to strip it out.
  • You would rather pay over time. The fit-out cost is effectively spread through the rent instead of paid up front.

It suits you less when your work needs a specific layout — a lot of cellular offices, a client suite, secure rooms, heavy AV or specialist IT — or when the lease is long enough that a fit-out designed around you pays back. Then a Cat A floor and your own Cat B is usually the better route.

Is a Cat A+ office more expensive?

Usually the rent is higher than for the same building at Cat A, because the landlord is recovering what it spent on the fit-out. Whether that is more expensive overall depends on what you would have spent fitting out a Cat A floor yourself, how long you stay, and what you owe in dilapidations at the end. Put both options side by side over the full lease, not just year one.

What to check before you sign a Cat A+ lease

The fitted floor looks finished at the viewing. The lease decides what it means for you. These are the questions to put to the landlord’s agent, in writing, before heads of terms are agreed.

Who owns the fit-out in a Cat A+ office?

Usually the landlord, because the landlord paid for it. That matters in three ways. Ownership decides who repairs and replaces it when a chair breaks or a partition is damaged. It decides what you can remove when you leave. And it affects tax: who can claim capital allowances on fit-out depends on who incurred the expenditure, so ask your accountant before you assume you can. The lease should attach a fit-out schedule listing every item the landlord has provided.

Can I change a Cat A+ office?

Yes, with the landlord’s consent, and that consent usually comes as a licence to alter. Section 19(2) of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1927 means that where the lease requires consent for improvements, the landlord cannot unreasonably withhold it — but the landlord can still attach reasonable conditions, including reinstatement. Adding a booth or rebranding a wall is usually straightforward; moving meeting rooms built by the landlord is a bigger conversation. The licence to alter for an office fit out guide sets out the pack you will need.

What do you have to hand back?

This is where Cat A+ catches people out. Many leases require you to return the floor in the condition it was let — which, for a fitted floor, means the landlord’s meeting rooms, kitchen and furniture in good repair, and any of your own changes removed. Get a schedule of condition with photos on the day you move in, agree how wear and tear on the landlord’s furniture is treated, and read the dilapidations office lease guide before you sign, not before you leave.

The rest of the checklist

  1. The fit-out schedule. Every item listed, with make and model where it matters. If it is not on the schedule, assume it is not included.
  2. Occupancy. How many people the floor was designed for — escape routes, toilets in the core and fresh-air capacity all depend on it. Ask what number the fire strategy assumed.
  3. Meeting rooms. Are they ventilated and cooled on their own, and are the walls built to keep sound in? Rooms that are too warm or leak sound are common complaints.
  4. Data and Wi-Fi. Who provides the connection, on what contract, and can you bring your own provider and equipment?
  5. Repair and replacement. Who fixes the furniture, kitchen appliances and AV, and whether it goes through the service charge.
  6. Handover information. Under regulation 38 of the Building Regulations 2010, fire safety information has to be given to the responsible person when building work to which it applies is completed. Ask for it, with the O&M manuals, test certificates and warranties for the fit-out.
  7. The EPC. Under the MEES Regulations 2015, a landlord generally cannot let commercial property with an EPC rating below E unless a valid exemption is registered. Ask for the certificate. Proposals to raise that minimum are proposals, not law.

Often the change that costs you least is the one made before you sign. A landlord with a fitted floor that is not letting may move a wall or swap furniture to close the deal; the same change after completion needs a licence and your money. For what a full fit-out on a Cat A floor costs by comparison, see the office fit out cost London guide.

The counter-case: a fitted floor you do not like is not a saving

If you would rebuild half the rooms on day one, the Cat A+ floor is costing you twice: once in the rent premium for the landlord’s fit-out and again for your own. In that case, ask for the same floor at Cat A, or a different floor. The layout question is the same one as on any fit-out — the open plan vs cellular office guide is a good place to start it.
