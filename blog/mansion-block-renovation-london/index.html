Mansion Block Renovation in London — Licence, Lease, Floors and Noise

A mansion block renovation in London starts with the freeholder’s licence for alterations, then works to the lease’s rules on hours, floors and noise.

By Vladimir Castravet 27 November 2026 12 min read Updated November 2026
Alexandra · N22 · whole-house renovation Shown illustratively · not a mansion block

A mansion block renovation in London is a flat renovation with three extra parties at the table: the freeholder, the managing agent and the neighbours above, below and beside you. The building work itself is familiar — kitchens, bathrooms, rewiring, floors. What makes it different is everything you must agree before it starts, and the rules you work to once it does. Get the licence for alterations wrong and the job stops. Get the floor wrong and the complaint from downstairs can arrive years later, with your lease behind it. This guide is for owners of the red-brick and stucco blocks of Marylebone, Maida Vale, Kensington, Battersea and beyond. 2VP renovates London flats, so it has an interest in the subject. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Read your lease first. It says what you may alter, what needs consent, and what is forbidden outright. Structural work and moving bathrooms are the usual sticking points.
  • Get a licence for alterations. A formal written consent from the freeholder, usually through the managing agent, with drawings, a method statement and your contractor’s insurances. You pay the landlord’s fees.
  • Work to the building’s hours. Borough construction hours set the outer limit; the building’s own rules are often tighter, especially for noisy work.
  • Serve party wall notices where you cut into the walls or floors you share. In a block, the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 reaches upwards and downwards too.
  • Design the floor for the flat below. Many leases require carpet or an acoustic standard. A hard floor needs a resilient build-up, not just a thin underlay.
  • Survey for asbestos before anything is opened up in an older block.

What a mansion block renovation actually means

Mansion blocks are the purpose-built flats that went up across London from the late Victorian period into the 1930s: solid brick or stone, generous ceilings, often a porter, a lift and a communal staircase. You almost certainly own a long lease, not the building. The freeholder — a landlord, a company owned by the leaseholders, or an investor — owns the structure, the roof, the common parts and often the windows. A managing agent runs it day to day.

That ownership split decides what you can do. Inside your flat, your lease gives you the plaster, the floor finishes and the fittings. The walls between flats, the floors and ceilings, the structure and the drainage stacks usually belong to the freeholder, or are shared. Any work that touches them needs consent.

What is a licence for alterations?

A licence for alterations is the freeholder’s written consent to specific works, usually drawn up as a deed by the landlord’s solicitor. It lists the approved works, attaches the drawings, sets conditions — hours, protection of common parts, insurance, making good — and binds you, and later owners, to them. Without one, works that need consent are a breach of your lease, which can surface as a problem when you sell.

Do I need freeholder permission to renovate my flat?

For decoration and like-for-like replacement, usually not. For anything that touches the structure, the services, the windows or the floor finishes, usually yes. The lease decides. Many leases have a qualified covenant (no alterations without consent) and an absolute one for structural work (no structural alterations at all). Where a covenant is qualified, the Landlord and Tenant Act 1927 generally stops a landlord unreasonably refusing consent to improvements, but it does not stop them attaching conditions or charging their costs.

When you need it in London — the consents, in order

Start the consents before you finalise the design, because the freeholder’s answer can change it. Most licences take weeks rather than days; ask the managing agent for their usual timescale at the outset.

  1. Read the lease and the house rules of the block. Look for the alterations covenant, the flooring clause, any rule on where bathrooms and kitchens may sit, and the house rules on hours, lifts and deliveries.
  2. Talk to the managing agent early. Ask what they will want to see, who their surveyor is, whether a deposit or bond is required for damage to common parts, and how long approvals usually take.
  3. Prepare the application. Drawings, a specification, structural calculations where anything structural is proposed, a method statement, a programme, and your contractor’s insurance certificates.
  4. Agree the licence. The landlord’s surveyor reviews it; the solicitors draft the deed. You normally pay both sets of fees.
  5. Serve party wall notices on the flats above, below and beside you, and on the freeholder, where the Act applies.
  6. Apply for building control where the work is notifiable: structural changes, new drainage, electrical and gas work through a competent person, and any fire-separation changes.
  7. Tell the neighbours. A short letter with the start date, the hours and a phone number for the site manager does more for goodwill than anything else on this list.

What hours can builders work in a London flat?

Two sets of rules apply, and the stricter one wins. London boroughs use their powers under the Control of Pollution Act 1974 to control construction noise; the commonly applied hours are 8am to 6pm on weekdays and 8am to 1pm on Saturdays, with no noisy work on Sundays or bank holidays, but check your own borough. Your building’s rules often go further: a later start, noisy work such as drilling and breaking-out limited to set hours, and no work at all on Saturdays in some blocks. The licence will usually restate them.

Does the Party Wall Act apply to flats?

Yes. The Party Wall etc. Act 1996 treats the walls and floors that separate flats as party structures. Cutting into them — for a beam bearing, a deep chase, or removing a chimney breast — is notifiable work, and the owners on the other side, including leaseholders and the freeholder, are entitled to notice. Our party wall agreement guide explains notices, surveyors and timing.

Floors, noise and the flat below

The floor causes more mansion block disputes than anything else. Footsteps on a hard floor carry through the structure as impact sound, and the person who hears them is your downstairs neighbour, every evening.

Can I put wooden floors in a mansion block flat?

Only if your lease allows it, and usually only to a set standard. Many leases require floors to be carpeted with underlay, except in kitchens and bathrooms. Others allow hard floors if they achieve a stated acoustic performance, sometimes proved by a sound test before and after. If you replace a carpet with timber or tiles without consent, the neighbour can complain and the freeholder can require you to put it back.

What Approved Document E says, and does not say

Approved Document E is the Building Regulations guidance on sound. Its requirement for separating floors and walls applies to new flats and to flats formed by a change of use; refurbishing an existing flat is not normally caught. In practice, though, many leases and managing agents use its performance figures as the target for any new hard floor. For purpose-built flats, Approved Document E sets airborne sound insulation of at least 45 dB (DnT,w + Ctr) and impact sound of no more than 62 dB (L′nT,w) for separating floors. If your licence names a figure, design to it and test to it.

What a quiet floor build-up looks like

A quiet hard floor is a floating floor, isolated from the structure:

  • A resilient layer designed for impact sound, not a thin laminate underlay.
  • No rigid connections. The floating floor must not be screwed through to the structure, and skirtings and kitchen units sit on it or are fixed with a gap. One screw through the resilient layer can bridge it.
  • Perimeter strips so the floor does not touch the walls, which would carry sound sideways.
  • Mass where you can add it. Many Edwardian blocks have filler-joist floors of steel and concrete; others have timber joists. The build-up has to suit what is actually there, so open up a section before you design.

Every layer adds height. Underfloor heating adds more. Check door thresholds, the step at the front door and the kitchen plinths before the specification is fixed.

Bathrooms, kitchens and water

Moving a bathroom is the second most common sticking point. Many leases forbid putting a wet room over a neighbour’s bedroom, and new waste runs must reach the existing stacks with enough fall. A leak in a block is your neighbour’s ceiling, so waterproofing has to be done to the manufacturer’s system and tested before tiling. If the bathroom only needs a new surface, a new bathroom without removing tiles avoids weeks of noisy breaking-out. Upper-floor flats in older blocks can also have weak pressure: check water pressure before you renovate, before you choose a shower.

Fire, asbestos and the building’s safety rules

A block is a shared fire compartment system, so your works affect your neighbours’ safety as well as yours.

  • Compartment walls and floors. Every new hole for a pipe, cable or duct through a wall or floor between flats must be fire-stopped to restore the separation. Our guide to fire-stopping before walls are closed shows what good looks like.
  • The front door. Your flat entrance door is part of the building’s fire safety. Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, as clarified by the Fire Safety Act 2021, it falls within the responsible person’s duties. Do not change it, or its self-closer, without the freeholder’s consent.
  • Asbestos. Blocks of this age often had later refurbishments using asbestos-containing materials in floor tiles, textured coatings, pipe lagging or boxing. The Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012 make the managing agent responsible for the common parts; for your flat, commission a refurbishment survey of the areas to be disturbed before work starts.
  • Taller blocks. Under the Building Safety Act 2022, a building of at least 18m or seven storeys with two or more homes is a higher-risk building, and building control for work in it sits with the Building Safety Regulator. Some mansion blocks reach that height. Check before you apply.

Under the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015, a homeowner’s client duties normally pass to the contractor. The Building Regulations dutyholder rules work in a similar way; see client dutyholder under the Building Regulations.

What drives the cost of a mansion block renovation

The flat’s size and specification set the base. In a mansion block, these lines move the total most, and every one of them should appear in writing:

  1. The landlord’s fees. Surveyor and solicitor fees for the licence, and any deposit against damage to common parts.
  2. Restricted hours. Fewer noisy hours a day stretches the programme, and the programme drives the preliminaries.
  3. Logistics. No skip outside, a lift that cannot be used for materials, a parking bay suspension, protection of the common parts, and everything carried up stairs.
  4. The floor. An acoustic floating floor and any before-and-after sound test.
  5. Party wall surveyors for each adjoining owner who does not consent.
  6. Asbestos survey and removal, if any is found.
  7. What the walls hide. Old lead or iron pipework, rewiring to current standards, and making good original cornices and joinery.

It lists the approved works and drawings, and sets conditions such as working hours, protection of common parts, insurance and making good. You normally pay the landlord’s surveyor and legal fees.
