Design and Build vs Traditional Tender Cost in London

Design and build vs traditional tender cost turns on design risk: a tender price can look lower, but design gaps return as variations you pay.

By Vladimir Castravet 23 November 2026 9 min read Updated November 2026
Amott Road · Peckham SE15 · dormer loft and rear/side infill Architect · Nick Elias · built by 2VP

The honest answer to design and build vs traditional tender cost is that the cheaper route depends on where you measure. A traditional tender on a finished design often gives the lower starting price, because several builders price the same drawings. Design and build often gives the lower final cost, because one party carries the design risk, there are fewer separate fees, and design and construction overlap. This guide compares the two on the four things that move your final bill — fees, design risk, variations and programme — and shows you how to put a design and build price next to tender returns fairly. 2VP is a design and build contractor, so it has an interest in which route you choose. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Traditional tender: you appoint a designer, the design is finished, several contractors price it, you sign with one. You carry the design risk.
  • Design and build: one contractor designs and builds for one price. The contractor carries the design risk.
  • Fees: traditional usually means paying a designer through the build and a contract administrator to run the contract. Design and build folds the design into the contract.
  • Variations: in a tender, gaps in the drawings become variations you pay for. In design and build, only changes you make are variations.
  • Programme: traditional runs design, then tender, then build. Design and build overlaps them, so it is usually shorter.
  • Compare final likely cost, not the first number. Add fees, provisional sums, a realistic variation allowance and the cost of time.

What the two routes actually mean

The two routes differ in one thing above all: who is responsible if the design is wrong or incomplete. Everything else — fees, variations, time — follows from that.

Traditional tender

You appoint an architect or designer, who takes the project through planning and then produces a full set of construction drawings and a specification. Those go out to tender: several contractors price the same information, and you choose one. The contract — commonly a JCT form such as the Minor Works Building Contract, or one of the JCT contracts written for homeowners — is between you and the builder. Your architect, or a separate contract administrator, usually runs that contract for you: inspecting work, valuing payments, and issuing instructions for changes.

Design and build

You set out what you want — the brief, often with planning drawings already done — and one contractor takes responsibility for completing the design and building it. The JCT Design and Build Contract is the standard form; smaller domestic jobs often use simpler terms. You deal with one party for both the design and the build, and the contractor’s price covers both.

When each route suits a London home

Neither route is right for every job. The choice turns on how complete the design is, how much you want to control the detail, and how much risk you want to hold.

Is design and build cheaper?

Often by the end, rarely at the start. A competitive tender puts several builders against each other on the same drawings, and that pressure is real. But the tender price is the price for what is drawn. On a London refurbishment, much of what costs money is only found once the floors are up and the walls are open — and in a traditional contract, that risk is yours. Design and build prices that risk in at the start, which can make the first number higher and the last number lower.

When does a traditional tender make sense?

  • The design is genuinely complete — construction drawings, a full specification, structural details, services — before it goes out.
  • The building holds few unknowns, or they have been opened up and surveyed already.
  • You want control of every detail and are happy to pay a designer and contract administrator to keep it.
  • The building needs specialist design oversight — a listed house, for example — where you want your own architect checking every stage on your behalf.

When does design and build make sense?

  • You want one price and one party responsible for design and build.
  • Time matters — you are paying rent or carrying two homes while the works run.
  • The project is a familiar type — a loft, a rear extension, a whole-house refurbishment — where an experienced contractor can complete the detail without surprises.
  • You do not want to manage a contract between your designer and your builder.

Can I keep my architect?

Yes. Your architect can take the scheme through planning, then either stay on as your adviser — checking the contractor’s design and work for you — or be novated, which means the contractor takes over the appointment and the design responsibility for the build stage. Novation keeps the designer you chose and moves the design risk to the builder. Agree in writing what your architect will still do for you afterwards.

What drives the cost on each route

Four things decide what you actually pay, on either route. None of them is visible in a headline price.

1. Fees

On a traditional route, the designer stays with you through the build, and someone has to administer the contract: value each payment, inspect the work, issue instructions. That is usually your architect on a separate fee, sometimes a quantity surveyor too. In design and build, the design and the management sit inside the contract price. For reference, 2VP’s estimator allows 7% of the contract value, ex VAT, for architect or designer fees, within a range of 6–12% — that is the allowance 2VP uses, not a market survey. Ask every party you speak to what is in their fee and what is extra.

2. Design risk

This is the biggest difference and the least visible. If a drawing misses a steel, a drainage route clashes with a joist, or a detail cannot be built as shown, someone pays to fix it. In a traditional contract that someone is usually you, through a variation, and then you may have a claim against your designer. In design and build, it is the contractor’s problem, within the price. Under the JCT Design and Build Contract, the contractor’s design liability is generally the same standard of reasonable skill and care that applies to an architect — so it is a real transfer of responsibility, not a guarantee of perfection.

3. Variations and provisional sums

A variation is any change to the work after the contract is signed. A provisional sum is an allowance for something that could not be priced at tender — a kitchen not yet chosen, a repair not yet opened up — and is replaced later by the actual cost. Both are where tender prices grow. Count them: how many provisional sums does each price carry, and what is the basis for pricing variations? A low tender with many provisional sums is a less certain number than a higher price with few. The two quotes, same job guide shows how to read what is inside each price.

4. Programme

Time is cost. A traditional route runs in sequence: finish the design, issue the tender, allow several weeks for builders to price, compare and negotiate, then sign and start. Design and build overlaps those stages. On a London home, the cost of a longer programme lands on you as rent, a second mortgage, storage or simply months more of disruption — and on the contractor as time-related site costs it prices in. If the programme overruns on a traditional job because of design information arriving late, the delay is usually yours to pay for.

The counter-case: design and build can cost you controlA contractor completing the design will choose the detail you did not specify. If the specification is loose, “equivalent” products and simpler details are within its rights. The protection is a tight brief and specification at the start, and the right to approve key items — windows, steelwork, finishes — before they are ordered.
The counter-case: a tender can be the right testIf your design is complete and the building holds few unknowns, a competitive tender is a fair way to test the market. Several prices on identical information are hard to argue with.

How to compare a design and build price with tender prices

To compare the two fairly, turn each into a final likely cost. Do this on paper before you choose.

  1. Start from the same scope. The same drawings, specification and exclusions. If a design and build price covers detailed design and a tender does not, the tender is missing that cost.
  2. Add every fee. Designer through the build, contract administrator, quantity surveyor, structural engineer, party wall surveyors — on each route, who pays them?
  3. List the provisional sums. How many, what each covers, and how the final figure will be agreed.
  4. Allow for variations honestly. On a traditional route, allow for design gaps as well as your own changes.
  5. Price the time. The difference in programme, multiplied by what each month costs you.
  6. Check the payment terms. Deposit, stage payments and retention. A heavily front-loaded schedule changes your risk on either route — see builder payment schedules and front-loaded pricing.
  7. Check who holds the duties. Under CDM 2015 regulation 7, a domestic client’s duties pass to the contractor or principal designer. The client dutyholder guide covers who is responsible for what under the Building Regulations.
  8. Check the insurances. On design and build, ask to see the contractor’s professional indemnity cover for its design, alongside public liability, employer’s liability, contractors all-risk and product liability.

Your rights do not depend on the route. Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, section 49, a service must be performed with reasonable care and skill whichever form of contract you sign.

Frequently asked questions.

Is design and build cheaper than a traditional tender?

Not always at the point of signing. A competitive tender on a complete design can produce a lower starting price, because several contractors price the same drawings. Design and build often costs less by the end, because one party carries the design risk, the design and the build overlap, and there are fewer separate fees. Compare final likely cost, not starting price: fees, a realistic allowance for variations, provisional sums, and the cost of a longer programme.

Who carries the design risk in design and build?

The design and build contractor. If the design has a gap, a clash or an error, it is the contractor's problem to resolve, within the price, as long as you have not changed what you asked for. In a traditional tender, you carry the design risk: the contractor builds what is drawn, and anything missing or wrong in the drawings becomes a variation you pay for, or a claim against your designer.

Why do traditional tenders end up costing more than the tender price?

Usually because the design was not complete when it was priced. Missing details become variations; items nobody could price become provisional sums that are settled later at actual cost; and you pay a contract administrator to manage it all. Delays add the contractor's time-related costs. None of that shows in the tender figure you compared.

What are provisional sums?

A provisional sum is an allowance in a contract for work that cannot yet be defined or priced, such as a kitchen not yet chosen or a structural repair not yet opened up. It is replaced by the actual cost when the work is known. The more provisional sums a price carries, the less certain it is. Ask how many there are, what each covers, and how the final figure will be agreed.

Is design and build faster than traditional procurement?

Usually, yes. In a traditional route the design must be finished before the tender goes out, then the tender period runs, then the contract is agreed, then building starts. In design and build, design and pre-construction overlap, and the contractor can order long-lead items and plan the programme while the detail is finalised. The time saved matters most where you are paying rent or a second mortgage while the works run.

Can I keep my own architect with design and build?

Yes. Your architect can take the scheme to planning, and then either stay on as your adviser checking the contractor's work, or be novated to the contractor, who takes over the appointment and the design responsibility for the build stage. Novation keeps the designer you chose while moving the design risk to the builder. Agree in writing what your architect does for you after novation.
