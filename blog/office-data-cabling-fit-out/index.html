Office Data Cabling for a Fit-Out — A London Planning Guide

Office data cabling for a fit-out means Cat6A to desks and access points, a cooled comms room and landlord-approved riser routes, agreed before ceilings close.

By Vladimir Castravet 26 November 2026 10 min read Updated November 2026
Alexandra · N22 · residential reference photo Office fit-out photo · to follow

Office data cabling is the fixed network inside your fit-out: copper cable from a patch panel to every desk, access point, screen and door reader, and fibre linking your comms room to the building’s incoming lines. You live with it for the length of the lease, because once ceilings and raised floors are closed every change becomes a variation. This guide sets out what to specify, where the comms room should go, how to plan Wi-Fi, what your landlord controls, and what drives the cost. 2VP fits out offices and prices cabling packages, so it has an interest in this. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Specify Cat6A for new horizontal cabling. It carries 10 Gb/s Ethernet over the full standard channel length and copes better with the heat that higher-power Power over Ethernet puts into cable bundles.
  • Count outlets from the layout, not the headcount. Desks, meeting-room screens, printers, Wi-Fi access points, door access readers, cameras and AV all need a cable.
  • Put the comms room where runs stay short. A copper permanent link is designed to a maximum of 90 metres, and that sets the radius the comms room can serve.
  • Plan Wi-Fi with a survey, not a guess. Each ceiling-mounted access point needs its own data drop, positioned before the ceiling grid is set out.
  • Your landlord controls the risers, the entry point and the fire-stopping. Get the routes approved in the licence to alter, not after.
  • Ask for the test results and the labelling schedule at handover. Every link tested, every outlet numbered to match the patch panel.

What office data cabling actually means

Office data cabling is one layer of what the industry calls structured cabling: a planned, standardised system rather than cables pulled in one at a time as needs arise. In the UK it is designed to BS EN 50173-1, planned and installed to BS EN 50174, and installed in buildings to BS 6701.

What is structured cabling?

It has four parts. A backbone, usually fibre, from the building’s entry point or riser to your comms room. A comms room or cabinet holding patch panels, switches and the router. Horizontal cabling, the copper runs from the patch panel to each outlet. And the outlets themselves: floor boxes, desk modules, wall plates and ceiling points for access points and cameras. Every run is terminated, tested and labelled at both ends, so your IT lead can trace any fault in minutes.

Is Cat6A worth it for an office?

For most new fit-outs, yes. Cat6A is specified to carry 10 Gb/s over a full 100-metre channel; Cat6 only reaches that speed over shorter runs. The bigger reason is Power over Ethernet. Access points, cameras, door readers and desk phones now take their power down the data cable, and at higher power levels large bundles of cable warm up. Cat6A’s heavier construction handles that heat better. Cabling also outlives the equipment plugged into it: you may change switches and access points two or three times in one lease.

The counter-case is real. On a short lease in a Wi-Fi-first office with few fixed desks, Cat6 may do everything your IT lead needs. Cat6A is thicker and stiffer, so it needs more containment space and bigger bend radii. Make it your IT lead’s decision, written into the specification, not a default nobody chose.

Copper, fibre or Wi-Fi?

All three, doing different jobs. Fibre carries the backbone, where distance and bandwidth matter. Copper carries the last run to each device, because it carries power as well as data. Wi-Fi serves laptops and phones, but every access point is itself a wired device, so a Wi-Fi-first office still needs a cabling plan.

When you need to plan it in a London fit-out

Plan cabling at design stage, before the mechanical and electrical design is frozen. In a ceiling void, the cable tray competes with ductwork, sprinkler pipes, lighting and fan coil units; in a raised floor, it competes with power and floor boxes. A cable route drawn late is a cable route squeezed into whatever space is left.

How many data points does an office need?

There is no legal number. Build the count from the furniture layout: each desk position, each meeting-room table and screen, each printer, each Wi-Fi access point, each door access reader and camera, and any AV or room-booking panels. Then ask for spare ports on the patch panel and spare capacity in the containment, so a later reshuffle does not mean opening the ceiling.

Do I need a comms room?

A small floor may need only a wall or floor-standing cabinet in a ventilated cupboard. A larger one needs a room. Either way, it needs dedicated power circuits, cooling sized to the heat the equipment gives off, a lockable door, no water pipes running overhead and space to work at the back of the cabinet. Put it near the middle of the floor plate, so the longest run to the furthest desk stays inside the 90-metre permanent link limit.

What does the landlord control?

In a multi-let London building, your landlord usually controls the shared risers, the building’s entry point for fibre, any roof space for antennas, and the fire-stopping wherever your cables pass through a compartment wall or floor. The riser may already be congested with previous tenants’ abandoned cable. Get your routes, fixings and fire-stopping method approved as part of your licence to alter for an office fit-out. The broadband carrier may also need a wayleave from the landlord before it can bring a dedicated line in, so order that line as soon as the lease is agreed: lead times in London buildings often run to months, not weeks.

Is data cabling Cat A or Cat B?

Usually Cat B. A Cat A floor gives you the raised floor, ceiling grid, lighting and base services; the data cabling, comms room and Wi-Fi are part of the tenant’s own fit-out. The Cat A vs Cat B office fit-out guide sets out where the line normally falls. Check your lease and the landlord’s specification, because some Cat A+ floors arrive with cabling already installed.

What happens to the cabling at the end of the lease?

Read the reinstatement clause before you install anything. Many leases require you to remove your alterations, including redundant cabling, when you leave. That cost lands in your dilapidations office lease settlement. Agree in the licence to alter what can stay, and keep the as-built drawings and labelling schedule: they are what the next tenant, or your landlord’s surveyor, will ask for.

Can existing office cabling be reused?

Sometimes, and it is one of the bigger savings available on a refurbishment. Have the existing cabling tested first: if it passes to the category you need and the outlets suit your new layout, keep it. If it fails, or the layout has moved, reuse costs more in fault-finding than it saves. The office fit-out without demolition guide covers what else can usually be kept.

How long does office data cabling take?

It runs in two stages within the fit-out programme. First fix, the containment and cable pulling, happens while the ceiling and floor voids are open. Second fix, termination and testing, comes near the end, after the decorators. Ask for both stages as named lines in the programme; the office fit-out timeline guide shows where they sit.

Fire safety and the standards that apply

Cabling is a fire-safety issue as much as an IT one, for three reasons.

Fire-stopping at every penetration. Where cables pass through a fire-resisting wall or floor, the gap has to be sealed to keep the compartment intact. The Building Regulations 2010 require it, and Approved Document B is the guidance. Your landlord will specify the product and method, and will want photographic evidence before the ceiling closes.
Supports that do not fail in a fire. BS 7671 requires electrical wiring systems to be supported so they do not collapse early in a fire and trap people escaping or firefighters, and BS 6701 applies the same thinking to telecoms cabling. In practice that means metal fixings and tray, not plastic ties alone.
Cable fire performance. Data cable sold in the UK carries a Construction Products Regulation fire class, and offices normally specify low-smoke, zero-halogen sheaths. Write the class into the specification so every tenderer prices the same cable.

Separation matters too. BS EN 50174-2 sets out how far data cables should run from power cables to avoid interference, which is why the two usually share a raised floor in separate compartments. The fire-stopping record is also worth keeping for the responsible person under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, who has to keep the premises’ fire precautions in order.

Approved Documents are statutory guidance to the Building Regulations 2010 for England. Your building control body, and your landlord’s fire strategy, have the final word on your building.
