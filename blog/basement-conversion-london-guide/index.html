Basement Conversion in London — the guide

Basement conversion in London is settled by groundwater, your neighbour’s foundations and one escape route — answer those three and the rest is buildable.

By Vladimir Castravet 27 August 2026 12 min read Updated October 2026
Chester Square · Belgravia, SW1W · listed, conservation area · shown illustratively, no basement scope 22-week consent path · completed 2VP project

A basement conversion in London is decided underground, before anyone draws a room. Three things settle it: the groundwater level under your floor, the depth of your neighbour’s foundations, and whether the new storey can be escaped from. Get those three answers and the rest is buildable. Skip them and you are buying a hole with a design attached. This guide gives the tests in order, the clauses that govern each one, and the honest case for spending the same money on a loft instead. 2VP builds basements — that is a commercial interest, and it is declared here rather than hidden.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Three types. Convert an existing cellar, lower an existing cellar floor, or dig a new basement under the house or garden. The cost gap between the first and the third is the whole argument.
  • The ground decides. A site investigation gives you soil, groundwater level and bearing capacity. Nothing else does, and no drawing is worth commissioning without it.
  • Waterproofing is a designed system. Approved Document C sends structures subject to water pressure to BS 8102 — not to a bag of render.
  • Escape is a hard rule. A basement storey with habitable rooms needs an emergency escape window or external door, or a protected stairway of minimum REI 30 to a final exit (Approved Document B Volume 1, paragraph 3.9).
  • Your neighbours have a statutory say. Excavating within 3 metres and deeper than their foundations triggers a notice under section 6 of the Party Wall etc. Act 1996.
  • The honest comparison. 2VP’s delivered loft conversions run £3,200–£4,800 per m² NET. A basement does not beat that rate. It buys space where no other space exists.

The three kinds of basement, and why they are not one product

Builders and homeowners use one word for three jobs with very different risk. Name yours before you price anything.

1. Converting a dry cellar you already have

The floor stays where it is. The work is tanking, drainage, ventilation, insulation, lighting and a compliant stair. No underpinning. No new foundations. This is the cheapest version by a wide margin and the one most London terraces can actually justify.

2. Lowering the floor of an existing cellar

You want head height, so the floor goes down. The moment the new floor is below the bottom of the existing footings, the walls have to be underpinned in sequence to reach it. This is the version most people mean, and the version that carries the neighbour risk.

3. Digging a new basement where none exists

Under the house, under the garden, or both. Temporary works hold the building up while the new box is built beneath it. This is engineering, not building work with a bigger digger, and it is priced accordingly.

Key takeaway The word “basement” hides a tenfold spread in risk. Convert, lower, or dig — decide which one you are buying before you compare a single quote.

The ground decides first

Everything below this point — the structure, the waterproofing, the drainage, the programme — is priced off the ground investigation. Commission it before the architect, not after.

What a site investigation tells you

  • The soil, by layer and depth. It sets the underpinning sequence and the temporary works.
  • The groundwater level, and how it moves. Standing water against a wall is a permanent pressure, not a wet week.
  • The bearing capacity. What the new foundation can be founded on, and how deep it has to go.
  • Contaminants and ground gas. Including radon, which Approved Document C treats separately.

The radon question, answered with the actual threshold

Approved Document C, paragraph 2.40, sets the trigger through BRE Report BR 211: basic radon protective measures where 3% to 10% of homes are predicted to be at or above the Radon Action Level of 200 Bq/m³, and full protective measures where more than 10% are. A radon risk report for your postal address answers it in days. It is a cheap question with an expensive wrong answer, because the membrane goes in under the slab and cannot be added later.

How much ground you are actually moving

The arithmetic is worth doing early. Lowering a 40m² footprint by one metre removes 40 cubic metres of ground. On a London terrace that leaves by barrow, through your hallway or up a light well, into a lorry parked on a residential street. The dig is rarely the constraint. Getting the spoil out is.

Waterproofing: what BS 8102 asks you to decide

Approved Document C deals with floors and walls in contact with the ground. Paragraph 4.12 sends floors subject to water pressure to BS 8102, and paragraph 5.6 does the same for walls under groundwater pressure. A basement below the water table is outside the standard damp-proofing guidance in the Approved Document. It is a designed system under a British Standard.

BS 8102 asks two questions. What grade of dry does this space have to be — plant room, or bedroom? And which types of protection deliver it: the structure itself, a barrier applied to it, or a drained cavity that collects water and pumps it away? Habitable space is the highest grade, and a habitable grade is normally achieved by combining types rather than trusting one.

The full method — the three types, the three grades, and how to check a waterproofing design before you sign it — is in our basement waterproofing BS 8102 guide.

Three points that decide whether the system survives twenty years:

  • A drained system needs power and maintenance. A cavity drain that relies on a pump is a mechanical system in your floor. It needs a maintenance access, a service interval, and an answer for a power cut.
  • The damp-proof course has a fixed geometry above ground. Approved Document C, paragraph 5.5(b), requires the damp-proof course in an external wall to sit at least 150mm above the adjoining ground. Raise a patio or a light well floor after the build and you have bridged it.
  • Warranty and insurance are part of the specification. Ask who stands behind the waterproofing system, in writing, before the first coat goes on.

The clause most people meet too late

Paragraph 3.9 of Approved Document B Volume 1. A basement bedroom needs a way out that is not the staircase — or a staircase protected to minimum REI 30 all the way to a final exit. On a terrace with a single stair core, that second option usually means fire doors and upgraded construction on every storey above, not just the new one. It is the difference between a media room and a bedroom, and it is decided on the drawing.

The notice that sets your start date

Section 6 of the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 is what makes basements slow. Notice runs at least one month for excavation, and works to a party structure under sections 2 and 3 run at least two months. A neighbour who dissents appoints a surveyor, and a schedule of condition is prepared on their property before you dig. Put those weeks in the programme at the start. They are not a delay; they are the job.

What moves a basement price, in order of weight

  1. Which of the three jobs it is. Converting a dry cellar and digging a new box are different orders of magnitude.
  2. Groundwater. A permanently drained system with pumps costs more to build and keeps costing to maintain.
  3. Access for spoil and concrete. A terrace with no side return removes every cubic metre through the house.
  4. The neighbours. Two party structures means two sets of notices, two surveyors and two schedules of condition.
  5. What the new space has to be. A habitable bedroom pulls in escape, ventilation and fire construction that a store room does not.

Consultants sit outside any build figure. For 2VP’s loft work the published bands are architect £4,000–£9,000, structural engineer £1,500–£3,000, and party wall surveyor £1,200–£3,000 per side, all NET. Basement engineering and party wall work sit above those bands, and the ground investigation is an additional cost before any of them start.

Eight checks before you commission a drawing

  1. Name the job. Convert a dry cellar, lower an existing floor, or dig new. Write it down before you speak to anyone.
  2. Commission the ground investigation. Soil, groundwater level, bearing capacity, contaminants. First, not last.
  3. Pull a radon risk report for the address. BR 211 sets basic measures at 3–10% and full measures above 10% of homes at or above 200Bq/m³.
  4. Ask the sewerage undertaker about surcharge risk. That single answer decides pumped drainage or an anti-flooding valve, under Approved Document H paragraph 2.9.
  5. Decide what the room is. Habitable triggers paragraph 3.9 escape. A store does not.
  6. Measure the escape route on the drawing. 0.33m² opening, 450mm each way, sill no more than 1100mm above the floor — or a protected stair to a final exit.
  7. Count your party structures and serve early. One month for excavation notices, two months for party structure notices.
  8. Check the borough’s basement policy. Several London boroughs run specific basement policies and validation requirements. Read your local plan before the architect draws, not after the application is refused.

What a basement does not solve

The case against is real, and any builder who will not make it is selling.

  • It is the most expensive square metre in the house. Every other route to new floor area — loft, rear extension, reconfiguration — is cheaper per m². A basement earns its place when those routes are already used or unavailable.
  • Daylight has to be engineered. A light well or a sunken courtyard is not a detail; it is a piece of the structure, with guarding under Approved Document K paragraph 3.1(a) and drainage of its own.
  • It adds a machine to your house. A drained waterproofing system with pumps, and often a foul pump under Approved Document H paragraph 2.9, means service intervals and a plan for a power cut.
  • It puts your neighbour’s house in your risk register. That is exactly why the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 exists, and why the schedule of condition matters more here than anywhere else.
  • The programme is long and front-loaded. Investigation, design, notices and consents run for months before anything visible happens.

The honest test: if a loft or a rear extension can give you the room you actually need, take it. Reach for the basement when the space has nowhere else to go — a flat with no roof, a plot with no garden to build into, or a house already extended to its limits.

Frequently asked.

Do I need planning permission for a basement conversion in London?

Treat a full planning application as the base case and confirm it with your borough before you draw. Several London boroughs run specific basement policies and their own validation requirements, and those requirements — not a general rule of thumb — decide what you have to submit. Converting a dry cellar you already have, with no change to the outside of the house, is a different question from digging new space, and it is worth asking the planning department that question in writing before you commission a design.

Do I need a party wall agreement to dig a basement?

Almost always. Section 6 of the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 catches excavation within 3 metres of a neighbouring owner's building where you are going to a lower level than their foundations, and excavation within 6 metres where you go below a line drawn downwards at 45 degrees from the bottom of their foundations. Notice runs at least one month before you excavate. Work to a party structure itself, under sections 2 and 3, runs at least two months. A dissenting neighbour appoints a surveyor and a schedule of condition is prepared on their property before work starts.

Is a basement conversion waterproof for good?

It is a system that has to be designed, built and then maintained. Approved Document C sends floors and walls subject to water pressure to BS 8102 at paragraphs 4.12 and 5.6, rather than to the standard damp-proofing provisions. BS 8102 asks what grade of dry the space needs and which types of protection deliver it — the structure, a barrier, or a drained cavity that collects water and pumps it away. A drained system is mechanical: it needs maintenance access, a service interval and an answer for a power cut. Ask, in writing, who warrants the system before the first coat goes on.

Does a basement room need a window?

It needs a way out and it needs air, and a window is the usual way to give it both. Approved Document B Volume 1, paragraph 3.9, requires a basement storey containing habitable rooms to have either an emergency escape window or external door, or a protected stairway of minimum REI 30 leading to a final exit. Where the escape is a window, paragraph 2.10 sets it: minimum 0.33m² unobstructed openable area, minimum 450mm high and 450mm wide, with the bottom of the openable area no more than 1100mm above the floor. For ventilation, a habitable room with no external wall can be treated as one room with an adjoining room where there is a permanent opening of at least 1/20 of their combined floor area plus at least 10,000mm² of background ventilation (Approved Document F Volume 1, paragraphs 1.43 and 1.44).

How much does a basement conversion cost in London?

2VP publishes rates it has billed, and it has not delivered a basement dig in London — so there is no 2VP basement rate on this page. The price is quoted after a ground investigation, because the ground is what sets it. For comparison, 2VP's delivered loft conversions run £3,200–£4,800 per m² NET depending on type, with VAT in addition. A basement does not beat that rate. Five things move a basement price: whether you are converting, lowering or digging; the groundwater; access for spoil; the number of party structures; and whether the new space is habitable.

Is a basement worth it compared with a loft conversion?

Per square metre, no. A loft or a rear extension is the cheaper route to new floor area every time, and 2VP's delivered loft rates of £3,200–£4,800 per m² NET are the benchmark to beat. A basement earns its place when those routes are gone: a flat with no roof above it, a plot with no garden to build into, or a house already extended to its limit. If a loft can give you the room you actually need, take the loft.
