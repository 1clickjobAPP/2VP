Fire Safety Office Fit-Out: Duties, Escape Routes and Handover

Fire safety in an office fit-out means a reviewed fire risk assessment, escape routes kept open, fire-stopping proven and fire safety information handed over.

By Vladimir Castravet 10 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Moncrieff · SE15 · loft and extension Office photo to follow · real 2VP image

Fire safety in an office fit-out is three sets of rules meeting on one floor. The Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 decides who is responsible for fire safety while the office is in use. The Building Regulations 2010, with Approved Document B, decide how the new layout must be designed and built. And the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 decide how the site is kept safe while the work is happening — often with your neighbours, or your own staff, next door. This guide takes each in turn, shows where a fit-out changes the fire strategy of your floor, and lists the information you should be handed at the end. It is the office companion to the residential guide on fire-stopping before the walls are closed. 2VP builds office fit-outs, so it has an interest in this subject; weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • You are almost certainly a “responsible person”. If you employ people in the space, the Fire Safety Order puts the fire risk assessment for your floor on you. The landlord covers the common parts. You must cooperate.
  • A fit-out is a significant change. New partitions, rooms or kitchens mean your fire risk assessment has to be reviewed before the new layout is used.
  • The design must meet Approved Document B. Escape routes, travel distances, fire doors, alarm coverage and fire-stopping are the usual pressure points.
  • Most fit-outs need building control. Changing partitions, escape routes or the fire alarm is building work, and it has to be signed off.
  • The site has its own fire plan. Under CDM 2015 the principal contractor manages fire risk during the works, including hot works, waste and alarm isolations.
  • Ask for the fire safety information at handover. Drawings of compartments, fire-stopping, doors, alarm and emergency lighting — the information Regulation 38 describes.

What fire safety in an office fit-out actually means

It means each of three regimes is satisfied, by the right person, at the right time. Confusion between them is the commonest reason fire safety items get missed on a fit-out.

Who is the responsible person in a leased office?

Usually the employer in control of the space — which, for a tenant fitting out its own floor, means your business. In a multi-let building the landlord or managing agent is the responsible person for the common parts: stairs, lobbies, risers and the base-build fire alarm. The Order requires responsible persons who share premises to cooperate and coordinate, so your fit-out has to fit the landlord’s building-wide fire strategy, not just your floor plan. Your licence to alter for an office fit-out is where the landlord usually sets those conditions.

Do I need a new fire risk assessment after a fit-out?

You need to review it, and in practice that usually means rewriting much of it. The Order requires the assessment to be reviewed regularly and whenever there has been a significant change — and a new layout of rooms, doors and escape routes is exactly that. Since 1 October 2023, amendments made by the Building Safety Act 2022 mean every responsible person must record the full assessment in writing, whatever the size of the business. Plan for the reviewed assessment to be ready before your staff use the new space.

What does the designer have to prove?

That the new layout meets the fire safety requirements of Schedule 1 to the Building Regulations 2010 — Requirements B1 to B5, covering means of warning and escape, internal and external fire spread, and access for the fire service. Approved Document B Volume 2 is the guidance for buildings other than homes, offices included. As client you also carry duties under the 2023 dutyholder rules; the guide to client dutyholder duties under the Building Regulations explains them.

Who is responsible for fire safety during the works?

The construction site is a fire risk in its own right, and CDM 2015 makes that the principal contractor’s job to manage. As a business client, you must make sure suitable arrangements are in place and give the contractor the information it needs — including the building’s fire strategy, alarm zones, escape routes and the landlord’s rules. Where a project is large enough, CDM 2015 also requires it to be notified to the HSE before work starts.

  • A site fire plan in the construction phase plan: escape routes from the works area, alarm arrangements, extinguishers, a named person in charge. HSE guidance HSG168, Fire safety in construction, sets out what it should contain.
  • Hot works permits for anything that makes heat or sparks, with a fire watch afterwards.
  • Alarm isolations by permit only, zone by zone, agreed with the landlord, reinstated every evening and logged.
  • Waste out daily. Cardboard and packaging are the commonest fuel on a fit-out.
  • Escape routes never blocked by deliveries or materials, and fire doors never wedged open.

If people will be working on the floor, or next to it, while you build, the risks multiply, and your own fire risk assessment has to cover the space your staff keep using.

When a fit-out changes your floor’s fire strategy

Every time a wall, door or ceiling changes. An open Cat A floor is usually designed around one fire strategy; the moment you add rooms, you are changing how people find their way out and how fire and smoke spread. These are the points to check on your drawings before anything is ordered.

Will new rooms make escape routes too long?

They can. Approved Document B sets maximum travel distances — the distance a person has to walk to reach a protected stair or final exit. For offices it allows a shorter distance where escape is possible in one direction only (18m) than where people have a choice of directions (45m). Cellular rooms, cul-de-sac corridors and a reception desk in the wrong place all lengthen the real route. The open plan vs cellular office layout guide covers the space-planning side; the fire check has to happen at the same time.

What is an “inner room” and why does it matter?

An inner room is one whose only way out is through another room — a meeting room off the open-plan floor, for example. Approved Document B permits inner rooms only under conditions: the outer room must not be a high-risk area, and people in the inner room need a way of seeing or being warned about a fire outside, such as a vision panel or smoke detection in the outer room. Glass-walled meeting rooms in the middle of a floor need this checked early.

Which doors need to be fire doors?

Doors to protected stairs, lobbies, risers, plant and comms rooms, and doors on protected escape corridors are the usual candidates. Specify certified doorsets, not just fire-rated leaves: the frame, glass, ironmongery, intumescent strips and smoke seals are tested together. An FD30S door resists fire for 30 minutes and has smoke seals. Self-closers must actually close the door; hold-open devices should release on the fire alarm, never be replaced by a wedge.

Does the fire alarm and emergency lighting need extending?

Almost always. New rooms need detection and sounders so that a fire inside them is detected and an alarm is heard in them, to the system’s design category under BS 5839-1. Emergency lighting has to cover the new routes and rooms to BS 5266-1. In a multi-let building the system usually belongs to the landlord, so the extension is designed with the landlord’s fire alarm contractor and tested against the building’s cause-and-effect. If anyone on your team is deaf or hard of hearing, visual alarms belong in the design too. If the floor is sprinklered, any change to the ceiling or room layout means sprinkler heads have to be checked and possibly moved by a specialist.

Where does fire-stopping go in an office?

Wherever a cable, pipe or duct passes through a fire-resisting wall or floor, and in the voids. Offices hide a lot of void: above the suspended ceiling and under the raised floor. Cavity barriers in those voids stop smoke and fire travelling over or under a fire-resisting wall. Every new data cable through a riser wall is a new hole. Fire-stopping is only as good as its photo record, because once the ceiling tiles go back nobody checks it again.

Do office partitions need building control approval?

Usually yes, when they affect means of escape, fire separation or the fire alarm — which most office layouts do. A building notice is not available for work on premises covered by the Fire Safety Order, so expect a full plans application. Building control consults the fire and rescue authority on fire safety, which adds time to the programme. Ask your contractor to show the route in writing before you fix a start date.

Regulation 38 and the fire safety handover pack

Regulation 38 of the Building Regulations 2010 requires the person carrying out building work to give the responsible person fire safety information about the building — the information needed to operate and maintain it safely — no later than completion or occupation. Strictly, it is triggered by work that erects or extends a building, or changes its use. A fit-out inside an existing floor may not trigger it on its own. That does not change what you need: the responsible person cannot review the fire risk assessment, or maintain fire doors and the alarm, without the same information. Ask for it in your contract either way.

What drives the cost of fire safety in a fit-out

Fire safety is rarely one line on a quote. It is spread across partitions, doors, electrics and fees, which is why it is easy to under-price. These are the drivers, and what to ask for in writing.

Frequently asked questions.

Who is the responsible person for fire safety in a leased office?

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 it is usually the employer in control of the space — so, for a tenant, your business is responsible for your floor. The landlord or managing agent is usually responsible for common parts such as stairs, lobbies and the base-build alarm. Where responsible persons share a building, the Order requires them to cooperate and coordinate.

Do I need a new fire risk assessment after an office fit-out?

You must review it, and a new layout usually means rewriting much of it. The Order requires a review whenever there is a significant change, and new rooms, doors and escape routes are one. Since 1 October 2023 every responsible person must record the full assessment in writing. Have the reviewed assessment ready before staff use the new space.

What is Regulation 38?

Regulation 38 of the Building Regulations 2010 requires the person carrying out building work to hand fire safety information to the responsible person by completion or occupation. It is triggered by work that erects or extends a building or changes its use, so a pure fit-out may not trigger it. Ask for the same information in your contract anyway: drawings, fire-stopping record, door schedule and alarm certificates.

Do office partitions need building control approval?

Usually, when they affect means of escape, fire separation or the fire alarm, which most new office layouts do. A building notice cannot be used for premises covered by the Fire Safety Order, so expect a full plans application. Building control consults the fire and rescue authority, so allow time for it in the programme.

Who is responsible for fire safety during the works?

Under CDM 2015, the principal contractor plans and manages fire risk on site, including the site fire plan, hot works permits, waste removal and alarm isolations. As client you must make sure suitable arrangements are in place and give the contractor the building’s fire strategy, alarm zones and the landlord’s rules. Your own fire risk assessment still applies to any space your staff keep using.

Does an office fit-out have to follow Approved Document B exactly?

No. Approved Document B is statutory guidance showing one way to meet the fire safety requirements of the Building Regulations 2010. A different solution can be accepted if a fire engineer shows it meets the requirements and building control agrees. Following the guidance is usually quicker to approve; departing from it costs fire engineering time.
