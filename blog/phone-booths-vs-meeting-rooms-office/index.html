Office Phone Booths vs Meeting Rooms — Which Does Your Team Need?

Office phone booths vs meeting rooms is a headcount question: booths for one-person calls, built rooms for meetings of three or more.

By Vladimir Castravet 24 November 2026 10 min read Updated November 2026
Chester Square · 2VP project photo Photo · office fit-out photo to follow

The question of office phone booths vs meeting rooms comes up on almost every fit-out now, because hybrid working changed what people come into the office to do. Many people spend much of their office day on calls — often alone, on video, with colleagues who are at home. A floor planned around four-person meeting rooms ends up with one person in each, while the open plan fills with people talking into laptops. This guide sets out what booths and built rooms each do well, how to work out the mix from your own diary data, and the fire, ventilation and lease questions that freestanding pods raise and their brochures rarely answer. 2VP builds meeting rooms and installs booths on office fit-outs, so it has an interest in both. Read what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Booths are for one person, rooms are for groups. A single-person call does not need a meeting room. A meeting of three or more does not belong in a pod.
  • Count your calls before you choose. Two weeks of diary and room-booking data will tell you more than any rule of thumb.
  • Booths move with you; rooms stay. A freestanding pod is usually furniture you take to the next office. A built room is part of the floor, and may have to be stripped out at lease end.
  • Pods raise fire questions. A booth with a roof can block sprinklers and hide a fire from the alarm. Your fire risk assessment has to cover it.
  • Pods need fresh air. A booth’s fan moves office air through it; the office’s own ventilation has to supply that air.
  • Sound ratings are not equal. Ask for a booth’s speech level reduction tested to ISO 23351-1, not a single marketing decibel figure.

What phone booths and meeting rooms actually are

The two look like alternatives, but they are different kinds of thing: one is a product you buy, the other is a part of the building you construct.

What is an office phone booth?

An office phone booth is a freestanding, factory-made acoustic enclosure for one person, sometimes two. It arrives in panels, is assembled on site in a few hours, and has its own door, light, fan and power, usually from a single plug or floor box. Larger “meeting pods” seat four and work the same way. Because they stand on the floor and are not fixed to the building, they are usually treated as furniture.

What is a built meeting room?

A built meeting room is construction: partitions to the ceiling or slab, a door, lighting, power and data, ventilation, fire detection and often glazing. It is designed into your layout, needs the landlord’s consent, and becomes part of the floor. The open-plan vs cellular office layout guide covers how rooms change the way a floor works.

When you need booths, rooms, or both

Most London offices need both, in a ratio set by how your own team works. The way to find it is to measure, not guess.

How many phone booths does an office need?

There is no legal or industry-standard number, and any ratio quoted without data from your own office is a guess. Do this instead: for two typical weeks, pull room-booking data and ask your team to log calls. You need four numbers — how many calls happen at the busiest hour, how many of those are one person, how long they last, and how many are confidential. Peak one-person calls tell you how many booths you need; peak group meetings tell you how many rooms.

When is a meeting room the right answer?

  • Three or more people in the room. Pods for four exist, but they are tight for long meetings and hard to light for video.
  • Client-facing meetings and interviews. A built room with a table, a screen and daylight sets a different tone from a pod.
  • Long, confidential conversations. HR, legal and finance discussions need speech privacy and enough fresh air for an hour or more.
  • Hybrid meetings with a camera. A room can be designed around the screen: lighting on faces, absorption on the walls, a microphone that hears everyone.

When is a phone booth the right answer?

  • Short one-person calls that would otherwise tie up a four-person room.
  • A floor you may leave or re-plan before the lease ends. Booths go with you.
  • Where the landlord will not allow more partitions, or the floor’s ventilation and lighting cannot easily serve new rooms.
  • Focus work. A booth booked for an hour of quiet is a cheap way to give back what open plan took away.

What about speech privacy?

Neither a booth nor a room is automatically private. A booth’s sound performance depends on its seals, door and ventilation path; a room’s depends on its partitions, the ceiling void above, the door and any glazing. For booths, ask for the speech level reduction measured to ISO 23351-1, which rates enclosures in a laboratory in a comparable way. For rooms, the office acoustics design guide covers partition ratings, flanking through ceilings and background sound.

Fire and ventilation questions to ask about every booth

A freestanding pod is an enclosed room with a roof, placed inside a floor whose fire and ventilation systems were designed without it. That does not make pods unsafe, but each one raises questions that need answers before it arrives.

Do office phone booths need fire detection?

Often, yes. The ceiling’s smoke detectors cannot see a fire inside a closed pod with a solid roof. Many pods can be supplied with a built-in detector, and the question is whether it should link to the building’s fire alarm, which is the landlord’s system. Ask the landlord’s fire alarm engineer, and check against BS 5839-1 and your fire risk assessment under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005.

What about sprinklers?

If the floor is sprinklered, a pod with a roof can sit in the shadow of a sprinkler head, so a fire inside it is not reached. Some pods are made with open or mesh roofs for this reason; others need a sprinkler head of their own. This is a question for the landlord and their insurer, and the answer should be in writing before the order is placed.

Will the occupant hear the alarm?

A pod built to keep sound out also keeps the fire alarm out. Ask the supplier for the alarm sound level inside the closed pod, or plan a visual alarm or a linked sounder. Someone on a headset in a sealed booth is the person most likely to miss an evacuation.

Can a pod block an escape route?

It can, and this is the commonest mistake. A pod placed in a corridor or across a route narrows the escape width your fire strategy relies on. Approved Document B, Volume 2, and the building’s fire strategy set those routes; your fire risk assessment has to be reviewed whenever pods are added or moved. Also ask for the materials’ reaction-to-fire classification.

How do booths get fresh air?

Most pods have a fan that draws air from the office and exhausts it back. That keeps the booth from getting stuffy, but it only moves air that the office ventilation has already supplied. Approved Document F, Volume 2, sets a fresh-air rate per person for offices, and the people in your booths count towards it. Ask how many air changes the pod’s fan gives, check the booth’s heat build-up with someone on a laptop for an hour, and make sure the floor’s system can cover the extra pressure on fresh air.

Power, floor loading and access

  • Power: one socket or floor box per pod, without trailing leads across a walkway.
  • Weight: ask for the assembled weight and check it against the floor’s design loading, especially on older London buildings and raised floors.
  • Delivery: check the panel sizes against the goods lift and the stairs.
  • Accessibility: under the Equality Act 2010, consider at least one booth a wheelchair user can enter, with a wider door, a level threshold and room to turn.

What drives the cost of booths vs meeting rooms

A booth is a product cost plus installation; a meeting room is a construction cost that spreads across several trades. 2VP does not publish a rate for either, because floors and products differ too much. These are the drivers to compare:

  1. Booth specification. Acoustic performance, ventilation, built-in detection, lighting and finishes all move the price. Compare tested data, not brochures.
  2. Fire alarm and sprinkler work. Linking pods to the building’s alarm, or adding sprinkler heads, is work by the landlord’s approved engineers.
  3. Meeting room partitions and doors. Acoustic rating, glazing and door type drive the room’s cost more than its size.
  4. Services into each room. Lighting, ventilation, detection, power and data — a room needs all of them, a pod brings most of its own.
  5. Landlord consent. Rooms usually need a licence to alter, with fees and review time. See the guide to licence to alter for an office fit-out.
  6. The end of the lease. Rooms may need stripping out; pods leave with you. Over a short lease, that difference can decide the question.

Ask for booths and rooms priced side by side in writing, including the fire, ventilation and reinstatement lines. The guide to office fit-out cost in London shows how they sit in a full budget.

Frequently asked questions.

How many phone booths does an office need?

There is no legal or standard number. Measure it: for two typical weeks, log calls and pull room-booking data. Count the one-person calls at your busiest hour, how long they last and how many are confidential. Peak one-person calls set the number of booths; peak group meetings set the number of rooms.

Do office phone booths need fire detection?

Often. Ceiling smoke detectors cannot see a fire inside a closed pod with a solid roof. Many pods can be supplied with a built-in detector; whether it links to the building’s fire alarm is a question for the landlord’s fire alarm engineer, checked against BS 5839-1 and your fire risk assessment under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005.

Do phone booths need planning or building control approval?

A freestanding pod that is not fixed to the building is usually treated as furniture, so it does not normally need planning permission or a Building Regulations application. It still has to fit your fire strategy, must not narrow an escape route, and may need landlord consent for power or fire alarm links. If in doubt, ask your building control body.

Are phone booths cheaper than meeting rooms?

Per person served, a booth is usually the smaller cost for one-person calls, because it brings its own light, fan and sound insulation and needs no partitions. A meeting room spreads across several trades and may need stripping out at lease end. Compare them side by side, including fire, ventilation and reinstatement lines.

Can phone booths be moved to a new office?

Usually, yes. Most pods are built from panels that can be dismantled and reassembled, so they can move with you at the end of a lease. Check that the supplier’s warranty covers relocation, and that the new floor’s fire strategy, sprinklers and ventilation suit the pods before you move them.
