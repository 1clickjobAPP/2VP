MEES Office Tenant Guide — The EPC Rules That Apply Today

As a MEES office tenant, the law binds your landlord: since April 2023 it cannot continue letting below EPC E without a registered exemption.

By Vladimir Castravet 16 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Chester Square · Belgravia, SW1W · townhouse Shown illustratively · a 2VP home, not an office fit-out

If you are a MEES office tenant — a business renting office space in England or Wales — the minimum energy efficiency standard is your landlord’s legal duty, not yours. It still shapes your lease talks, what your fit-out is allowed to change, and what you could be asked to put back when you leave. This guide sets out what the law requires today under the Energy Efficiency (Private Rented Property) (England and Wales) Regulations 2015, what is only a government proposal, what a fit-out does to an EPC, and what to ask before you sign. It is written by a contractor, not a solicitor: take legal advice on your own lease. 2VP fits out London offices, so it has an interest in how you plan this. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • The law today: a landlord of non-domestic private rented property in England and Wales must not let it with an EPC rating below E — that is, F or G — unless a valid exemption is registered.
  • Two dates. Since 1 April 2018 for new lettings, renewals and extensions. Since 1 April 2023 for lettings that were already running.
  • The duty and the penalties fall on the landlord. You are not fined for your landlord’s rating.
  • EPC C and EPC B targets are proposals, not law. Do not sign on the assumption they are in force, or on the assumption they will never come.
  • Your fit-out can move the rating. Lighting, heating, cooling, ventilation and controls are all part of the EPC calculation. Some fit-outs also trigger a new EPC.
  • Read three lease clauses: alterations, energy and EPC, and reinstatement.

What MEES actually means for an office tenant

MEES stands for minimum energy efficiency standards. For offices, shops and other non-domestic buildings, it is the part of the 2015 Regulations that stops a landlord letting a “sub-standard” property — one with an EPC rating of F or G.

What is the current minimum EPC rating for letting an office?

E. Since 1 April 2018, a landlord must not grant a new tenancy of a sub-standard non-domestic property, and that includes renewing or extending one. Since 1 April 2023, a landlord must not continue to let a sub-standard property under a tenancy that already existed. In both cases the landlord can let lawfully if a valid exemption is registered on the PRS Exemptions Register. The rules apply to most commercial leases, and only to buildings that are legally required to have an EPC.

Does MEES apply to the tenant?

Not directly. The Regulations put the prohibition on the landlord, and enforcement — by the local weights and measures authority — is against the landlord. For a non-domestic breach, the 2015 Regulations set the financial penalty as a share of the property’s rateable value: 10% for a breach of under three months and 20% for three months or more, each with a statutory minimum and maximum, and the breach can be published.

The effects still reach you. A landlord that cannot lift the rating, or register an exemption, may not be able to renew your lease. It may want access to your floor to carry out improvement works. And it may try to recover the cost through the service charge, if your lease allows it.

What exemptions can a landlord register?

  • All relevant improvements made. Every improvement that passes the cost-effectiveness test has been done, or none passes it, and the rating is still below E.
  • Seven-year payback. Improvements are only “relevant” if the expected energy savings over seven years equal or exceed the cost.
  • Consent refused. A third party whose consent is needed — which can include you as tenant, a superior landlord or the planning authority — has refused it.
  • Devaluation. An independent surveyor advises that the improvements would cut the property’s market value by more than 5%.
  • New landlord. A short temporary exemption for someone who has just become the landlord.

Most exemptions last five years and then fall away. The consent exemption matters to you: if you refuse access for improvement works, you can hand your landlord its exemption, and the question of the rating comes back at your next renewal.

What is law and what is only a proposal

Only the EPC E minimum is law today. Every stricter target you will read about for offices is a government proposal that would need new regulations before it binds anyone.

Proposals become law only when new regulations are made. Check the position on the day you sign or renew, and ask your solicitor how the lease deals with a future change in the minimum.

Is EPC C by 2027 law?

No. Raising the non-domestic minimum to EPC C from 2027, and to EPC B by 2030, are proposals the government has consulted on. Until regulations are made, the legal minimum stays at E. A well-drafted lease treats them as a risk to plan for, not a duty that exists today.

What should a tenant do about the proposals?

Ask, in writing, for the current EPC and its recommendation report, the landlord’s plans for the building’s plant and lighting over your lease term, and how the cost of any future improvement would be shared. A ten-year lease signed now runs past both proposed dates.

When MEES matters in a London office fit-out

As a MEES office tenant, you meet the rules at three points: before you sign, while you design the fit-out, and when you leave.

What should I check before I sign?

  1. The EPC. Look it up on GOV.UK’s “Find an energy certificate” service. Note the rating, the date (an EPC is valid for ten years) and whether it covers your floor or the whole building.
  2. Any exemption. Search the PRS Exemptions Register. An exemption close to its five-year end is a renewal risk.
  3. The recommendation report. It lists the improvements the assessor thinks would raise the rating. Those are the works your landlord may later want to do on your floor.

Can my office fit-out change the EPC rating?

Yes. A non-domestic EPC models the building’s fabric and its fixed building services — heating, cooling, ventilation, hot water and lighting — under standard occupancy assumptions. How your staff use the office does not change it. What your fit-out installs can. Efficient lighting with presence and daylight controls, better heating and cooling plant and better controls can improve the modelled result. Adding cooling to space that had none, or electric water heaters at every tea point, can make it worse. Ask the energy assessor to model the design before it is built, not after.

Does a fit-out trigger a new EPC?

It can. The Building Regulations require a new EPC where a building is modified so that it has more or fewer parts designed for separate occupation, and the work includes providing or extending fixed services for heating, hot water, air conditioning or mechanical ventilation. Splitting a floor into two tenancies, or combining two, is the common case. Many leases stop the tenant commissioning its own EPC, or require the landlord’s approval of it, so check before you instruct an assessor.

Which lease clauses should I read?

  • Alterations. Many leases now forbid alterations that would lower the EPC rating. Your landlord will check this when it reviews your design for consent — the licence to alter for an office fit out guide explains that process.
  • Energy and EPC. Who may commission an EPC, whether you must share energy data, and whether the landlord can enter to carry out improvement works.
  • Service charge. Whether “improvements” are recoverable from you, or only repairs and maintenance.
  • Reinstatement. What you must strip out at the end. Removing efficient lighting to put back worse could lower the rating, so agree in writing what stays. The dilapidations office lease guide covers the end of the lease.

Who looks after the air conditioning?

If your fit-out installs air conditioning that you control, the Energy Performance of Buildings (England and Wales) Regulations 2012 require systems above 12 kW to be inspected by an accredited assessor at least every five years, and the duty sits with whoever controls the system. Put the inspection date in your handover file.

What drives the cost of meeting MEES

Who pays depends on where the work sits: on the landlord’s base build, inside your fit-out, or recovered through the service charge under your lease. 2VP does not publish prices in its guides; these are the drivers to understand.

Age of the base-build plant. Boilers, chillers and air handling units near the end of their life are the biggest lever on the rating, and they are usually the landlord’s.
Lighting and controls. Often inside the tenant’s fit-out. Efficient fittings with controls can be specified at little extra effort if they are in the design from the start.
Fabric. Windows, roofs and walls belong to the landlord. Replacing glazing in an occupied building is slow and disruptive.
Working round you. Improvement works on an occupied floor run out of hours and in phases. The office fit out without demolition guide covers working within an existing fit-out.
Assessment. An accredited assessor’s fee for the EPC, and for modelling options. Ask for the fee and the options in writing.
The seven-year test. An improvement the landlord can show does not pay back within seven years is not one it has to make. Ask to see that calculation.

Energy-related works add design and approval time to a fit-out programme. The office fit out timeline in London guide shows where they sit.

Frequently asked questions.

What is the minimum EPC rating for letting an office?

E. Under the Energy Efficiency (Private Rented Property) (England and Wales) Regulations 2015, a landlord must not grant a new tenancy of non-domestic property rated F or G (since 1 April 2018), or continue to let it (since 1 April 2023), unless a valid exemption is registered on the PRS Exemptions Register.

Does MEES apply to office tenants?

Not directly. The duty and the penalties fall on the landlord. As a tenant you feel it through your lease: whether it can be renewed, whether the landlord can enter to carry out improvement works, whether you pay through the service charge, and what you must reinstate when you leave.

Is EPC C by 2027 law?

No. Raising the minimum for non-domestic lettings to EPC C from 2027 and EPC B by 2030 are government proposals that have been consulted on. Until new regulations are made, the legal minimum remains EPC E. Check the position on the day you sign.

Can my landlord make me pay for EPC improvements?

Only if your lease allows it. Many service charge clauses cover repairs and maintenance but not improvements. Read the service charge and alterations clauses with your solicitor before you sign, and agree in writing how the cost of any future improvement would be shared.

Can my office fit-out change the EPC rating?

Yes. A non-domestic EPC models the fixed building services — lighting, heating, cooling, ventilation and hot water — so what your fit-out installs can raise or lower the rating. Some fit-outs, such as splitting a floor into separate tenancies with new services, also trigger a new EPC. Ask an accredited assessor to model the design before it is built.

What happens if my landlord breaches MEES?

The local weights and measures authority can fine the landlord a share of the property’s rateable value — 10% for a breach of under three months, 20% for three months or more, within a statutory minimum and maximum — and publish the breach. The breach does not of itself end your lease. This is a summary, not legal advice.
