Is a Builder’s Quote Legally Binding? Quote vs Estimate Explained

Yes, a builder’s quote is legally binding once you accept it: offer plus acceptance makes a contract, whereas an estimate is only a guide.

By Vladimir Castravet 5 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Alexandra · N22 · whole-house renovation Completed · 2VP project

Is a builder’s quote legally binding? Once you accept it, yes: a quote is an offer to do defined work for a stated price, and an accepted offer is a contract that binds you both. An estimate is different. It is the builder’s informed guess, and the final bill can move away from it. What makes either one useful to you is the detail: a quote that says exactly what is included, what is excluded and how changes are priced protects you; a one-line total protects nobody. This guide explains the law in plain English, the difference between a quote and an estimate, when a quoted price can legitimately change, and what an itemised quote must show. It is a summary of the general position in England, not legal advice. 2VP writes quotes for a living, so it has an interest here. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • An accepted quote is a contract. Offer plus acceptance, in writing, by email, or even out loud, binds both of you.
  • An estimate is a guide, not a promise. The final price can differ, but the builder must still charge a reasonable price.
  • The quote’s detail is your protection. Scope, drawings, exclusions, provisional sums and VAT basis decide what “the price” actually covers.
  • The price can change legitimately when you change the work, when a provisional sum is adjusted to its real figure, or when something the contract puts at your risk turns up.
  • Get every change priced in writing before the work is done. Most quote disputes are really variation disputes.
  • Ask for an itemised quote. Lines with quantities let you check it, compare it, and price changes against it.

What “legally binding” actually means for a builder’s quote

A contract in English law needs an offer, an acceptance of that offer, something given in exchange (here, the work for the money), and an intention by both sides to be bound. A builder’s quote is usually the offer. Your “yes, please go ahead” is usually the acceptance. From that moment both of you are bound by what the quote says.

Is a quote the same as an estimate?

No, and the word on the document matters less than what it says. A quote states a price for a defined scope of work. An estimate gives an approximate figure that both sides expect to change. A document headed “estimate” that sets out a firm price for described work may be treated as a quote; a “quote” full of guesses gives you little more than an estimate.

Is a verbal quote legally binding?

It can be. Most building contracts do not have to be in writing to be valid, so a price agreed on the doorstep and accepted can bind you both. The problem is proof: when a dispute comes, nobody remembers the same words. The Consumer Rights Act 2015 also makes things a trader says or writes to you about the service binding where you take them into account when you decide to go ahead. Even so, ask for any verbal price to be confirmed by email before work starts.

Does an accepted quote bind me too?

Yes. A contract binds both sides, so walking away after acceptance can leave you liable for the builder’s losses, such as materials ordered for your job. Consumer law may give you a cooling-off period, for example where the contract was made at your home rather than at the builder’s premises. If you ask the builder to start inside that period, you may have to pay for work already done. Ask the builder to set out any cancellation rights in writing.

When a builder’s quoted price can change

An accepted quote fixes the price for the work it describes. The price changes legitimately only when the work changes, or when the quote itself said a figure would be confirmed later.

Can a builder charge more than the quote?

Not for the work the quote describes, unless the contract allows it. A builder who underpriced the job cannot simply add to the bill. The price can rise for:

  • Variations you ask for. A different tile, an extra socket, a moved wall. Each one is a change to the contract and should be priced before it is done.
  • Provisional sums. Allowances for work nobody can price yet, adjusted up or down to the real cost.
  • Risks the contract puts on you. Ground conditions are the common one: if building control requires deeper foundations once the trench is open, the contract decides who pays. The guide to why foundation depth changes on site explains how that happens.
  • An expired quote. Quotes are valid for a stated period. Accept after it ends and the builder can re-price.
  • Exclusions. Work the quote said it did not include was never in the price.

What if no price was agreed at all?

If you hire a builder without agreeing a price, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 says you must pay a reasonable price, and the work must be done within a reasonable time. “Reasonable” is decided on the facts, which is exactly why you want a price agreed in writing before work starts.

What is a provisional sum?

A provisional sum is an amount in the quote for work that cannot be priced accurately yet, such as repairs behind a wall that has not been opened. It is adjusted to the real cost once that work is known. Ask what each provisional sum is based on, and treat a quote with many large ones as closer to an estimate.

What is a prime cost sum?

A prime cost (PC) sum is an allowance for an item you have not chosen yet, such as a kitchen, tiles or sanitaryware. If you pick something dearer or cheaper than the allowance, the price moves by the difference, and the quote should say whether the builder’s fitting and handling charge moves with it.

What an itemised quote is — and what it must show

An itemised quote breaks the price into separate lines, each with a description, a quantity, a unit and a rate, so you can see what you are paying for and check that nothing is missing. It is the opposite of a single total with “all works as discussed” underneath.

Before you accept any builder’s quote, check it shows:

  1. The drawings and specification it is based on, by revision. So “the work” has a fixed meaning.
  2. Line items with quantities, units and rates. For example, square metres of plastering or the number of sockets, not “electrics”.
  3. Preliminaries as their own lines. Scaffold, skips, protection, welfare, site management and licences.
  4. Provisional and prime cost sums, clearly marked. With what each is based on.
  5. Exclusions. What is not included, in plain words: party wall surveyors, building control fees, decoration, landscaping, or anything else left out.
  6. The VAT basis. Whether figures include VAT or VAT is added on top.
  7. A validity period. How long the price holds if you do not accept straight away.
  8. Payment terms and programme. When each payment falls due, tied to stages of work, and the planned start and duration.
  9. How changes are priced. Ideally against the same rates as the original lines.

What is an open-book quote?

An open-book quote shows not just the lines but how each was built up: the quantities and the rates behind them. That lets you see where the money goes, compare two quotes properly and price any change against a rate you have already seen. The guide to why two quotes for the same job differ shows how much an itemised comparison reveals.

What drives the cost in a builder’s quote

Two quotes for the same job rarely differ because one builder is cheaper. They differ because the quotes price different things. These are the lines that move the total most:

  • Scope gaps. One quote includes the structural steel and the making good; the other excludes them.
  • Provisional and PC sums. Low allowances make a quote look cheaper until the real figures arrive.
  • Preliminaries. Scaffold, protection and site management for the full length of the job, or for part of it.
  • Specification. The same “new bathroom” can mean very different products.
  • Risk. Who carries the cost of what is found when walls and floors are opened.
  • Payment profile. A quote that asks for most of the money early is costing you security, even if the total is the same. See builder payment schedules and front-loaded pricing.

How to turn a quote into a contract that protects you

Knowing that a builder’s quote is legally binding once accepted is only half of it. The quote is the offer; the contract is everything that surrounds it. Five steps close most of the gaps disputes come from:

  1. Price the drawings, not a conversation. Get quotes once the design and specification are fixed enough to price.
  2. Ask for the quote to be itemised. And ask what each exclusion and provisional sum means for you.
  3. Accept in writing, referring to the quote by date and reference. So there is no doubt which version you accepted.
  4. Sign a written contract. It should attach the quote, drawings and specification, and set out payments, programme, the defects period and warranty.
  5. Agree the variation procedure. Every change instructed in writing, priced before it is done, and approved by you. Keep it all in one email thread.

Remember that you also have duties as the client on a building project; the client dutyholder guide sets out what they are and how they pass to your builder.

Frequently asked questions.

Is a builder’s quote legally binding?

Once you accept it, yes. A quote is an offer to do defined work for a stated price, and an accepted offer is a contract that binds both you and the builder. The price can then change only as the quote and contract allow, for example for variations you request or provisional sums adjusted to the real cost. This is the general position in England, not legal advice.

What is the difference between a quote and an estimate?

A quote states a price for a defined scope of work and binds both sides once accepted. An estimate is an approximate figure that both sides expect to change. What the document says matters more than its heading: an “estimate” that sets a firm price for described work may be treated as a quote.

Can a builder charge more than the quote?

Not for the work the quote describes, unless the contract allows it. The price can rise for variations you ask for, provisional sums adjusted to the real cost, risks the contract puts on you such as ground conditions, work the quote excluded, or if you accept after the quote’s validity period has ended.

What is an itemised quote?

An itemised quote breaks the price into separate lines, each with a description, quantity, unit and rate, based on named drawings and a specification. It also shows preliminaries, provisional and prime cost sums, exclusions, the VAT basis, a validity period and payment terms, so you can check it, compare it and price changes against it.

Is a verbal quote legally binding?

It can be. Most building contracts do not have to be in writing, so a price agreed and accepted out loud can bind you both. Proving what was said is the problem. Ask for any verbal price to be confirmed by email, against the drawings, before work starts.

Can I cancel after accepting a builder’s quote?

Ask the builder to set out cancellation rights in writing.
