Office Refurbishment While Occupied — Keeping London Teams Working

Office refurbishment while occupied works when the floor is split into phases, noisy work moves out of hours, and every escape route stays open.

By Vladimir Castravet 12 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Amott Road · Peckham SE15 · dormer loft and rear/side infill Office photo to follow · real 2VP image

An office refurbishment while occupied is a building project and a business-continuity plan at the same time. Your team keeps working, your clients keep visiting, and the works move around them in planned phases — with the noisiest and dirtiest jobs moved to evenings and weekends, and every fire escape route open every day. It can be done well, but it is slower and needs more planning than working in an empty floor, and some jobs should never be done next to people at their desks. This guide sets out how to phase the work, what to move out of hours, how to control dust and noise, and how fire safety works while the building is in use. 2VP refurbishes London offices, so it has an interest in this subject; weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Yes, you can refurbish while you stay in. It works when the floor is split into zones and the works move zone by zone, with staff moved out of each one in turn.
  • Noisy and dusty jobs go out of hours. Drilling, cutting, chasing and floor removal belong in evenings and weekends, within your landlord’s rules and the borough’s.
  • Dust is controlled at the tool, not just with sheets. On-tool extraction, sealed screens and protected air-handling grilles.
  • Escape routes stay open every single day. Your fire risk assessment is reviewed for the works period, and the contractor’s site fire plan fits with it.
  • Check for asbestos first. In an older building, a refurbishment survey comes before anyone opens a ceiling.
  • It costs more and takes longer than an empty-floor project, because each phase repeats set-up, protection and cleaning. The trade is that you keep working.

What refurbishing an occupied office actually means

It means the building work and the business share the floor, and the plan for one is built around the other. Three words come up early.

  • Phasing — splitting the floor into zones that are worked on one at a time, in a fixed order.
  • Decant — moving the people and furniture out of the next zone before work starts there.
  • Swing space — the temporary place they go: another part of your floor, a spare meeting room, a different floor, or home working for that phase.

Is this the same as an office fit-out without demolition?

No. An office fit-out without demolition is about what you keep — reusing the ceiling, floor, partitions and services you already have rather than stripping them out. Refurbishing while occupied is about who is in the building while you work. You can do either without the other: a no-demolition refresh of an empty floor, or a heavy refurbishment phased around your team. Many projects combine both, because keeping more of the existing fit-out also means less noise and dust.

When should you move out instead?

When the work cannot be separated from people safely or sensibly. Moving out, even briefly, is usually the better answer if:

  • Asbestos has to be removed from the areas you would be working next to.
  • The air conditioning or ventilation is being replaced across the floor, rather than zone by zone.
  • The whole ceiling or raised floor is being replaced, so there is no clean zone to work from.
  • The floor is too small to phase — if one zone would hold your whole team, there is nowhere to decant to.

When you need a phased refurbishment in London — and how to run it

Most often when moving out is not an option: the lease has years left, there is no affordable swing space nearby, or the business cannot stop — a client-facing reception, a trading desk, a clinic or a call team. London adds two sets of rules that shape when you can work.

What noisy work has to be done out of hours?

Anything that would stop your team, or the building’s other tenants, from working: breaking out, drilling into concrete, cutting, chasing walls, lifting floors, and heavy deliveries. Multi-let office buildings usually have building rules that restrict noisy work to before or after core hours and at weekends, and the landlord’s managing agent enforces them. Read them before your contractor prices the job, because they decide how many hours are paid at out-of-hours rates.

Can the borough stop out-of-hours work?

Yes, if it disturbs neighbours. Under section 60 of the Control of Pollution Act 1974, a London borough can serve a notice setting the hours and methods for construction noise, and section 61 lets a contractor apply for prior consent in advance. Most boroughs publish standard hours for noisy building work, and they are aimed at daytime working. That can clash with a landlord who wants the same noise after 6pm. In a building with flats nearby, settle the hours with both before the programme is fixed.

Does a refurbishment while occupied need landlord consent?

Usually yes, the same as any other alteration under your lease. The licence to alter for an office fit-out guide covers the application. For an occupied refurbishment, include the phasing plan, the working hours and the fire arrangements in the pack: the landlord will want to know how its other tenants and its common parts are protected.

How do you phase an office refurbishment?

Start from the business, not the building. Ask each team when it can move, what it cannot do without, and which weeks are off-limits — a year-end, a product launch, an audit. Then build the phases.

  1. Survey and zone the floor. Split it into zones that can each be closed off with their own access, without blocking a fire exit or the only route to the toilets.
  2. Work out the decant. For each phase: who moves, where to, for how long, and who moves the furniture and IT. Label everything.
  3. Do the enabling works first. Anything shared by several zones — power, data backbone, comms room, toilets, kitchen — is usually done first, out of hours, so the later phases do not need repeated shutdowns.
  4. Build the screens. Full-height, sealed temporary partitions between the site and the office, with a lockable door. Where a screen forms part of an escape route, it needs to be fire-resisting.
  5. Agree the hours, phase by phase. Which tasks happen during the day, which in the evening, which at weekends.
  6. Plan shutdowns in writing. Power, water, data, the fire alarm zone and the lifts: dates, times and duration, agreed with your IT lead and the landlord.
  7. Hand back each zone properly. Snag it, clean it, test the services, and move people back before the next phase starts.

Each phase repeats set-up, protection and cleaning, so phasing adds time. The office fit-out timeline in London shows the stages of a standard programme; expect each phase of an occupied job to run through a smaller version of them.

How do you control dust in a working office?

At the source first. Construction dust, including the fine silica dust from cutting concrete, plaster and blocks, falls under the Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002, and HSE guidance puts on-tool extraction ahead of masks and sheeting. Then seal the site: full-height screens taped at floor and ceiling, a lobby or sticky mats at the door, and the ceiling void considered too — dust travels over a screen that stops at the suspended ceiling. Cover return-air grilles in the works zone so the air conditioning does not carry dust to the rest of the floor, and plan a filter clean at the end.

How do you keep noise down during the day?

Move the noise, not just the people. Daytime work should be the quiet trades — second-fix joinery, decorating, ceiling tiles, furniture assembly. Anything percussive or cutting goes in the evening. Share the week’s plan with your team so people know when to book meeting rooms on the far side of the floor. If acoustics are part of the brief, the office acoustics design guide covers the finished result.

How do you keep fire escape routes open during building work?

By planning them before the screens go up, and checking them every day. Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, you remain the responsible person for the space your staff keep using, and a refurbishment is a significant change: your fire risk assessment needs reviewing for each phase, not just at the end. The contractor’s site fire plan, required under CDM 2015, has to fit with it. In practice that means:

  • A fire plan for each phase showing escape routes, exits and the assembly point, briefed to your staff and the site team.
  • No zone left without detection. Where the alarm is isolated for works, it is reinstated before staff return, and a fire watch covers any gap.
  • Hot works by permit, with a fire watch after the work stops.
  • Fire doors kept shut, never wedged, and protected from damage.
  • Waste out every day, so the site never holds a fuel load next to your desks.

What about asbestos?

In any building that may contain it — HSE advises that buildings built before 2000 can — check before anything is opened up. The Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012 require the duty holder in non-domestic premises to manage asbestos, and a refurbishment and demolition survey is needed before work that disturbs the fabric. Ask the landlord for the asbestos register first. If anything is found where you plan to work, its removal should happen with the area empty.

The same work costs more when people are in the building, because of the extra hours, protection and repetition. These are the drivers, and what to ask for in writing.

Set that against what you avoid: a second lease, a temporary office, or weeks of lost working time. A quote that leaves out out-of-hours work, phasing or temporary works is not cheaper — it is incomplete, and the missing lines come back later. Frequently asked questions.

Can you refurbish an office while staff are working in it?

Yes, in most cases. The floor is split into zones, staff move out of each zone in turn, and the works move round them. Noisy and dusty jobs go out of hours, the site is sealed off with full-height screens, and escape routes stay open every day. It is slower and costs more than an empty floor, and some work — asbestos removal or a floor-wide air conditioning replacement — is better done with the area empty.

How do you phase an office refurbishment?

Survey the floor and split it into zones that can each be closed off without blocking a fire exit. Agree with each team when it can move and where to. Do shared works such as power, data and the comms room first, out of hours. Then work zone by zone, handing each one back snagged, cleaned and tested before the next starts.

What noisy work has to be done out of hours?

Breaking out, drilling into concrete, cutting, chasing walls, lifting floors and heavy deliveries usually go in evenings and weekends, so your team and other tenants can keep working. Your landlord’s building rules set the hours. If homes are nearby, the borough can also control construction noise under the Control of Pollution Act 1974, so agree hours with both.

How do you keep fire escape routes open during building work?

Plan them before the screens go up and check them daily. The responsible person reviews the fire risk assessment for each phase, and the contractor’s site fire plan under CDM 2015 fits with it. Escape routes are marked and kept clear, any alarm isolation is reinstated before staff return, hot works need a permit and a fire watch, and waste leaves site every day.

Does refurbishing while occupied cost more?

Usually, yes. Each phase repeats set-up, protection, cleaning and snagging; out-of-hours work costs more per hour; and the programme is longer. Against that, you avoid a temporary office and lost working time. Ask for phasing, out-of-hours work and temporary works as separate lines in the quote so you can compare like with like.

Do I need an asbestos survey before refurbishing an office?

If the building may contain asbestos — HSE advises that buildings built before 2000 can — a refurbishment and demolition survey is needed before work disturbs the fabric. Ask the landlord for the asbestos register first. Any removal should be done with the area empty.
