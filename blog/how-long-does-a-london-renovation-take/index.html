How Long Does a London Renovation Take? Weeks, Stages and Delays

A London renovation takes 14–22 weeks on site for a whole house, on 2VP’s published figures, plus the design, approvals and notices that come first.

By Vladimir Castravet 11 November 2026 10 min read Updated November 2026
Amott Road · Peckham, SE15 · dormer loft, rear and side infill, ground-floor refurbishment 14 weeks on site · completed 2VP project

How long does a London renovation take? On 2VP’s published figures, a whole-house renovation runs 14–22 weeks on site, a loft conversion 10–14 weeks and a single-storey rear extension 14–20 weeks. Those are site weeks. Before the first day on site come design, any planning or prior approval, party wall notices and the lead time on windows and joinery — and those, not the build, are where most London projects lose time. This guide sets out both halves, the order the stages run in on site, and the reasons projects run late, so you can plan the months around the build as well as the build itself. 2VP builds renovations and has an interest in how you choose a builder, so weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • On site: whole-house renovation 14–22 weeks; loft 10–14 weeks; single-storey rear extension 14–20 weeks (2VP published figures).
  • Combined jobs can share one programme. Amott Road in Peckham — a dormer loft, a rear and side infill extension and a ground-floor refurbishment — was 14 weeks on site.
  • Before site: a householder planning application has an 8-week target; prior approval up to 42 days; party wall notices one to two months; windows 4–8 weeks and bespoke joinery 8–12 weeks to make.
  • Projects run late for predictable reasons: decisions made late, things ordered late, what is found when the house is opened up, and changes mid-build.
  • The fix is written down: a programme, a decisions schedule and a long-lead order list, agreed before you sign.

What a renovation timeline actually means

A renovation timeline has two parts, and most quotes only show one. The pre-construction period runs from your first brief to the day the builder takes over the house: design, surveys, engineering, approvals, notices and ordering. The construction period runs from the first day on site to practical completion — the point at which you can use the house, with only minor snags left.

When a builder quotes weeks, they almost always mean the second part. When a homeowner asks “how long”, they usually mean both. Ask for the two to be shown separately.

How long does a full house renovation take in London?

On 2VP’s published figures, 14–22 weeks on site for a whole house. The spread is the scope: a refurbishment of an otherwise sound house sits at the short end; one with structural alterations, a new layout, rewiring, new heating and an extension sits at the long end. How long does a kitchen or bathroom renovation take?

2VP does not publish a site-weeks figure for a kitchen-only or bathroom-only job, so this page does not give one. What decides it is the same list as for a whole house, on a smaller scale: whether the layout moves (new drainage and electrics mean more first-fix work and possibly building control), the lead time on units, worktops and tiles, and drying time for screeds and plaster. Ask for a written programme before you sign. The kitchen renovation cost guide for London lists the trades involved.

The stages on site, in order

Every renovation runs through the same sequence. The number of weeks in each depends on the house, but the order does not change, and each stage has to finish — and be checked — before the next one covers it up.

  1. Set-up and protection. Scaffold, welfare, floor and stair protection, skip or grab licence, parking suspensions.
  2. Strip-out. Old finishes, kitchens, bathrooms and services removed. This is when the house tells you what is wrong with it.
  3. Structure. Steels, padstones, new openings, foundations or underpinning, floor and roof structure. Building control inspects at key points.
  4. First fix. New drainage, pipework, wiring and ventilation routes run through walls and floors before they are closed.
  5. Close-in and plaster. Insulation, fire-stopping, boarding, plaster and screeds — then drying time, set by the material manufacturer, before anything goes on top.
  6. Second fix. Sockets, switches, radiators, sanitaryware, doors, skirtings and the kitchen fitted.
  7. Finishes. Tiling, flooring, decoration, joinery.
  8. Commissioning, certificates and snagging. Systems tested, certificates collected, snags listed and closed.

Drying time is the step most often squeezed. Tiling on a screed that is not dry, or decorating fresh plaster, is how cracks and failed adhesives appear in the first year.

Can I live in my house during a renovation?

For a loft conversion, often yes — much of it is built from the scaffold. For a rear extension, the kitchen is usually out of action. For a whole-house renovation with no working kitchen, bathroom, heating or water for parts of the programme, most people move out. Living in can also slow the job: rooms have to be cleared and protected, services kept running and work sequenced around you. Rent, storage and travel are on no builder’s quote, so put them in your own budget for the full on-site period.

Why London renovations run late

Projects rarely run late for mysterious reasons. The same handful of causes come up again and again, and most of them are decided before anyone starts on site.

1. Decisions made late

Tiles, taps, kitchen layout, socket positions, the colour of a window frame. Each one is small, but if it is not decided by the date the trade needs it, that trade waits — and so does every trade behind it. A decisions schedule, listing what you need to choose and by when, is the single most useful document in a renovation.

2. Things ordered late

Windows at 4–8 weeks and bespoke joinery at 8–12 weeks have to be ordered early, not when the room is ready for them. Steels can only be ordered once the engineer’s design is approved. Utility work — a bigger electricity supply, a new water connection — has lead times outside the builder’s control.

3. What the house hides

London’s Victorian and Edwardian housing hides things until it is opened up: rotten joists, lintels that have lost their bearing, old repairs, foundations shallower than expected. These are real, and an honest programme carries time for them. Why foundation depth changes on site is a good example of how a discovery changes the plan and why that is not a builder’s trick.

4. Changes mid-build

Moving a wall, adding a room, changing a kitchen after it is ordered. Each variation can mean re-design, re-approval and re-ordering. Some are worth it. All of them should be priced and programmed in writing before they are built.

5. A programme written to win the job

A short programme on a quote can be a sales number rather than a plan. If one builder’s weeks are far below another’s for the same scope, ask what is missing. Two quotes for the same job shows how to compare what is inside each.

6. Payments that run ahead of the work

When a payment schedule is front-loaded, the builder has already been paid for work not yet done, and the pressure to finish your job drops. Payments tied to completed, checked stages keep the money and the programme moving together — see builder payment schedules and front-loaded pricing.

7. London itself

Parking suspensions, skip and scaffold licences, deliveries on narrow streets and working-hours limits all add friction. Most boroughs restrict noisy work to weekday daytimes and Saturday mornings, using powers under the Control of Pollution Act 1974; the exact hours vary by borough. External work also depends on the weather.

What drives the time — and the cost of delay

Time is a cost, even when it is not on the quote. These are the things that move the length of your programme, and what each delay costs you.

  • Scope and structure. Structural alterations, an extension or underpinning add whole stages, not days.
  • How complete the design is at contract. A fully specified job runs to programme far more often than one with “to be confirmed” on half the lines.
  • Long-lead items. Windows, joinery, steels and special-order finishes, and whether they were ordered at contract.
  • Approvals and notices. Planning, party wall and building control, and whether they were started in time.
  • Living in or moving out. Living in usually lengthens the programme.
  • The cost of delay to you. Extra weeks of rent, storage and travel, and the scaffold hire that runs on while it stands. Ask your quote to say who pays for standing time in each case.

Frequently asked questions.

How long does a full house renovation take in London?

On 2VP’s published figures, a whole-house renovation runs 14–22 weeks on site. Design, any planning or listed building consent, party wall notices and lead times for windows and joinery come before that. A combined job can share one programme: Amott Road in Peckham, a dormer loft with a rear and side infill extension and ground-floor refurbishment, was 14 weeks on site.

How long does a kitchen or bathroom renovation take?

2VP does not publish a site-weeks figure for a single room. The length depends on whether the layout moves, which brings new drainage, electrics and possibly building control; the lead time on units, worktops and tiles; and drying time for screeds and plaster. Ask for a written programme that shows each stage before you sign.

What has to happen before work starts?

Design and a measured survey; planning permission (8-week target) or prior approval (up to 42 days) if needed; structural design and building control approval; party wall notices, which take one to two months; and orders for long-lead items — windows take 4–8 weeks and bespoke joinery 8–12 weeks to make. Several of these can run in parallel.

Why do building projects run late?

The common causes are decisions made late, long-lead items ordered late, problems found when an old house is opened up, changes made mid-build, a programme written to win the job rather than to plan it, payments that run ahead of the work, and London access and working-hours limits. Most of them can be controlled before you sign.

Can I live in my house during a renovation?

Often during a loft conversion, which is largely built from the scaffold. A rear extension usually takes the kitchen out of use, and a whole-house renovation usually means moving out for at least part of the programme. Living in can lengthen the job, and rent, storage and travel are on no builder’s quote.

How do I keep my renovation on schedule?

Finish the design before you sign, agree a decisions schedule with dates, order long-lead items at contract, serve party wall notices early, and price and programme every change in writing before it is built. Tie payments to completed, checked stages, and ask for daily progress reports rather than weekly updates.
