Structural Engineer vs Architect in London — Who You Need

Structural engineer vs architect in London: the architect decides what gets built and gets it consented; the engineer proves it will stand up.

By Vladimir Castravet 17 October 2026 12 min read Updated October 2026
Amott Road · Peckham, SE15 · architect Nick Elias · rear and side infill, dormer loft, cellar tanking 14 weeks on site · completed 2VP project

Structural engineer vs architect in London comes down to one line: the architect decides what the building is and gets it consented; the structural engineer proves it stands up, with calculations and drawings that building control will check. Most London jobs that move a wall, a roof or a foundation need both. Some need one. A few need neither. This guide sets out who does what, which job needs whom, what each fee costs on 2VP’s own estimator, and when you are better off appointing your own architect than buying design through a builder. 2VP sells design and build — the designers and the build under one contract — so it has a commercial interest in the answer. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • The architect answers “what is it, and is it allowed?” Layout, appearance, planning drawings, building regulations drawings and the specification.
  • The structural engineer answers “will it stand up, and can you prove it?” Beams, padstones, connections and foundations, with calculations for building control.
  • “Architect” is a protected title. Under section 20 of the Architects Act 1997, only people on the ARB register may practise under it. “Architectural designer” and “architectural technologist” are not protected.
  • If a load moves, you need an engineer. A load-bearing wall, a chimney breast, a dormer’s steels, an extension’s foundations.
  • On 2VP’s estimator the architect or designer is allowed at 7% of the contract ex VAT (range 6–12%), and the engineer at a fixed £1,500–£3,500 by project type. All NET of VAT.
  • Design and build puts both under one contract. An independent architect is the better call for listed buildings, difficult planning, and when you want to own the design and tender it.

What a structural engineer vs an architect actually means

The two roles overlap on every drawing and answer different questions. Confusing them is how a project ends up with a beautiful planning consent and no idea how big the steel is.

The architect: what gets built, and whether it is allowed

The architect or designer turns your brief into a building. On a London extension or loft that means a measured survey, concept options, the planning drawings, the building regulations drawings and a specification a builder can price. 2VP’s estimator scopes the role as concept, planning, tender and site visits — RIBA Stages 1 to 5. The architect owns the look, the layout, the planning strategy and how the parts fit together.

The protected title — and the titles that are not

Section 20 of the Architects Act 1997 makes it an offence to practise or carry on business under a name or title containing the word “architect” unless you are registered with the Architects Registration Board. You can check anyone on the ARB register online in a minute. “Architectural designer”, “architectural technologist” and “plan drawer” are not protected. Plenty of very good rear extensions are drawn by people with those titles. The point is not that only architects can draw. The point is that the word tells you something checkable, and the other words do not.

The structural engineer: proving it stands up

Schedule 1 of the Building Regulations 2010 sets the structural requirements in Part A: A1, loading; A2, ground movement; and A3, disproportionate collapse. Approved Document A shows ways to meet them. When you take out a wall, add a storey or dig a foundation, building control will ask how you know the structure complies. The answer is the engineer’s package: calculations, beam and column sizes, padstones and bearings, connection details and the foundation design.

“Structural engineer” is not itself a protected title in the UK. Chartered status is the check. Look for MIStructE (Institution of Structural Engineers) or MICE (Institution of Civil Engineers), usually alongside CEng, chartered engineer registration with the Engineering Council.

Approved Documents are statutory guidance to the Building Regulations 2010 for England. Compliance is judged against the Requirements in Schedule 1; the Approved Document shows one way of meeting them. Your building control body has the final word on your building.

Where the two meet — and where the gap sits

The architect draws a 4.5-metre opening at the back of the house. The engineer sizes the steel that spans it and the padstones that carry it. The builder props the house while the steel goes in. Three people, one opening. The gap is coordination: who checks that the steel fits in the ceiling depth the architect drew, and that the foundation the engineer designed matches the ground found on site.

The Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 also apply to domestic work. Under regulation 7, the duties of a domestic client pass to the contractor where there is one contractor, or to the principal contractor where there is more than one — unless you agree in writing that the principal designer takes them on. In practice, that means the people you appoint carry the health and safety paperwork, not you.

Key takeaway The architect decides what the building is. The engineer proves that it stands up. Someone still has to make the two agree on site — decide who that is before you sign anything.

The three questions that sort any job

  1. Does it change the outside of the house or how it is used? Then it needs planning drawings, and you need a designer.
  2. Does any load move? A wall, a beam, a roof, a foundation. Then you need an engineer, and building control will want the calculations.
  3. Does it touch a shared wall, or dig within 3m of a neighbour’s building and below its foundations? Then the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 applies, and the engineer’s drawings usually go with the notice.

The chimney breast and the knock-through are where people get caught. Neither needs planning, so neither feels like a “project”. Both remove structure. A chimney breast taken out downstairs leaves a stack upstairs with nothing under it. A knocked-through wall often carries floor joists. Both need an engineer, a steel or a designed support, and a building control sign-off you will be asked for when you sell.

When to appoint your own architect instead

Design and build is not always the right answer, and a builder who says it is would be selling. Appoint your own independent architect when:

The planning is the hard part. A listed building, a sensitive conservation area, or a scheme the borough is likely to resist. You want someone whose only job is the consent, and who has argued that borough’s policies before.
You want to own the design and tender it. An architect-led tender gets several builders pricing the same drawings, which is the only fair way to compare them. Read why two quotes for the same job differ before you do.
You want someone checking the builder for you. An architect acting as contract administrator certifies payments and practical completion on your side of the table. That role sits outside a design and build contract by definition.
The design ambition is the point. If the house is meant to be a piece of architecture, hire the architect whose work you want, then choose the builder.

2VP builds to independent architects’ drawings as well. Amott Road in Peckham, pictured above, was designed by architect Nick Elias and built by 2VP. The trade-off is coordination: with separate appointments, you are the one who makes the architect, the engineer and the builder agree. For a straightforward rear extension, side return, loft or refurbishment, one contract is usually the simpler, safer route. For a listed house or a contested consent, it often is not.

Frequently asked questions.

Do I need an architect or a structural engineer for an extension in London?

Usually both. The architect or designer produces the planning drawings and the building regulations drawings; the structural engineer designs the foundations and steels and produces the calculations building control will check under Part A of the Building Regulations 2010. Planning law does not require you to use a registered architect — a competent architectural designer can draw a rear extension — but any extension that carries load or removes part of the existing wall needs an engineer.

Can a structural engineer do my planning drawings?

Some can, but it is not their job. Planning drawings decide what the building looks like and argue why it should be allowed; engineering drawings prove it stands up. Anyone can submit a planning application, so there is no legal bar, but a design that has not been thought through as architecture tends to cost more to change later. The usual arrangement is a designer for planning and an engineer for the structure, working from the same measured survey.

Is architect a protected title in the UK?

Yes. Section 20 of the Architects Act 1997 makes it an offence to practise or carry on business under a name or title containing the word architect unless you are registered with the Architects Registration Board. You can check the ARB register online. Architectural designer and architectural technologist are not protected titles. Structural engineer is not a protected title either; check for chartered membership — MIStructE or MICE — and CEng.

How much does a structural engineer cost in London?

2VP’s estimator allows a fixed fee by project type: £1,500 for a flat or full-house refurbishment, £2,500 for a loft conversion, £3,000 for a rear or wrap-around extension, and £3,500 for a basement, heritage or new-build project. These are NET of VAT, and they are the fees 2VP allows when it prices a job, not a survey of the market. On a 25m² Balanced rear extension at £104,625 ex VAT, the engineer’s £3,000 is under 3% of the contract.

Do I need a structural engineer to remove a chimney breast?

Yes. Removing a chimney breast takes away the support for whatever sits above it — often the rest of the breast and the stack. An engineer designs the replacement support, building control approves it, and if the breast is on a party wall, section 2 of the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 applies and notice has to be served on your neighbour. Keep the completion certificate: a buyer’s solicitor will ask for it.

Is design and build cheaper than hiring an architect separately?

Not necessarily, and you should be wary of anyone who says it always is. On 2VP’s estimator the architect or designer is allowed at 7% of the contract ex VAT either way. What design and build changes is responsibility: one contract, one party answerable when the drawings and the site disagree. An independent architect gives you competitive tendering and someone checking the builder on your behalf — worth it for listed buildings, hard planning and design-led projects.
