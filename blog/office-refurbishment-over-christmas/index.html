Office Refurbishment Over Christmas: Why You Book It in November

Office refurbishment over Christmas works only if you book it in November: licence to alter, building control, long-lead orders and landlord access all take weeks.

By Vladimir Castravet 2 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Chester Square · London · 2VP project exterior Shown for context · residential, not an office

An office refurbishment over Christmas is the one time of year your floor can be empty for more than a long weekend without anyone losing a working day. That is why so many London firms ask for it. It is also why so many find out in mid-December that it cannot happen: the landlord has not consented, the building control application is not in, the partitions are still at the factory and the building manager is on leave. This guide sets out what has to be done in November for the holiday shutdown to be useful, what kind of work actually fits, and where the money goes. 2VP sells office refurbishment, so it has an interest in you booking one. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • The window is shorter than it looks. If your office closes after Wednesday 23 December 2026 and reopens on Monday 4 January 2027, it is empty for 11 days. Only 4 of them (24, 29, 30 and 31 December) are ordinary weekdays. The rest are weekends or bank holidays.
  • Everything that takes weeks has to start in November. Licence to alter, building control, long-lead orders, the asbestos survey and the landlord’s access rules all sit before the first day on site.
  • The scope has to fit the window, not the other way round. Decoration, flooring, lighting, small partition changes and meeting pods fit. A full strip-out and new air conditioning usually do not, unless you phase them.
  • Get access agreed in writing. Many London buildings run reduced management and security over the holidays. No access, no works.
  • Plan the first Monday back. Cleaning, IT reconnection, snagging and fire alarm reinstatement happen before your team walks in, not after.

What an office refurbishment over Christmas actually means

It means the noisy, dusty and disruptive part of the work happens while your team is away, so the business never has to work around a building site. It does not mean the project starts at Christmas. The project starts weeks earlier, on paper, and the holiday is only the part where the floor is open to the trades.

Why the holiday shutdown is the quiet window

Most firms already close between Christmas and New Year, or run a skeleton team. There is no rent to pay for decant space, no meeting room to fence off, no out-of-hours premium every single night for weeks. Neighbouring tenants are usually away too, which matters for noise. The trade-off is that suppliers, merchants, building managers and building control also slow down, and that is the risk you are planning around.

Crews can work weekends and bank holidays, but you pay for it, and your building may not let them in. If you can close from Friday 18 December, you add four ordinary weekdays and a weekend. That one decision often matters more than anything else in the plan.

When you need to start planning in London

For a Christmas start, the paperwork needs to be moving in the first half of November. Here is why each item takes the time it does.

How long does a licence to alter take?

Longer than you think, because it runs on the landlord’s timetable, not yours. Most office leases let you make non-structural alterations only with the landlord’s consent, and the landlord will want drawings, a method statement, your contractor’s insurances and often its own surveyor’s review, which you usually pay for. Where the lease says consent cannot be unreasonably withheld, the Landlord and Tenant Act 1988 puts the landlord under a duty to decide within a reasonable time, but “reasonable” is not a date. Send a complete pack in early November. The licence to alter for an office fit out guide sets out what goes in it.

Do I need building control for an office refurbishment?

Often yes. New partitions that change escape routes, new or altered fire doors, new lighting circuits, changes to ventilation and new washrooms are all building work under the Building Regulations 2010. Offices are workplaces covered by the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, and for those buildings building control normally expects a full plans application rather than a building notice, so the drawings need to be done and submitted early. Like-for-like decoration and carpet replacement usually do not need building control. If you are not sure which side of the line your scope sits, ask before you book the trades, not after.

Why long-lead items decide the date

Anything made to order sets the critical path: glazed partitions, doors and frames, acoustic pods, joinery, light fittings and some carpet ranges. Lead times move during the year, so ask every supplier for a delivery date in writing before you confirm the start. Merchants and factories close over Christmas too, which means every item has to be on site, or in a store you can reach, before the shutdown begins. Nothing will be delivered on 29 December.

Asbestos and CDM 2015

If your building was built before 2000, the duty holder must manage asbestos under regulation 4 of the Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012. Before refurbishment work that disturbs the fabric, a refurbishment survey of the affected area is normally needed, and the results can change the plan. Book it in early November. As a commercial client you also have duties under the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015: appoint a contractor who can show they are competent, give them the information you hold about the building, and allow enough time for the work to be done safely. A compressed Christmas programme is exactly where “enough time” gets tested.

The November checklist, week by week

Work back from the first Monday of January. This is the order 2VP would put it in for a small London office of one floor or less.

  1. Week of 2 November — fix the scope. Walk the floor with your contractor, agree the list, and split it into “must be done over Christmas” and “can be done in the evenings in January”. Ask for an Open-Book Itemised Quote against that scope.
  2. Week of 2 November — book the asbestos survey if the building predates 2000 and the work will disturb ceilings, floors or walls. Ask the landlord for the existing asbestos register first.
  3. Week of 9 November — send the licence to alter pack. Drawings, specification, method statement, programme, insurance certificates and the working hours you want. Ask the landlord’s agent for a target date in writing.
  4. Week of 9 November — submit to building control if the scope needs it. Include the fire strategy changes and any new emergency lighting.
  5. Week of 16 November — place long-lead orders once the design is signed off, and get delivery dates in writing. Book a delivery slot before the shutdown.
  6. Week of 16 November — agree access with building management. Holiday opening hours, security cover, the loading bay and goods lift, hot works permits, fire alarm isolation, and who holds the keys.
  7. Week of 23 November — plan the IT. Your IT provider must disconnect and protect equipment before the trades arrive and reconnect it before the team returns. Book them for both dates now.
  8. Week of 30 November — tell your team and your neighbours. Clear desks, label what stays and what goes, and give the other tenants a contact number for the works.
  9. Mid-December — pre-start meeting. Confirm deliveries, access, the daily programme and the handover date. Agree who walks the floor with you on 3 or 4 January.

What office works can be done in two weeks?

It depends on the floor size and how many trades can work at once, so treat this as a guide rather than a promise. Work that typically fits a holiday shutdown on a small floor:

  • Full redecoration, including doors and frames.
  • Replacing carpet tiles or other floor finishes on a sound floor.
  • Swapping light fittings for LED panels on the existing circuits and grid.
  • Small partition changes: one or two meeting rooms or a phone booth, if the parts are on site.
  • Installing freestanding acoustic pods and furniture.
  • Tea point refresh where the drainage and water supply stay where they are.

Work that usually does not fit a single shutdown: new air conditioning, moving washrooms or drainage, a full strip-out back to shell, and anything waiting on a landlord’s structural engineer. These can still be done with your team in place, phased in evenings and weekends — see office fit-out without demolition for how that works — or planned as a longer project using the office fit out timeline in London as the starting point.

Will my landlord allow works over the holidays?

Usually, if you ask early and in writing. Landlords care about three things: that the work is consented, that the building is secure, and that fire safety is not compromised while the alarm is partly isolated or doors are propped. Some buildings close fully over Christmas or allow contractors in only on certain days. Ask for the holiday access hours before you agree the programme, not once the crew is standing in the lobby.

What drives the cost of a Christmas refurbishment

Doing the work over the holiday does not change what the materials cost. It changes how the labour is organised and what you pay around it. These are the lines to look for in your quote, and the questions to ask.

The hidden cost is the one you do not see on the quote: a project that runs past the first Monday back. Your team then works around the site, which is the thing you were trying to avoid. That is why the scope decision in the first week of November matters more than any rate.

The counter-case: sometimes January is the better plan

If the licence to alter will not be in place by mid-December, or the scope needs new air conditioning, do not force it. A rushed holiday job with half the materials missing leaves you with a building site in January anyway. Use the shutdown for the part that fits — decoration and flooring, say — and phase the rest. A project that is honest about the window costs less than one that tries to beat it.
