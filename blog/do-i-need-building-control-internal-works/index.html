Do I Need Building Control for Internal Works in London?

You need building control for internal works when they are structural, change drainage, fire safety or ventilation, or include notifiable electrics; decorating does not.

By Vladimir Castravet 9 November 2026 10 min read Updated November 2026
Alexandra · N22 · whole-house renovation Completed · 2VP project

Do I need building control for internal works? The honest answer is: it depends on what the work does, not where it is. Taking down a wall that holds up the floor above, adding a shower with a new waste, rewiring a kitchen or moving a door off a staircase are all building work under the Building Regulations 2010, even though nobody outside will see them. Painting, new flooring and a like-for-like kitchen are not. This guide gives you a yes/no table for the common internal jobs, the three ways to get the work signed off, what drives the fees, and what to do if work was done without approval. 2VP manages building control on its own projects and has a page on it at 2vp.uk/building-control; this post answers the question first, and you can weigh it knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Yes for anything structural: removing or altering a load-bearing wall, taking out a chimney breast, new steels, new openings in a structural wall.
  • Yes for new drainage, a new bathroom or WC on a new waste, new heating appliances, and work that changes ventilation in a kitchen or bathroom.
  • Yes for anything that affects how you escape in a fire: an open-plan ground floor in a house with a loft room, removing doors off a stair, new rooms off the stair.
  • Notifiable electrics — a new circuit, a new consumer unit, or work in a room with a bath or shower — need sign-off, usually through a registered electrician who self-certifies.
  • No for decorating, flooring, plastering repairs, replacing kitchen units or a bathroom suite in the same place on the same drainage, and like-for-like repairs.
  • Building control is not planning. Internal works rarely need planning permission — unless the house is listed.

What building control actually means

Building control is the check that building work meets the Building Regulations 2010 — the legal minimum standards for structure, fire safety, ventilation, drainage, energy and access. It is a different thing from planning permission. Planning asks whether you may build something; building control asks whether it has been built safely.

What counts as “building work”?

Regulation 3 of the Building Regulations 2010 defines it. For internal works, three parts of the definition matter:

  • Material alteration — work that, while it is being done or once it is finished, would make the building worse than before against the requirements for structure, fire escape and fire spread, or access. Taking out a load-bearing wall is the classic example.
  • Controlled services and fittings — installing or extending things like drainage, sanitary fittings, heating appliances, hot water systems, ventilation and fixed electrical installations.
  • Renovating a thermal element — for example stripping and re-plastering or dry-lining a large part of an external wall, which can trigger an insulation upgrade.

The detailed standards sit in Schedule 1 to the Regulations and are explained in the Approved Documents: A for structure, B for fire safety, F for ventilation, P for electrical safety in dwellings, and so on.

Approved Documents are statutory guidance to the Building Regulations 2010 for England. They show one way of meeting the requirements, not the only way. Your building control body has the final word on your building.

Who is responsible: you or the builder?

Both. Since the Building Safety Act 2022 reforms, the client, the designer and the contractor each carry legal duties for compliance. If you commission the work, you are the client, with duties of your own; client dutyholder duties under the building regulations sets out what that means for a London homeowner.

Do I need building control to remove an internal wall?

If the wall is load-bearing, yes, always. You need a structural engineer to design the support — usually a steel beam on padstones — and building control to approve the calculations and inspect the work. That steel also needs fire protection; steel beam fire protection and propping explains what to expect on site. If the wall is a non-structural stud partition, it is usually not building work, unless taking it out affects how people escape in a fire.

Do I need building control for a new bathroom?

For a new bathroom where there was none, or one on a new waste route, yes: drainage, sanitary fittings and ventilation are all covered. Replacing a suite in the same place usually is not — but new circuits or alterations to the electrics in a room with a bath or shower are notifiable. The bathroom renovation cost guide for London breaks down the trades involved.

Why fire safety catches people out

In a house with a floor above the first floor — a loft room, or a three-storey terrace — the stair is usually the protected escape route. Knocking the hall into the living room or taking doors off can remove that protection. That turns a non-structural job into a material alteration, and building control will expect a fire-safe solution, such as fire doors, a sprinkler or misting system or a revised layout. What goes inside the walls matters too; see fire-stopping before the walls are closed.

Do I need planning permission for internal works?

Usually no. Works that affect only the inside of a house are not normally treated as development under the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. The exception is a listed building: internal works that affect its character need listed building consent under the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Act 1990, and working without it is a criminal offence. Flats have one more layer: your lease may require the freeholder’s written consent before you alter anything.

The three ways to get sign-off

There are three routes, and the right one depends on the job.

Full plans application. Drawings and calculations are checked before work starts, then the work is inspected at stages. The building control body must decide within five weeks, or up to two months if you agree. It is the right route for structural work: you know the design is approved before the steel is ordered.
Building notice. You give notice and can start two days later, with no plan approval. The inspector checks the work as it goes. It suits small, simple jobs, but you carry the risk that something already built has to be changed.
Competent person scheme. Registered installers — electricians, Gas Safe engineers, window installers — self-certify their own work and notify building control for you. You receive a compliance certificate. No separate application is needed for that part of the job.

You can use your council’s building control service or a registered building control approver, a private body regulated under the Building Safety Act 2022 regime. Either way, at the end you should receive a completion certificate (from the council) or a final certificate (from an approver). Keep it: a buyer’s solicitor will ask for it when you sell.

What drives the cost of building control

2VP does not publish a building control fee on this page: councils set their own charges, and the right figure depends on the job. These are the things that move it.

  • The route. A competent person scheme adds nothing as a separate application. A full plans application has a plan-check charge and an inspection charge. A building notice combines them.
  • The scope. Charges are usually set by the type of work and its estimated cost, so one structural opening costs less to approve than a whole floor of alterations.
  • Engineering. Structural work needs an engineer’s design and calculations, which are a separate fee from building control itself.
  • Inspections. More stages — foundations for a padstone, steel, drainage, fire-stopping, completion — mean more visits.
  • Regularisation. Retrospective approval usually costs more than applying at the start, and opening up finished work to prove it complies adds builder’s costs on top.
  • Party wall. Structural work on or near a party wall brings surveyor fees too; party wall agreement costs in London explains how they are set.

Ask for the building control charge, the engineer’s fee and any party wall fees as separate lines in your quote, and ask who is making each application.

What happens if work was done without building control

Work that needed approval and did not get it is a breach of the Building Regulations, and the responsibility can follow the owner. The council can take enforcement action and require the work to be altered or removed. More often, the problem surfaces when you sell: the buyer’s solicitor asks for the completion certificate and there is none.

Can I get approval after the work is done?

Usually, through a regularisation application to your council. An inspector will want to see the work, which can mean opening up floors, ceilings or boxing round steels, and you may have to put right anything that does not comply. Regularisation is available from the council only, not from a private approver. If you are buying a house where work was done without approval, take advice from your solicitor and surveyor before you exchange.

Frequently asked questions.

Do I need building control to remove an internal wall?

Yes if the wall is load-bearing: you need a structural engineer’s design and calculations, approval from building control and inspections of the work. A non-load-bearing stud wall usually does not need building control, unless removing it affects the escape route in a fire — for example opening the hallway into a room in a house with a loft room or a second floor.

Do I need building control for a new bathroom?

Yes for a new bathroom, WC or shower where there was none, or one on new drainage: drainage, sanitary fittings and ventilation are controlled. Replacing a suite in the same place on the same waste usually is not building work, but new circuits or alterations to circuits in a room with a bath or shower are notifiable electrical work.

Is electrical work notifiable?

Some of it. Under Approved Document P, a new circuit, a replacement consumer unit and additions or alterations to circuits in a room with a bath or shower are notifiable. A registered electrician in a competent person scheme can self-certify the work and notify building control for you. Replacing a socket or switch like-for-like is not notifiable.

What is the difference between a building notice and full plans?

A full plans application has drawings and calculations approved before work starts, then inspections as the work goes. A building notice lets work start two days after notice is given, with no plan approval, and the inspector checks the work as it is built. Full plans suits structural work; a building notice suits small, simple jobs.

What happens if I did work without building control?

The work is in breach of the Building Regulations and the council can take enforcement action. It most often surfaces when you sell and there is no completion certificate. You can usually apply to the council for a regularisation certificate; an inspector may need parts of the work opened up and anything non-compliant put right.

Do I need planning permission for internal works?

Usually no: works that affect only the inside of a house are not normally development. A listed building is the exception — internal works that affect its character need listed building consent, and working without it is a criminal offence. In a flat, check your lease: the freeholder’s written consent may be needed before you alter anything.
