What Is a JCT Homeowner Contract? A London Guide

A JCT homeowner contract is a plain-English standard building contract for home projects that fixes price, programme, payments, changes and defects before work starts.

By Vladimir Castravet 17 October 2026 11 min read Updated October 2026
Moncrieff · SE15 · loft and extension · shown illustratively Completed 2VP project · contract terms not published

A JCT homeowner contract is a standard building contract, published by the Joint Contracts Tribunal, written for people having work done to the home they live in. It puts the price, the dates, the payments, the change process and the defects period on paper before anyone starts. It is one good option, not the only one. What protects you is not the logo at the top of the contract but the clauses inside it, and the law that applies whatever you sign. This guide covers both. 2VP is a design-and-build contractor that writes contracts for a living — a commercial interest, declared here, so weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • JCT is the Joint Contracts Tribunal, the body that publishes the standard building contracts most of the UK industry uses.
  • Two homeowner forms. The Homeowner Contract (HO/B) is for dealing directly with your builder. The Homeowner Contract with Consultant Administrator (HO/C) is for when an architect or surveyor runs the contract for you.
  • Bigger or more complex jobs often move to Minor Works (MW), Intermediate (IC) or Design and Build (DB) forms, usually with a consultant involved.
  • The law protects you anyway. The Consumer Rights Act 2015 requires reasonable care and skill (section 49) whatever contract you sign.
  • Read the clauses, not the cover. Price, programme, payments, variations, retention, defects, insurance, disputes and what happens if the builder fails — ten checks below.

What a JCT homeowner contract actually means

The JCT produces families of contracts for different sizes of job and different ways of buying a building. Most are written for professional clients. The homeowner forms are the exception: short, in plain English, and designed for a consumer, not a developer.

HO/B — you and the builder

The Homeowner Contract is for where you deal directly with the builder. No architect is administering the contract; you agree the work, the price and the payments with the builder yourself. It suits a defined job with drawings or a clear schedule: a kitchen extension, a loft, a refurbishment.

HO/C — with a consultant administrator

The Homeowner Contract with Consultant Administrator adds a third party: an architect or surveyor you appoint to manage the contract on your behalf, check work and value payments. You pay for that service, and you get a professional between you and the builder.

What sits inside either form

  • The price and what it covers, by reference to drawings, specification or schedule.
  • The programme — start and completion dates.
  • Payments — when and how much.
  • Variations — how changes are instructed and priced.
  • Insurance — who insures the works and the existing house.
  • Defects and snagging — what the builder must put right after completion.
  • Disputes — how a disagreement is resolved without going straight to court.

JCT revises its forms from time to time. Before you sign, check which edition is current on JCT’s own site, and that your copy is that edition and unamended — or that every amendment is shown.

When you need a JCT homeowner contract in London

You never legally need a JCT form. You do need a written contract that covers the clauses below, and on a London extension, loft or whole-house renovation — jobs that run from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand pounds — you need it before any money moves.

MW, IC and DB were drafted for commercial clients. If you sign one as a homeowner you are still a consumer, and Part 2 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 still requires its terms to be fair and transparent. Your consultant should adapt the form for a consumer rather than send you the commercial version.

Design and build vs traditional contract

TraditionalYou appoint an architect to design. Builders price the finished design, and you contract with one of them. If the drawings are wrong or incomplete, the gap is yours to pay for, through variations.
Design and buildOne firm is responsible for the design and the build. Gaps between drawing and site are its problem, not yours. The trade-off: you need a tight brief and a clear specification, or “design” becomes the builder’s choice.

Neither is better in the abstract. A traditional route gives you an independent designer; design and build gives you one point of responsibility. Choose the risk you would rather hold.

What the law gives you whatever you sign

A contract adds to your statutory rights. It cannot take the core ones away.

The statute that does not apply to your house

The Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 gives commercial parties a statutory right to adjudication and fixed payment rules. Section 106 excludes contracts with a residential occupier — work to a dwelling that one of the parties lives in or intends to live in. So you have no statutory adjudication right unless your contract creates one. Check whether yours does, who appoints the adjudicator, and who pays.

This is general information, not legal advice. Building Regulations 2010 approval is separate from your contract and applies whatever you sign; your building control body has the final word on the building.

Ten clauses to check before you sign

Use this list on a JCT homeowner form, a Minor Works contract or a builder’s own terms. If a clause is missing, ask for it in writing.

  1. The contract documents. The quote, drawings, specification and schedule are listed by name and date, with an order of precedence if they disagree.
  2. The price and its exclusions. A fixed sum for the defined work. Every provisional sum and prime cost sum named, with its amount. A quote full of them is a fixed price in name only.
  3. The dates. Start, completion, and what extends the time: your changes, the weather, things found in the walls.
  4. The payment schedule. Payments tied to work done, not to the calendar or to the builder’s purchases. A large deposit or front-loaded stages are a warning sign.
  5. Variations. Every change priced and signed by you before money is spent on it. No verbal instructions, no “we’ll sort it at the end”.
  6. Retention and defects. How much is held back, for how long, and what releases it. A defects period of 12 months is a common benchmark; get the snagging process at completion in writing.
  7. Insurance. Certificates for the builder’s cover before work starts, and who insures the works and your existing house during the build. Tell your home insurer before work starts.
  8. Approvals and certificates. Who coordinates building control, party wall notices, electrical and gas certificates — and that the final building control certificate is a condition of the last payment.
  9. Disputes. A route short of court: adjudication or mediation by contract, with who nominates and who pays.
  10. If the builder fails. What happens on insolvency or abandonment: termination rights, return of materials, and any bond or guarantee that pays for completion.

When a JCT form is overkill

The honest counter-case: not every job needs a standard-form contract.

  • Small, single-trade work. A bathroom — 2VP’s published band is £7.8k–£15.4k net — or redecoration is usually well served by a clear written quote plus short terms: scope, price, dates, payments on completion of stages, and how changes are agreed.
  • A form you have not read is worse than a page you have. Signing 30 pages unread protects you less than two pages you understand.
  • The statutory floor is already there. The Consumer Rights Act 2015 applies to that small job just as it does to a big one.

The line moves up as soon as there is structure, a party wall, building control or a six-figure price. At that point, use a full written contract — JCT or otherwise — that covers all ten clauses.

Frequently asked questions.

What is a JCT homeowner contract?

It is a standard building contract published by the Joint Contracts Tribunal for people having work done to the home they live in. It is written in plain English and sets out the price, the start and completion dates, the payments, how changes are agreed, insurance, what happens to defects and how disputes are resolved. Check which edition is current on JCT’s own site before you sign.

What is the difference between HO/B and HO/C?

HO/B, the Homeowner Contract, is for when you deal directly with the builder. HO/C, the Homeowner Contract with Consultant Administrator, is for when you appoint an architect or surveyor to run the contract for you: checking the work, valuing payments and dealing with the builder on your behalf. HO/C costs you the consultant’s fee; it buys you a professional between you and the builder.

Do I need a JCT contract for a home extension?

No law requires a JCT form. You do need a written contract that covers the price and its exclusions, the dates, a payment schedule tied to work done, signed variations, retention and a defects period, insurance, certificates, a dispute route and what happens if the builder fails. A JCT homeowner form is one way to get those clauses; a builder’s own contract can do the same if it contains them.

What is a fixed price renovation contract?

A contract where the builder agrees one sum for a defined scope of work and carries the risk of its own pricing. The price changes only through variations you sign, or through items the contract names as provisional sums. Count those provisional sums: a quote with many of them is a fixed price in name only.

What is the difference between design and build and a traditional contract?

In a traditional contract you appoint an architect to design, and the builder builds what is drawn; gaps in the drawings become your variations. In design and build one firm is responsible for both design and construction, so gaps between drawing and site are its problem. Traditional gives you an independent designer; design and build gives you a single point of responsibility.

Does the Consumer Rights Act apply if I sign a builder’s contract?

Yes. If you are a consumer having work done to your home, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 applies whatever contract you sign. Section 49 requires the work to be done with reasonable care and skill, section 52 requires it within a reasonable time where no time is agreed, and section 57 stops a contract excluding the section 49 duty. Unfair terms do not bind you under Part 2.
