How Much Office Space Do I Need? A Step-by-Step London Guide

How much office space do I need? Size it on your busiest day’s attendance, not headcount, then add meeting, booth and support space.

By Vladimir Castravet 4 November 2026 12 min read Updated November 2026
Alexandra · N22 · open-plan living space Shown for scale · residential, not an office

The honest answer to how much office space do I need is: less than your headcount suggests, and more than the busiest desk count alone. Since hybrid working settled down, many London firms are taking smaller floors and spending more on each square metre — the “flight to quality” that fit-out contractors and market reports such as Glenigan’s and GXI’s describe for 2026. Getting the size right is the single biggest decision before a fit-out, because it fixes your rent, your service charge, your fit-out cost and your dilapidations for the whole lease. This guide gives you the method, a worked example you can copy, the legal minimums, and the point at which you should stop calculating and ask for a test-fit. 2VP fits out offices, so it has an interest in the floor you choose. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Start from your busiest normal day. Not total staff, not the average. Four to six weeks of attendance data tells you more than any rule of thumb.
  • Desks are only part of it. Meeting rooms, phone booths, a tea point, storage, a comms room and the routes between them often take as much floor as the desks do.
  • Know the legal floor. The Workplace Regulations’ Approved Code of Practice sets 11 cubic metres per person in a workroom. That is a minimum, not a design target.
  • Check the measurement basis. London office rents are usually quoted on net internal area (NIA). 1 m² is 10.764 sq ft.
  • Test-fit before you sign. A layout on the real floor plan catches columns, cores and window lines that no spreadsheet does.

What “how much office space” actually means

It means the area you will lease and pay for, laid out so your team can do its work on its busiest day. Three terms decide the number.

Is office space measured in square feet or square metres?

Both. Agents in London still advertise in square feet; drawings, building regulations and most fit-out specifications work in square metres. Convert carefully: 1 m² = 10.764 sq ft, so a 4,000 sq ft floor is about 372 m². The bigger trap is the basis. Office rents are usually quoted on net internal area (NIA): the usable area inside the walls, excluding cores, stairs, lifts, plant rooms and washrooms shared with the building. Ask the agent which basis applies and which measurement standard was used, because two floors with the same advertised area can lay out very differently.

Headcount, attendance and desks

Headcount is everyone on the payroll who will use the office. Attendance is how many are in on a given day. Desks are how many work settings you provide. Since hybrid working, the gap between the three is where the savings are — and where the mistakes are. Size on average attendance and Tuesday will be standing room only. Size on headcount and you pay rent on empty desks four days a week.

Workspace and support space

Workspace is where people sit: desks, focus rooms, booths. Support space is everything that makes the floor work: reception, meeting rooms, tea point, storage, print, comms, first aid and a quiet room. On a small floor, support space is often the part people forget to count.

When you need the answer in London

You need it before you shortlist floors, and you need it confirmed before you sign heads of terms. After that, every change costs more.

  • Before the search. A range in square feet lets your agent filter the market instead of showing you everything.
  • Before heads of terms. A test-fit on each shortlisted floor shows whether your brief actually fits. It is also your evidence when negotiating rent-free periods and landlord contributions.
  • Before a lease event. A break clause or lease expiry is the moment to ask whether you should stay, downsize or move. Remember that leaving also has a cost — the dilapidations office lease guide explains what you may owe on the way out.
  • When the floor stops working. If meetings spill into corridors and calls happen in the stairwell, you may not need more space — you may need different space.

Should I downsize my office?

Downsize if your peak-day attendance is well below your desk count and is likely to stay there, and if the money saved on rent and service charge can go into a better layout. Do not downsize just because the average looks low; check the busiest day, check what your team is planning to hire, and check how much notice your lease needs. A smaller floor with enough meeting rooms and phone booths usually works better than a larger floor full of unused desks.

Work it out, step by step

To answer how much office space do I need for your own team, run this simple method before a test-fit. The numbers in the worked example are example allowances to show the method, not a standard: your designer’s test-fit on the real floor replaces them.

  1. Measure peak-day attendance. Use badge data, desk bookings or a simple daily count for four to six weeks. Take the busiest normal day — ignore the all-hands and the August week.
  2. Set the desk count. Start from peak attendance. Add regular visitors and the growth you expect during the lease. Decide which roles need a fixed desk (finance, reception, anyone with specialist kit).
  3. List the rooms. Count meeting rooms by size from your calendar data, not from habit. Add phone booths for calls and a focus room if your team needs one.
  4. Add support space. Reception, tea point, storage and lockers, comms room, print and post, first aid and a wellbeing room.
  5. Allow for circulation. The routes that join the zones, plus the escape routes your fire strategy needs.
  6. Test-fit real floors. Columns, cores, window lines and riser positions decide what actually fits.

How many desks do I need for hybrid working?

As many as your busiest normal day needs, plus a buffer. If 40 people use the office and 30 are in on the busiest day, 30 desks is your starting point — a ratio of 0.75 desks per person. Then add for visitors and growth. Track it after you move: a ratio that is too tight shows up within a month as people working from the tea point.

How many meeting rooms does an office need?

Count the meetings, not the people. Look at a month of calendars: how many meetings run at the same time at the peak, how many people attend, and how many are calls with one person in the room. Most hybrid teams find they need fewer large rooms and more small rooms and booths than they had. The office acoustics design guide explains why rooms and booths only work if they are built to keep sound in.

Legal minimums and building rules

The law sets floors, not targets. You will almost always design above them, but they catch the extreme cases — a small room with a lot of people in it.

What is the legal minimum office space per person in the UK?

Regulation 10 of the Workplace (Health, Safety and Welfare) Regulations 1992 requires enough floor area, height and unoccupied space for health, safety and welfare. The Approved Code of Practice (L24) sets 11 cubic metres per person in a workroom, counting no more than 3.0 m of ceiling height. In a room with a 3.0 m ceiling that is about 3.7 m² of floor per person; with a 2.6 m ceiling it is about 4.2 m². The space taken by furniture and equipment is part of the calculation, so in practice you need more.

Fire escape and ventilation set limits too

  • Means of escape. Approved Document B Volume 2 uses a floor space factor to estimate how many people a floor holds, and the number of exits and the stair widths must cope with that number. A dense layout can need an extra exit or a review of the fire strategy, so check it at the test-fit, not after.
  • Ventilation. Approved Document F Volume 2 sets fresh-air rates per person for offices. Adding people to a floor, or turning open space into meeting rooms, can push the existing air conditioning past what it was designed for.
  • Toilets. The same Approved Code of Practice (L24) sets minimum numbers of toilets and washbasins by headcount; in a multi-let building the landlord’s core is usually designed to cover them, but ask what occupancy it was designed for.

Approved Documents are statutory guidance to the Building Regulations 2010 for England. Compliance is judged against the requirements in Schedule 1; the Approved Document shows one way of meeting them. Your building control body has the final word.

What drives the cost of the space you choose

Every square metre you lease costs you three times: in rent and service charge every quarter, in fit-out once, and in dilapidations when you leave. The size decision is the cost decision.

A smaller floor with a better fit-out often costs less over the lease than a bigger floor fitted cheaply, because rent is paid every year and the fit-out once. Whether the floor comes as Cat A or partly fitted changes the fit-out line a lot — see Cat A vs Cat B office fit out — and the office fit out cost London guide sets out what moves the fit-out price.

The counter-case: do not over-trim

Cutting the floor to the bone saves rent and then costs you in other ways: no room to hire, meetings in the kitchen, and a move two years early. Leave space for the growth you actually expect, and prefer a floor where a meeting room can become desks, or the other way round, without moving the services.
