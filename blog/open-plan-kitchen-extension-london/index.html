Open Plan Kitchen Extension in London — Steel, Glass, Heat and Sound

An open plan kitchen extension in London works when the steel, glazing, heating and acoustics are designed together as one room, before anyone prices it.

By Vladimir Castravet 25 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Moncrieff · SE15 · loft and extension Completed · 2VP project, shown illustratively

An open plan kitchen extension in London is four jobs sold as one room. There is the steel that carries the back of the house once the walls come out. There is the glass, which building control limits. There is the heating, which has to keep up with all that glass in January. And there is the sound: hard floors, a glass roof and an extractor fan turn a family room into an echo chamber unless someone designs against it. Most disappointing kitchen extensions get one of the four wrong because each was treated as somebody else’s problem. This guide takes them in the order they need deciding. 2VP designs and builds kitchen extensions, so it has an interest in the subject. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Steel first. Removing the back wall, and often the wall between the old kitchen and dining room, needs a structural engineer’s calculations, steel beams on padstones, and building control approval.
  • Glass is limited. Approved Document L limits glazing in an extension unless a calculation shows the house still performs. Decide the glass before the steel is sized: big sliding doors need stiff beams.
  • Heat the room you are building. A room-by-room heat loss calculation tells you whether underfloor heating alone will cope in front of a wall of glass.
  • Design out the echo. Soft surfaces, an acoustic ceiling or panels, and quiet appliances. It costs far less to plan than to retrofit.
  • Check the escape route. In a house with a loft conversion, knocking the kitchen through to the hallway can break the protected stair that Approved Document B expects.
  • Paperwork runs alongside. Planning or permitted development, Party Wall etc. Act 1996 notices, and a build-over agreement if a public sewer runs under the new floor.

What an open plan kitchen extension actually means

It means extending the back of the house and removing the internal walls so that kitchen, dining and living share one space, usually with a large glazed opening to the garden. On a London terrace that takes one of three shapes.

Rear extension. The back wall comes out and the room grows into the garden. On most terraces this also means removing the wall between the old back reception and the kitchen.
Side return infill. The narrow alley beside the back addition is enclosed, so a galley kitchen becomes a full-width room. Our side return extension guide covers that shape in detail.
Wrap-around. Side return and rear together, in an L. The largest room, and the most steel.

Whatever the shape, you are making one room do the work of three. That is why the decisions interlock. The beam depth decides the ceiling line. The ceiling line decides where the rooflight goes. The rooflight decides how much heat you lose and how loud the rain is. Change one late and the others move with it.

Do I need a steel beam for an open plan kitchen extension?

Almost always, yes. The rear wall of a London terrace carries the floors and roof above it, and so do many internal walls. When they come out, the load has to go somewhere: into steel beams, which sit on padstones, which sit on masonry or new foundations the engineer has checked. Building control has to approve the design under Approved Document A before the work is covered up.

When you need it in London — the four systems in order

Decide them in this order: structure, glazing, heating, acoustics. Each one constrains the next.

1. Steel: what holds the house up

The structural engineer sizes each beam from the loads above it and the span. Three things are worth asking about before the design is final:

  • Downstand or flush. A beam that hangs below the ceiling is simpler. A beam set flush within the ceiling depth looks cleaner but may need a deeper floor zone or a heavier section. Decide which you want before the engineer starts, not after.
  • Deflection under the glass. Every beam bends a little under load. Large sliding or folding doors tolerate very little movement in the beam above them. Give the engineer the door manufacturer’s deflection limit so the steel is designed to it.
  • Goalposts and columns. Wide openings, or a corner opened up on a wrap-around, often need a steel frame with posts down to their own foundations. That affects your floor build-up and the dig.

Steel inside a house normally needs fire protection, and the house needs propping while the walls come out. Our guide to steel beam fire protection covers both. Where a beam bears on, or a foundation is dug close to, a wall you share with a neighbour, the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 applies and notices have to be served before work starts. That is a time cost as much as a fee: the Act gives the neighbour notice periods and the right to appoint a surveyor.

2. Glazing: how much glass building control allows

Approved Document L Volume 1 (2021) limits the glazed area in an extension, broadly in proportion to the new floor area plus any windows and doors you are removing. Go over it and the designer has to show, by calculation, that the house as a whole still performs no worse — usually by improving the walls, roof or floor to compensate. New windows and doors also have to meet a limiting U-value, which for most homes means good double glazing or triple glazing with thermally broken frames.

Three glazing choices drive most of the cost and the comfort:

  • Doors. Sliding, folding or fixed panes with a single door. Sliding doors give the clearest view; folding doors open the whole width but leave a stack of frames. Steel-look doors need care: see thermally broken slim steel doors before you choose.
  • Rooflights. A rooflight over the kitchen end brings light deep into a long room, but it is the weakest part of the envelope for heat loss and for summer heat gain. An opening rooflight lets hot air out.
  • Orientation. A south or west facing glass box can overheat on a summer afternoon. Solar control glass, an overhang or external blinds are cheaper than an air-conditioning unit added later.

How much glass can I put in a kitchen extension?

As much as the Approved Document L calculation supports. Within the standard limit, the glazing simply has to meet its U-value. Above it, your designer needs a whole-house calculation showing the extra heat loss is offset elsewhere. Ask for that calculation before you fall in love with a glass roof, because it may mean extra insulation you had not priced.

3. Heating: keeping one big room warm

A large, glazed, open plan room loses heat faster than the rooms it replaced. The right first step is a room-by-room heat loss calculation, done by the heating designer from the actual U-values and glazed areas. It tells you three things: how many watts the room needs on a cold day, whether underfloor heating can deliver that through your chosen floor finish, and whether you need a second emitter.

  • Underfloor heating suits a big open floor and frees up wall space for units. A wet system in screed is slow to respond and cheap to run; an electric mat under tiles is quick and simple to fit but dearer to run. Our comparison of electric vs water underfloor heating sets the trade-off out.
  • Trench heaters in front of large glass stop the cold downdraught you feel on a winter evening, which underfloor heating alone sometimes cannot.
  • Boiler or heat pump capacity. Adding a large room can push the existing system to its limit. Check before the kitchen is designed around it.

Ventilation is part of the same job. Approved Document F Volume 1 (2021), Table 1.1, sets a kitchen’s intermittent extract at 30 litres per second with a cooker hood extracting to outside, and 60 litres per second otherwise. An island hob needs a route for that duct, through the ceiling or under the floor, decided before the steel and joists are fixed.

4. Acoustics: why open plan kitchens echo

Sound bounces off hard surfaces. An open plan kitchen extension is almost nothing but hard surfaces: glass, tiles or polished concrete, plastered walls, stone worktops, a flat ceiling. The result is a room where the dishwasher, the extractor and a conversation all compete, and where the television has to go up. No Building Regulation sets how your own living space should sound, so nobody designs it unless you ask.

  • Add absorption. Rugs, upholstered seating, curtains on the glass, and books on open shelves all help. So does an acoustic ceiling: slatted timber over acoustic felt, acoustic plaster, or a few absorbent panels over the dining table.
  • Choose quiet machines. Dishwashers show their noise in decibels on the energy label. Extractors vary widely; a remote or inline motor is quieter in the room than one in the hood.
  • Separate when you need to. A large glazed internal screen or sliding door between the living end and the kitchen lets you close the room off without losing the light.
  • Rain on the roof. Lightweight flat roofs and big rooflights can drum in heavy rain. Ask what the roof build-up is and whether it includes a heavier layer.

Does knocking through to the kitchen affect fire safety?

It can. If your house has a floor more than 4.5m above the ground — which most terraces with a loft conversion do — Approved Document B expects the stair to be a protected route to the front door. Opening the kitchen straight onto the hallway removes that protection. It can still be done, but the design has to replace what is lost, for example with a fire-resisting partition and door, or with sprinklers and other measures agreed with building control. Raise it at design stage, not on site.

Do I need planning permission for a kitchen extension in London?

Not always. A single-storey rear extension to a house often falls within permitted development, subject to depth, height and materials limits. A flat or maisonette has no householder permitted development rights, and conservation areas, listed buildings and Article 4 directions remove or restrict them. If in doubt, a Lawful Development Certificate gives you written proof.

What drives the cost of an open plan kitchen extension

The size of the room is only the starting point. Two kitchen extensions of the same floor area can be priced very differently, and the difference is usually in these lines:

  1. How much wall comes out. One rear wall, or the rear wall plus the internal wall plus the side wall. Each opening adds steel, padstones, propping and making good.
  2. The foundations. Clay soil, nearby trees and drains can push foundations deeper than first assumed, and the depth is often only confirmed once the dig is open.
  3. The glass. Door type, the width of each pane, rooflight size and number, and whether the glazing exceeds the Approved Document L limit and needs compensating insulation.
  4. The heating. Wet or electric underfloor heating, trench heaters, and whether the boiler or heat pump needs upgrading.
  5. Moving the services. An island sink or hob means new drainage falls, sometimes a pumped waste, and a ducted extract route.
  6. The kitchen itself. Units, worktops and appliances can be a large share of the total. Price them separately so you can see the build cost on its own.
  7. Party wall and approvals. Surveyors’ fees, building control and any build-over agreement.

Ask for each of these as a separate line in writing, with any provisional sums named and explained. For 2VP’s published ranges, see the house extension cost London guide, or put your own room into the 2VP estimator, which itemises the build NET of VAT.

Frequently asked questions.

Do I need a steel beam for an open plan kitchen extension?

Almost always. The rear wall of a London terrace, and many internal walls, carry the floors and roof above. When they are removed the load goes into steel beams on padstones, designed by a structural engineer and approved by building control under Approved Document A. Large sliding doors need the beam designed to the door maker’s deflection limit.

How much glass can I put in a kitchen extension?

Above that limit your designer must show by calculation that the whole house still performs no worse, usually by adding insulation elsewhere. Every new window, door and rooflight also has to meet its limiting U-value.

Is underfloor heating enough for a glazed kitchen extension?

Sometimes. A room-by-room heat loss calculation, using the real U-values and glazed areas, tells you whether underfloor heating can deliver enough heat through your chosen floor finish. In front of a large wall of glass, a trench heater is often added to stop cold downdraughts on winter evenings.

How do I stop an open plan kitchen echoing?

Add soft and absorbent surfaces: rugs, upholstered seating, curtains on the glass, an acoustic ceiling or a few absorbent panels over the table. Choose quiet appliances, since dishwashers show their noise on the energy label, and consider a remote or inline extractor motor. It is far cheaper to plan this at design stage than to retrofit it.
