Snagging List London — The Final Walkthrough Checklist

A snagging list in London is the room-by-room defects record you agree at the final walkthrough, with retention held until every item closes.

By Vladimir Castravet 17 October 2026 11 min read Updated October 2026
Alexandra · N22 · whole-house renovation Handed over · completed 2VP project

A snagging list in London is the written, room-by-room record of every defect you find at the final walkthrough, agreed with your builder and closed out before the last of your retention is released. It is not a complaint and it is not a wish list. It is the document that turns “nearly finished” into “finished”. This guide gives you a checklist you can print, the paperwork to collect before you pay, how to write a snag your builder cannot misread, and how retention — not withholding the whole final payment — is the tool that gets snags fixed. 2VP hands homes over and receives snagging lists, so it has an interest in how this goes. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Walk the house in daylight with a torch. Room by room, ceiling to floor, testing everything that opens, switches, flows or heats.
  • Write each snag properly. Location, photo, description, and the standard you expect. “Paint’s bad” is not a snag.
  • Collect the paperwork before you pay. Building control completion certificate, Electrical Installation Certificate, gas and boiler certificates, commissioning records, warranties, manuals, as-built drawings.
  • Agree the list in writing. With a date to fix it and a date to re-inspect it.
  • Retention is your leverage. On a 2VP contract, 5% is retained: 2.5% released at practical completion, 2.5% at the end of the 12-month defects period.
  • Be proportionate. Holding back the entire final payment over a scuffed skirting usually slows the fix, not speeds it.

What a snagging list actually means

Three terms get used loosely, and the difference between them is money.

Practical completion

Practical completion is the point at which the works are complete for practical purposes: you can live in the house and use it as intended, with only minor items left. It is defined by your contract, not by the calendar. It usually ends the builder’s responsibility for insuring the works, starts the defects liability period, and releases the first half of retention. Read your contract’s definition before the walkthrough — the JCT homeowner contract guide covers how the standard forms deal with it.

Snags, and defects

A snag is something you can see at handover: a door that binds, a missing cover plate, grout that stops short. A defect is something that shows itself later: a hairline crack as the plaster dries, a drip under a trap after a month of use, a radiator that will not heat in January. The snagging list deals with the first. The defects liability period deals with the second.

The defects liability period and retention

The defects liability period is the window after practical completion in which the builder returns to fix defects at its own cost. Retention is the money you hold back to make sure that happens. 2VP’s structure, from its estimator, is fixed and published. When you need a snagging list in London

Every project that ends with a handover needs one — a bathroom as much as a whole house. The timing is what most homeowners get wrong.

  1. The builder’s own pre-handover snag. The builder should walk the job first and fix what it finds. Your walkthrough should not be their first look.
  2. Your final walkthrough, before practical completion is agreed. This is the list that matters most.
  3. A re-inspection once the builder says the list is closed. Tick items off in writing, together.
  4. The end-of-defects inspection, shortly before the 12 months run out. Anything that has shown itself since handover goes on this list, and the last 2.5% follows it.

How to write a snag your builder cannot misread

Each line needs four things:

  • Location. Room, wall, height. “Bedroom 2, window wall, left reveal, 1.2m up.”
  • Photo. With something for scale, and a coloured sticker on the spot so the trade can find it.
  • Description. What is wrong, in plain words. “Hairline crack along the plasterboard joint, 400mm long.”
  • Standard expected. What “fixed” looks like: the specification, the manufacturer’s instructions, or the sample you approved. If you cannot name a standard, ask whether it is a snag or a preference.

Then agree the whole list in writing within a set number of days of the walkthrough, with a target date for each item and a re-inspection date. A list sent in pieces over six weeks is how snags turn into arguments.

The final walkthrough checklist, room by room — and the paperwork

Print this. Take a torch, a plug-in socket tester, your phone, a roll of coloured stickers, the floor plan and the specification. Walk in daylight. Start at the top floor and work down; in each room, go ceiling, walls, floor, then everything that moves.

Walls and ceilings

  • Hold a torch flat against the surface. Raking light shows ridges, joints and patches that face-on light hides.
  • Paint: even coverage, no misses behind radiators and doors, clean lines at ceilings and skirtings.
  • Cracks at plasterboard joints and around new openings. Note them now; some will be drying cracks for the defects period.

Doors and ironmongery

  • Every door opens, closes and latches without force, and stays where you leave it.
  • Even gaps round the leaf. Handles, hinges and stops tight; fire doors close fully onto their seals where specified.

Windows

  • Every window opens, closes and locks. Keys handed over.
  • Trickle vents present where specified, and they open and close.
  • Seals continuous, glass unscratched, sills clean, no light round the frame.

Floors

  • Walk every floor: listen for squeaks, feel for movement, look for gaps at skirtings and thresholds.
  • Lay a long straight edge or spirit level where it looks off. Note dips, and where boards meet tiles, the transition strip.

Tiling

  • Lippage — tile edges standing proud of their neighbours. Run a fingertip across the joints and use the torch.
  • Grout: full, even, consistent colour. Silicone: continuous, smooth, no gaps at corners or where the bath meets the wall.
  • Cut tiles neat at outlets and edges. If you went for a jointless finish instead, the microcement vs resin guide sets out what to check.

Kitchen

  • Doors and drawers aligned, gaps even, soft-close working, no chips on edges or worktop joints.
  • Every appliance commissioned and run: oven heats, hob ignites, dishwasher and washing machine complete a cycle without leaking.
  • The cooker hood runs and extracts. Approved Document F Volume 1 (2021), Table 1.1, sets a kitchen’s intermittent extract at 30 litres per second with a cooker hood extracting to outside, and 60 litres per second otherwise.

Bathroom

  • Run every tap and the shower together. Water should leave the tray or wet-room floor without ponding — the fall to the waste has to be visible in practice, not just on the drawing.
  • Fill and drain the basin and bath. Listen for gurgling and check for smells: a trap that loses its water seal lets drain air in.
  • Check under everything you can reach for drips after ten minutes of running.
  • The extract fan runs. Table 1.1 of Approved Document F Volume 1 sets a bathroom’s intermittent extract at 15 litres per second.

Electrics

  • Every socket with the plug-in tester; every switch and every light, including outside and under-cabinet lighting.
  • Press the test button on each RCD at the consumer unit. It should trip; reset it.
  • Test every smoke and heat alarm, and check they are interlinked where the specification says so.

Heating and hot water

  • Turn the heating up. Every radiator should get hot, top to bottom, at roughly the same rate. A cold top means air; a cold radiator far from the boiler means the system needs balancing.
  • Hot water at every tap within a sensible time. Underfloor heating zones each respond to their own thermostat.

Outside

  • Gutters and downpipes run clear and discharge where they should. A hose test on a dry day will tell you.
  • The damp-proof course is not bridged. Approved Document C, paragraph 5.5(b), sets it at least 150mm above adjoining ground: check new paths, patios and planting beds. The damp-proof course and weep holes guide shows what to look for.
  • Pointing, flashings, the roof edges you can see, and any site damage to the garden or the neighbours’ side.

Approved Documents are statutory guidance to the Building Regulations 2010 for England. Compliance is judged against the Requirements in Schedule 1; the Approved Document shows one way of meeting them. Your building control body has the final word on your building.

What a snagging list costs — and what to hold back

Fixing snags costs you nothing extra. Making good the builder’s own work is inside the contract price, and defects in the following year are what the defects liability period is for.

You can pay a professional snagging inspector to walk the house for you. 2VP does not hold a figure for that service, so it does not publish one: get a fixed fee in writing, ask for a sample report, and make sure the inspector references a standard for each item rather than a list of opinions. It is most useful on a large job, or when you cannot be there.

The counter-case: do not withhold the whole final payment

The instinct at handover is to hold everything back until every snag is fixed. Resist it, for three reasons.

Retention is already the tool. It exists precisely to secure snags and defects. On the £104,625 example, £5,231.25 is held for that purpose. Withholding a stage payment as well, over items worth a few hundred pounds, is out of proportion.
It can put you in breach. Your contract sets out how and when payment can be withheld — usually with written notice of the amount and the reason. Withhold without following it, and the dispute becomes about you.
It slows the fix. A builder who has not been paid for finished work stops prioritising your job. A short, agreed, dated list with retention behind it moves faster.

Hold back what the contract allows for what is genuinely outstanding. Pay for what is done. If the snags are serious — water getting in, an unsafe installation, a missing certificate — that is a different conversation, and practical completion should not be agreed until they are resolved.

Your rights if snags are not fixed

The Consumer Rights Act 2015 applies to building work done for consumers. Section 49 says a service must be performed with reasonable care and skill. Where it is not, section 55 gives you the right to require repeat performance — the builder must put it right within a reasonable time, without significant inconvenience to you, and at its own cost. Where repeat performance is impossible or not done within a reasonable time, section 56 gives you the right to a price reduction. This is a summary, not legal advice; for a real dispute, take advice on your own contract.

How 2VP handles snagging on every project

Snagging is easier when quality was checked all the way through, rather than inspected in one go at the end. That is how the five protections are meant to work together.

  1. Stage Payments, QA-Gated. Each weekly stage payment follows a quality check of that stage, so problems are caught before they are covered up.
  2. Daily On-Site Report. Photos of what was done every working day — including the fire-stopping and the services before boarding — which become part of your handover record.
  3. One Named PM, Start to Finish. The PM who ran the build walks the house with you and owns the list. Nobody new arrives at handover to argue about it.
  4. Open-Book Itemised Quote. The specification you approved is the standard each snag is judged against. It is written down, so “standard expected” has an answer.
  5. 12-Month Workmanship Warranty. Defects that appear after handover are fixed through the 12-month defects period, and the final 2.5% of retention is released at the end of it.

The method is the same whether it is a bathroom or a whole house — across 2VP’s flat and full-house renovations, house extensions in London and loft conversions.

Frequently asked questions.

What is a snagging list?

A snagging list is the written, room-by-room record of defects found at the final walkthrough of a building project, agreed between you and the builder and fixed before the end of the job. Each item should give the location, a photo, a description of what is wrong and the standard expected. It deals with what you can see at handover; defects that appear later are handled during the defects liability period.

When should I do the final walkthrough with my builder?

After the builder has done its own pre-handover snag, and before you agree practical completion. Walk it in daylight with the project manager, top floor down, with a torch, a plug-in socket tester and the specification. Then agree the list in writing within a set number of days, with a date to fix each item and a date to re-inspect.

Can I withhold payment until snags are fixed?

You can hold back what your contract allows, in the way your contract sets out — usually written notice of the amount and the reason. Retention is the tool designed for this. On a 2VP contract, 5% is retained: 2.5% released at practical completion and 2.5% at the end of the 12-month defects liability period. Withholding the whole final payment over cosmetic snags is usually disproportionate and slows the fix.

What paperwork should I get at handover?

The building control completion or final certificate; the Electrical Installation Certificate to BS 7671 and the building regulations compliance certificate for notifiable electrical work; the Gas Safe certificate and boiler compliance certificate; commissioning records; warranties and operation and maintenance manuals; the fire-stopping photo record; as-built drawings; and, where the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 applied, the surveyor’s post-works close-out.

What are my rights if my builder will not fix snags?

Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, section 49 requires a service to be performed with reasonable care and skill. Where it is not, section 55 lets you require repeat performance — the builder puts it right within a reasonable time, without significant inconvenience to you and at its own cost. If that is impossible or not done in a reasonable time, section 56 gives a right to a price reduction. Take advice on your own contract before escalating.

Should I hire a professional snagging inspector?

It helps on a large project, when you cannot attend the walkthrough, or when you do not trust your own eye. 2VP does not publish a fee for it because it holds no figure. Ask for a fixed fee in writing and a sample report, and check that each item in the report is tied to a standard — the specification, a manufacturer’s instruction or an Approved Document — rather than an opinion.
