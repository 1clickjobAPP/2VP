Office Move Checklist London — From Lease to First Day

An office move checklist London businesses can rely on starts at the old lease: break conditions, licence to alter, fit-out, IT, dilapidations, then move week.

By Vladimir Castravet 8 November 2026 10 min read Updated November 2026
Chester Square · London · 2VP project exterior Office photo to follow · real 2VP image

An office move checklist for London is really three checklists running side by side: the property and legal steps, the fit-out of the new space, and the people, IT and logistics that get your team working on day one. Most moves that go wrong do so because one of the three started late — usually the landlord’s consent, the internet line or the exit from the old office. This guide puts all three on one page, in the order they need to happen, from the week you agree heads of terms to the first month in the new space. 2VP fits out London offices, so it has an interest in the middle part of this list. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Start with your current lease, not the new one. Your break date or lease end, and the conditions attached to it, set the deadline for everything else.
  • Survey before you sign. Know what the landlord is handing over — shell, Cat A or Cat A+ — and what it will take to make it work for your team.
  • Get the licence to alter moving early. No landlord consent, no start on site.
  • Order the long-lead items first. Internet connection, glazed partitions, furniture and AV are the usual programme killers.
  • Plan the exit as carefully as the entry. Reinstatement and dilapidations at the old office can cost you more time and money than the move itself.
  • Day one is a fire safety and IT test. A fire risk assessment, working access control and a tested network before your staff walk in.

What an office move checklist actually covers

A good checklist assigns every task to one owner and one date. On a typical London move there are four owners, and the list falls apart when two of them each assume the other is doing something.

You (the tenant)Lease decisions, budget, staff communication, the fire risk assessment for the new space, signing off the designDecisions arrive late, so the fit-out waits

Is an office move the same as an office fit-out?

No. The fit-out is one stream inside the move. The move also includes the lease, the exit from your old premises, IT, removals and the people side. A fit-out contractor can run the build and coordinate the rest, but your business still has to make the lease and staffing decisions. The office fit-out project manager guide explains where that role starts and stops.

What does “Cat A” or “Cat B” mean for my move?

Cat A is the landlord’s base finish — typically raised floors, suspended ceilings, lighting and air conditioning in an open floor. Cat B is everything that makes it yours: rooms, kitchen, furniture, data, branding. The more Cat B work your new space needs, the earlier the fit-out has to start. The Cat A vs Cat B office fit-out guide sets out what usually sits on each side of the line.

When to start your office move in London

Start by working backwards from the date you must leave your current office, not forwards from the date you find a new one. That date is fixed by your existing lease, and missing it can lock you into rent you no longer want to pay.

  1. Read your current lease first. Find the break date or expiry, the notice period, and every condition attached to the break. Courts tend to read break conditions strictly — rent paid to date, and vacant possession, can be enough to make or lose a break. Ask your solicitor to confirm in writing what you must do, and by when.
  2. Check your security of tenure. Under Part II of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1954, many business tenants have a right to renew unless the lease was “contracted out”. That changes your negotiating position on both the old and the new lease.
  3. Agree heads of terms on the new space with the fit-out in mind. Rent-free periods, the condition the space is handed over in, and what you must take out when you leave all belong in the heads of terms, not the small print later.
  4. Build the programme backwards. Move date, minus the fit-out, minus the licence to alter and building control, minus design. The office fit-out timeline in London breaks the build stages down so you can test your dates.

How far in advance should you plan an office move?

As early as your current lease allows you to act — and certainly before you serve any break notice. The fixed points are your notice period, the landlord’s response time on the licence to alter, and your internet provider’s lead time. Get each of those in writing early; the fit-out programme is built around them, not the other way round.

The office move checklist, stage by stage

Print this and give each line an owner and a date. The order matters: each stage depends on the one before it.

1. Before you sign the new lease

  1. Headcount and ways of working. How many desks, how many people in on a peak day, how many meeting rooms and phone booths. This sets the size of space you need.
  2. A measured survey and a test-fit layout. Check that your team, meeting rooms and kitchen actually fit before you commit.
  3. A condition check of the base build. Air conditioning capacity, power, floor boxes, ceiling grid, toilets, accessibility, risers for data. Photograph everything.
  4. The landlord’s building rules. Working hours, noisy-work restrictions, goods lift booking, loading bay access and the approval process for drawings.
  5. Reinstatement terms. Agree what you will have to remove at the end of the new lease before you build it.

2. Lease signed — before the works start

  1. Apply for the licence to alter. Your landlord will want drawings, method statements and insurance details. The licence to alter for an office fit-out guide explains what goes in the pack.
  2. Building control. New partitions, fire alarm changes, kitchens and toilets usually need building regulations approval. Your contractor should tell you which route and who applies.
  3. CDM 2015 appointments. As a business client you carry client duties under the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015. Where more than one contractor is involved, a principal designer and principal contractor must be appointed in writing.
  4. Order the internet line. Ask the provider for the installation date in writing on day one, and plan a backup connection in case it slips.
  5. Order long-lead items. Furniture, glazed partitions, AV and access control. Confirm delivery dates against the programme.
  6. Serve notices on the old lease. Through your solicitor, on the date and in the form the lease requires.

3. During the fit-out

  1. A weekly progress meeting with one decision-maker from your side. Late decisions are the commonest cause of delay on a fit-out.
  2. IT first-fix walk. Your IT lead checks data points, comms room power and cooling before ceilings close.
  3. Staff communication. New address, travel, cycle storage, showers and the first-day plan. Consult early if anyone’s role or location changes.
  4. Furniture and storage audit. Decide what moves, what is sold or donated, and what is recycled. Label everything that moves.
  5. Insurance. Arrange cover for the new premises and contents from the date you take the keys, and confirm the contractor’s cover for the works.

4. Move week

  1. Practical completion and snagging of the fit-out, with the handover pack: building control completion, electrical and fire alarm certificates, emergency lighting certificate, as-built drawings and manuals.
  2. Fire risk assessment for the new space. Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 the responsible person — usually you, as the employer in control of the space — needs one in place before staff start work there.
  3. Network live and tested. Wi-Fi coverage, printers, phones, meeting-room screens, access fobs.
  4. Removals. Goods lift and loading bay booked at both buildings; a parking bay suspension from the borough if the lorry needs the street.
  5. Day-one pack for staff. Floor plan, fire exits and assembly point, first aiders, fire marshals, Wi-Fi details.

5. Leaving the old office

  1. Get the schedule of dilapidations early. Then decide with your surveyor whether to carry out the works or negotiate a settlement. The dilapidations on an office lease guide covers both routes.
  2. Reinstatement. Remove what your old licence to alter says you must, and nothing you will be charged to put back.
  3. Data and waste. Wipe or destroy drives that hold personal data, use a licensed carrier for waste, and dispose of electrical equipment under the WEEE rules. Keep the paperwork.
  4. Hand back the keys properly. Meter readings, photos of every room, keys and fobs returned against a signed receipt.

6. The first 30 days in the new office

  1. Admin changes. Registered office address at Companies House (if it is moving), HMRC, your bank, insurers, suppliers, website and Google Business Profile.
  2. Business rates. Tell the borough the date you moved in, and tell the old borough the date you moved out.
  3. Workstation checks. Display screen equipment assessments for staff, as the Health and Safety (Display Screen Equipment) Regulations 1992 require.
  4. A 30-day snag walk with your contractor: doors, air conditioning balance, lighting controls, anything that only shows up once people are in.

Frequently asked questions.

How far in advance should you plan an office move?

Start as soon as your current lease lets you act, and before you serve any break notice. The fixed points are your notice period and break conditions, the landlord’s response time on the licence to alter, and your internet provider’s lead time. Get each in writing early and build the fit-out programme around them.

What should be on an office move checklist?

Five stages: before you sign (headcount, survey, base-build check, building rules, reinstatement terms); lease signed (licence to alter, building control, CDM appointments, internet line, long-lead orders, notices on the old lease); during the fit-out (weekly meetings, IT checks, staff communication, furniture audit, insurance); move week (handover pack, fire risk assessment, network test, removals); and leaving the old office (dilapidations, reinstatement, data and waste disposal, key return).

Who is responsible for the fire risk assessment in a new office?

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the responsible person — usually the employer in control of the space — must have a suitable fire risk assessment. In a multi-let building the landlord covers the common parts and you cover your floor, and the two of you must cooperate. Have it in place before staff start work in the new office.

Do I need a licence to alter before an office fit-out?

Almost always, if you lease the space. Most office leases require the landlord’s written consent before you change partitions, services or ceilings. The landlord will usually ask for drawings, method statements and insurance details, and its surveyor will review them. Start the application as soon as the lease is agreed, because works cannot start without it.

What happens to the old office when we move out?

Your lease decides. You usually have to reinstate alterations your licence to alter says must come out, leave the space in the repair the lease requires, and give vacant possession if you are using a break clause. The landlord may serve a schedule of dilapidations. Ask for it early and decide with your surveyor whether to do the works or negotiate a settlement.

Should the fit-out contractor manage the whole office move?

The contractor should run the build and coordinate with your IT, furniture and removals suppliers, because they all need the same programme. Lease decisions, staff communication and the fire risk assessment for your business stay with you. Agree in writing who owns each line of the checklist.
