Accessible Office Design — Part M and the Equality Act

Accessible office design means building to Approved Document M Volume 2 where you alter, and making reasonable adjustments under the Equality Act 2010.

By Vladimir Castravet 18 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Amott Road · Peckham, SE15 · entrance Shown illustratively · a 2VP home, not an office fit-out

Accessible office design rests on two duties that work in different ways. The Building Regulations 2010, with Approved Document M Volume 2 as the guidance, set the standard for building work: what a new door, WC or reception desk should achieve, and the rule that an alteration must not leave access worse than before. The Equality Act 2010 sets a duty on you as an employer, and on any business that serves the public, to make reasonable adjustments for disabled people. One is checked by building control when work is done. The other applies every day you occupy the office. This guide explains both, gives you the design figures a fit-out turns on, and shows why it is cheaper to draw access in than to build it in later. 2VP fits out London offices, so it has an interest in how you plan this. Weigh what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Two sources. Part M of the Building Regulations 2010 for the building work; the Equality Act 2010 for how you treat staff and visitors.
  • Approved Document M Volume 2 covers buildings other than dwellings: approach, entrance, reception, corridors, doors, lifts and stairs, WCs and facilities.
  • A fit-out must not make access worse. New elements — a new WC, new doors, a new reception — should meet the guidance.
  • Reasonable adjustments under section 20 of the Equality Act cover three things: how you do things, physical features, and auxiliary aids.
  • Design it in. Door widths, contrast, an accessible WC, a hearing enhancement system at reception, glare-free lighting and calm acoustics cost least on the drawing.
  • Your lease matters. Most physical changes need landlord consent, and the Equality Act has its own rules on that.

What accessible office design actually means

An accessible office is one that disabled staff and visitors can reach, enter, move through and use on the same terms as everyone else — including people who use wheelchairs, people with sight or hearing loss, people with limited strength or stamina, and neurodivergent people.

What does Part M require in an office?

Requirement M1 of Schedule 1 to the Building Regulations 2010 requires reasonable provision for people to gain access to and use the building and its facilities. Approved Document M Volume 2 is the statutory guidance that shows one way of meeting it for buildings other than dwellings, which includes offices. Volume 1 is for homes. As with every Approved Document, following it is not the only way to comply, but a different approach has to be shown to work just as well.

What does the Equality Act 2010 require?

Section 20 of the Equality Act 2010 sets the duty to make adjustments, in three parts:

  1. Provisions, criteria and practices. Changing how things are done — a fixed desk instead of hot-desking, a quiet room, flexible start times.
  2. Physical features. Removing, altering or avoiding a feature that puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage — a heavy door, a step, a high counter.
  3. Auxiliary aids. Providing equipment or services — a hearing loop, screen-reader software, an adjustable desk.

For employers the duty is owed to disabled employees and job applicants you know about, or could reasonably be expected to know about. For a business that serves the public, the duty looks ahead: you plan for disabled visitors in general, not only the ones you have met. What is “reasonable” depends on how much an adjustment helps, how practical it is, its cost and your resources. It is not a design standard with numbers; Part M is. This is a summary, not legal advice.

Is BS 8300 mandatory?

No. BS 8300-2:2018 is the British Standard code of practice for accessible and inclusive buildings. It is more detailed than Approved Document M and covers more — signage, lighting, acoustics, rest facilities. It becomes binding only if your contract or your landlord’s fit-out guide requires it. For neurodivergent staff, PAS 6463:2022 gives guidance on light, noise, smell and the sense of space that neither Part M nor BS 8300 covers in depth.

The design figures in Approved Document M Volume 2

These are the figures an office fit-out most often turns on. Existing buildings get some allowance where the original fabric makes the new-build figure impossible.

Approved Documents are statutory guidance to the Building Regulations 2010 for England. Compliance is judged against the Requirements in Schedule 1; the Approved Document shows one way of meeting them. Your building control body has the final word on your building. Check every figure against the edition current when your design is submitted.

How wide should doors be in an accessible office?

For new internal doors, plan on an effective clear width of 800mm to 825mm, depending on whether the door is approached straight on or from a corridor at right angles. Effective clear width is measured from the face of the door stop to the open door leaf or its furniture — not the size of the door you order. The nominal size of a door leaf is always larger than the clear width it gives once the frame, stops and handle are in, so specify the clear width, not the leaf. Fire doors on self-closers are the usual problem: they must close against smoke and still open within the force limits.

How many accessible toilets does an office need?

At least one wheelchair-accessible unisex WC wherever toilets are provided, within a reasonable travel distance of every workstation and on a level reachable without stairs. In a multi-let London building, that WC may be the landlord’s, on the core. If your fit-out adds toilets, the new ones should follow the guidance, and their position depends on the drainage risers more than on the plan you would like.

What about neurodivergent staff and visitors?

Part M says little about sensory design. Glare, flicker, constant background noise, strong smells near tea points and open floors with no retreat all make an office harder to work in. A quiet room, controllable lighting, good acoustics and clear wayfinding cost little when they are designed in. The office acoustics design guide covers the sound side, which matters for hearing-aid users as well.

When accessible office design applies in a London fit-out

The Equality Act applies all the time. Part M applies when your fit-out is building work — new WCs, new or moved doors on escape and circulation routes, structural openings, a new entrance or reception — and building control is involved.

Does an office fit-out have to comply with Part M?

When the fit-out is building work, it must not leave the building less compliant with Part M than it was, and new elements should meet the guidance. A decorative refresh is not building work, so Part M does not bite; the Equality Act still does. In practice the layout decision is where access is won or lost. Open-plan floors are easier to make step-free and wide; cellular floors multiply doors and corridors. The open plan vs cellular office guide sets out that trade-off.

What if the building cannot meet Part M in full?

Older London buildings often have a step at the entrance, narrow doors or a lift too small for a wheelchair and a companion. Approved Document M allows for existing buildings, and building control can agree a different solution where the full figure is impossible. An access audit by a qualified access consultant records what the building has, what you are changing and what you cannot, and helps you show why. In a listed building, access changes also need listed building consent.

Do I need my landlord’s consent for access changes?

Usually, yes: most physical changes fall under the alterations clause. The Equality Act 2010 has specific rules for leased premises. Where a lease would stop a tenant making a reasonable adjustment, the Act reads the lease as allowing it with the landlord’s consent, which must not be unreasonably withheld. Ask early, in writing. The licence to alter for an office fit out guide explains that approval route.

How does accessibility affect fire escape?

Everyone has to be able to get out. Upper floors in multi-storey buildings usually have refuges in protected stairs, and disabled staff who need help should have a personal emergency evacuation plan agreed with them. Visual alarm beacons help deaf staff in WCs and quiet rooms. The fire strategy is the landlord’s for the building and yours for the floor, so plan them together.

What drives the cost of accessible office design

Most access features are lines on a drawing if they are planned at the start, and demolition if they are added later. 2VP does not publish prices in its guides; these are the drivers to price.

The building you start with. A step-free entrance and a lift of the right size are the landlord’s. If they are missing, your options narrow before your fit-out starts.
New WCs and their drainage. The distance from the risers sets how far a new accessible WC can move, and whether the floor needs raising.
Doors. Replacing narrow existing doors and frames, and specifying closers that meet the force limits, is a line per door.
Reception and kitchen. A lowered counter section, a knee space at the tea point, a hearing enhancement system.
Finishes and contrast. Almost free if contrast is in the finishes schedule; a redecoration if it is not.
Advice. An access audit and, if needed, an access consultant to agree solutions with building control. Ask for the fee and scope in writing.

Adjustments for a named employee can be supported by Access to Work, the government scheme that helps with equipment and workplace adaptations. Ask the scheme before you buy. Frequently asked questions.

What is Approved Document M Volume 2?

It is the statutory guidance on access to and use of buildings other than dwellings, including offices, under Part M of the Building Regulations 2010 in England. It covers approach, entrances, reception, corridors, doors, lifts, stairs, WCs and facilities. Following it is one way to meet requirement M1; your building control body has the final word.

Does an office fit-out have to comply with Part M?

When the fit-out is building work — new WCs, new or moved doors, structural openings, a new entrance — it must not leave the building less compliant with Part M than before, and new elements should follow Approved Document M Volume 2. A decorative refresh is not building work, but the Equality Act 2010 still applies.

What are reasonable adjustments under the Equality Act 2010?

Section 20 of the Equality Act 2010 sets a duty to make reasonable adjustments in three areas: changing how things are done, altering or avoiding physical features, and providing auxiliary aids. What is reasonable depends on how much it helps, how practical it is, its cost and your resources. This is a summary, not legal advice.

How wide should doors be in an accessible office?

Approved Document M Volume 2 asks for an effective clear width of 800mm to 825mm for new internal doors, depending on the approach, and at least 1000mm for new entrance doors used by the public. Existing buildings have lower figures. Effective clear width is measured from the door stop to the open leaf, not the size of the door you order.

Does every office need an accessible toilet?

Approved Document M Volume 2 asks for at least one wheelchair-accessible unisex WC wherever toilets are provided, within a reasonable travel distance and reachable without stairs. In a multi-let building it may be the landlord’s, on the core. New toilets in your fit-out should follow the guidance.

Who pays for reasonable adjustments in a rented office?

The duty to make reasonable adjustments for your staff sits with you as the employer. The government’s Access to Work scheme can help with equipment and adaptations for a named employee. Physical changes usually need landlord consent under your lease, and the Equality Act 2010 says that consent must not be unreasonably withheld for a reasonable adjustment.
