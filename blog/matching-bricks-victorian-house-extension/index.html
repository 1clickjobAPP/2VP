Matching Bricks on a Victorian House Extension in London

Matching bricks on a Victorian house means matching four things — brick, mortar, bond and pointing — and proving them on a sample panel first.

By Vladimir Castravet 13 November 2026 10 min read Updated November 2026
Moncrieff · SE15 · loft and extension Completed · 2VP project

Matching bricks on a Victorian house is rarely about finding one perfect brick. A new wall disappears next to an old one when four things agree: the brick itself, the mortar between the bricks, the pattern they are laid in, and the way the joints are finished. Get three right and one wrong, and you will see the join from the end of the garden. This guide explains what London Victorian brick actually is, why new bricks often refuse to line up with old ones, whether reclaimed or new suits your extension, what planning expects, what drives the cost, and the sample panel that should be signed off before the first course goes in.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Match four things, not one. Brick, mortar, bond and pointing. The mortar alone can make a perfect brick look wrong.
  • Know your brick. Most London Victorian terraces are built in yellow London stock, with red bricks used for arches, bands and dressings.
  • Check the size. Victorian bricks were made to imperial sizes that vary. Modern bricks are 65mm tall. If yours are taller, the courses drift apart unless the joints are adjusted.
  • Reclaimed or new. Reclaimed stocks match most closely where the new wall sits next to the old one. New handmade stocks are more consistent and easier to buy in quantity.
  • Planning cares. Under permitted development, materials must look similar to the existing house. In a conservation area, expect to submit samples.
  • Prove it first. Build a sample panel on site, look at it wet and dry, from the street and close up, and sign it off in writing.

What matching bricks on a Victorian house actually means

It means matching what the eye reads as “the wall”. That is the brick’s colour, texture and size, the mortar’s colour and texture, the bond pattern, and the joint profile. Here is what each one involves on a typical London house.

What is a London stock brick?

A London stock is the soft, yellow-to-brown clay brick that built much of Victorian London. Historically, ash and other material were mixed into the clay, which helped the firing and gives stocks their mottled yellow colour with darker flecks and patches. No two are quite the same, and that variation is the point: a wall of perfectly uniform modern bricks looks flat and new next to it. Red bricks, including soft “rubbers” cut for arches, appear as window heads, bands and front-elevation detail.

Why do new bricks not line up with old ones?

Because the sizes are different. A modern UK brick is 215mm long, 102.5mm wide and 65mm tall. Victorian bricks were made to imperial sizes that varied by maker and decade, and they are often taller than 65mm. Lay a modern brick next to an old one on the same mortar joint and, within a few courses, the bed joints no longer line up. Your bricklayer can compensate by thickening the joints, but only within limits — thick joints look wrong too. The fix is to measure before you buy: count ten courses on the old wall, measure the height, and match it.

What is the mortar doing?

Mortar is roughly a sixth of what you see on a brick wall, so its colour matters as much as the brick’s. Victorian walls were built in lime mortar, which is softer and usually paler or warmer than modern grey cement mortar. A grey cement joint next to a buff lime joint is the most common reason a “matched” extension still looks new. The sand chosen sets most of the colour, so the mix should be agreed on the sample panel, not on the day.

What is the bond?

The bond is the pattern the bricks are laid in. Victorian front elevations are often in Flemish bond: long faces (stretchers) and short ends (headers) alternate in every course. That pattern comes from solid walls, where headers tie the two halves of the wall together. A modern extension wall is a cavity wall, and its outer leaf is naturally laid in stretcher bond — all long faces. Where the bond must match, the bricklayer can cut bricks in half and lay the halves as snap headers, which recreate the Flemish pattern on a cavity wall. It is slower work, and it costs more.

What about the pointing?

The pointing is the finished face of the joint: flush, slightly recessed, weather-struck, or, on some smarter fronts, tuck pointing — a fine raised line over a coloured joint. Most rear and side walls on terraces are plainer than the front. Match the wall your extension actually sits beside, not the smartest wall on the house.

When you need a brick match in London

You need one wherever new brickwork will be seen next to old, and planning almost always expects it. The common cases:

  1. Rear and side extensions. A side return extension often sits right against the original back addition, so the join is at eye level and in full view from the kitchen.
  2. Wrap-arounds and two-storey extensions. More new wall, more of it beside old wall, and usually more of it visible to neighbours.
  3. Loft conversions with brick gables. A hip-to-gable loft builds the new gable wall up in brick, high and visible from the street.
  4. Repairs and new openings. Filling an old doorway, rebuilding a parapet or patching after a new steel goes in.

Does planning require matching bricks on an extension?

Under permitted development, yes in substance. The conditions to Class A of Part 1, Schedule 2 to the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England) Order 2015 require the materials used on the exterior of the enlargement to be of a similar appearance to those on the existing house. That is “similar appearance”, not “identical”, but a council can and does take enforcement action where an extension clearly does not match. The detail of what permitted development allows is in the permitted development rules for extensions guide.

Where you need full planning permission, materials are judged as part of the design, and the permission may set a condition that samples are approved before work above ground starts.

What changes in a conservation area?

More scrutiny. Councils have a duty under the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Act 1990 to pay special attention to preserving or enhancing a conservation area’s character, and brickwork is a large part of that character. In practice, conservation-area permissions often require brick, mortar and pointing samples to be submitted and approved, and an officer may want to see a panel on site. The same thinking applies to lofts: see the conservation area loft conversion guide. On a listed building, the brickwork is part of what is protected and needs Listed Building Consent; do not assume an extension rule applies.

Can you choose a deliberate contrast instead?

Sometimes. Some councils accept, or even prefer, an extension that is clearly modern — a different brick, render or cladding — rather than a near-miss imitation. That is a design decision for your planning application, not a way round the permitted-development condition. If you are relying on permitted development, “similar appearance” is the test.

How to get a match: reclaimed or new, and the sample panel

Start with the old wall, not the brick catalogue. Then decide between reclaimed and new, and prove the choice on a panel.

How do I find bricks to match my Victorian house?

  1. Pick the right wall. Identify the wall the new brickwork will sit beside. Rear and side walls have often weathered differently from the front.
  2. Measure it. Brick length and height, joint thickness, and the height of ten courses. Note the bond and joint profile.
  3. Photograph it in daylight, both dry and after rain, with a tape measure in shot.
  4. Take a brick if you can. A brick from an opening you are forming, or from the demolition of an outbuilding, is the most accurate sample a supplier can work from.
  5. Get samples from more than one source. Reclamation yards and makers of new handmade stocks will send samples. Put them against the wall, not on the kitchen table.
  6. Build a sample panel with the chosen brick, mortar, bond and pointing, and sign it off before ordering in bulk.

Are reclaimed bricks better than new for a Victorian extension?

They match most closely, but they are not automatically the right choice. Here is the trade-off.

Whichever you choose, order the full quantity, plus extra for cuts and rejects, from one batch or one lot at the start. Running out halfway through a wall and buying a different batch is how a visible line appears across the middle of an extension.

What should a sample panel show?

A sample panel is a small section of wall, built on site before the real thing, using the exact brick, mortar mix, bond and pointing. It should be built near the old wall, ideally where the two can be seen together, and big enough to judge — around a square metre is a practical size. Look at it:

  • From the distance people will actually see it — the garden, the street, the neighbour’s window.
  • Dry and after rain. Stocks and lime mortar change colour noticeably when wet.
  • After the mortar has dried. Fresh mortar is darker than it will end up, so do not judge on day one.

Then photograph it, sign it off in writing with the mortar mix recorded, and keep it standing until the wall is built. If your planning permission requires approval of samples, the panel is often what the officer comes to see.

What happens where the new wall meets the old?

The junction is an engineering decision as well as a visual one. The new wall usually sits on a new, deeper foundation, while the old wall sits on a shallow Victorian one, and the two will move slightly differently. Your structural engineer decides whether the new wall is tied in, toothed into the old brickwork, or separated by a movement joint. A neat, straight movement joint is usually less visible than a crack that appears later where the two were forced together. The foundation depth guide explains why the new foundation may go deeper than drawn.

Can mismatched brickwork be fixed after it is built?

Partly. Specialist brick tinting applies a mineral stain to individual bricks or areas, and can close a colour gap noticeably. Repointing can change the joint colour if the mortar was the problem. What neither can fix is a size mismatch — courses that do not line up — or the wrong bond. Those mean rebuilding. That is why the panel comes first.

The counter-case: is a perfect match worth paying for?

Not always. On a rear wall that only you and one neighbour can see, a well-chosen new stock with the right mortar colour and joint may be all you need. Spend the extra where the new work sits directly beside the old, where it faces the street, and where planning or a conservation officer will judge it. Put the money where the eye goes.

Frequently asked questions.

How do I find bricks to match my Victorian house?

Start with the wall the new brickwork will sit beside. Measure the brick, the joints and the height of ten courses, note the bond and joint profile, and photograph it dry and wet. Get samples from reclamation yards and makers of handmade stocks, hold them against the wall, then build a sample panel with the chosen brick, mortar, bond and pointing and sign it off before ordering in bulk.

Are reclaimed bricks better than new for a Victorian extension?

Reclaimed London stocks usually give the closest match in colour, texture and size, because they are the same era and already weathered. They vary more, include rejects, need cleaning and are sold in limited lots. New handmade stocks are consistent and easy to order to quantity, but look new for some years and are usually 65mm tall. Choose on the panel, not the brochure.

Why do new bricks not line up with old ones?

A modern UK brick is 65mm tall. Victorian bricks were made to imperial sizes that varied and are often taller, so within a few courses the joints drift apart. Measure the height of ten old courses and choose a brick, or an imperial-size brick, that matches it.

Does planning require matching bricks on an extension?

Under permitted development, the Class A conditions in the General Permitted Development Order 2015 require exterior materials on an extension to be of a similar appearance to the existing house. With full planning permission, materials are judged as part of the design, and conservation-area permissions often require samples to be approved before work above ground begins.

Can mismatched brickwork be fixed after it is built?

Partly. Brick tinting can close a colour gap, and repointing can correct the wrong mortar colour. A size mismatch or the wrong bond cannot be tinted away and usually means rebuilding, which is why a signed-off sample panel comes before the wall.
