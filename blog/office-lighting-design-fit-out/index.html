Office Lighting Design for a Fit-Out — Lux, Glare and Part L

Office lighting design for a fit-out means about 500 lux per desk, no screen glare, controls that switch off empty space, and Part L compliance.

By Vladimir Castravet 20 November 2026 11 min read Updated November 2026
Alexandra · N22 · completed 2VP residential project Photo · office fit-out photo to follow

Office lighting design is the part of a fit-out that decides how much light lands on each desk, how much of it bounces off a screen into your eyes, what switches it on and off, and whether the installation passes Part L of the Building Regulations. Get it right and nobody notices the lighting at all. Get it wrong and you hear about it every day: headaches by the window, a dark meeting room, sensors that turn the lights off on someone typing. This guide covers the numbers that matter, the standards they come from, the controls worth paying for, and the questions to settle with your landlord before a single luminaire moves. 2VP fits out offices and prices lighting packages, so it has an interest in this subject. Read what follows knowing that.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Desks need around 500 lux. BS EN 12464-1, the European standard for indoor workplace lighting, sets a maintained 500 lux for writing, typing, reading and screen work.
  • Glare matters as much as brightness. Desk areas should meet a unified glare rating (UGR) of 19 or lower. Ask for the calculated figure, not the luminaire brochure.
  • Controls pay for themselves. Presence or absence detection and daylight dimming cut energy use in space that is often half empty in a hybrid week.
  • Part L applies to new and replacement lighting. Approved Document L, Volume 2, sets minimum efficiency and controls for lighting in buildings other than dwellings.
  • The landlord owns the starting point. In a Cat A building the ceiling grid, lighting and often the controls are the landlord’s. Changing them needs consent, and may need reinstating at the end of the lease.
  • Emergency lighting moves with your walls. Every new room and corridor has to be checked against BS 5266-1 and your fire risk assessment.

What office lighting design actually means

Office lighting design is a calculated layout, not a choice of fittings. A lighting designer or the electrical contractor models each room, places luminaires, and checks four numbers against the standard: illuminance, uniformity, glare and colour rendering. Then the controls are designed around how you actually use the space.

What is a lux level?

Lux is the amount of light falling on a surface — one lumen per square metre. The figure that matters is maintained illuminance: the level the room must still reach when lamps have aged and fittings are dusty, not on the day they are switched on. A design that only just hits 500 lux on day one will fall below it within a few years.

What is UGR 19?

The unified glare rating is a calculated measure of discomfort glare from the luminaires in a room, seen from a typical seated position. Lower is better. BS EN 12464-1 sets a limit of 19 for most office desk work and meeting rooms. A fitting sold as “UGR<19” was tested in a standard room; your room, ceiling height and layout give a different result, so the figure that counts is the one calculated for your floor.

What is uniformity, and why does it matter?

Uniformity is the ratio of the lowest light level to the average across a task area. A floor that averages 500 lux but drops to 200 between rows of fittings looks patchy and tires the eye. The standard sets a minimum ratio for desk areas, and the calculation should show it.

Colour temperature and colour rendering

Colour temperature, in kelvin, is how warm or cool the light looks. Most offices sit between 3,000K (warmer) and 4,000K (neutral) — that is common practice, not a rule. Colour rendering (Ra or CRI) is how faithfully the light shows colours; BS EN 12464-1 asks for at least Ra 80 in offices. If your team judges colour — design studios, print, retail buying — specify Ra 90.

BS EN 12464-1 is the standard most UK office lighting is designed to. The Society of Light and Lighting, part of CIBSE, publishes the SLL Code for Lighting and Lighting Guide 7: Offices, which interpret it for UK offices. Neither is law on its own; they are how a designer shows lighting is “suitable and sufficient” under the Workplace (Health, Safety and Welfare) Regulations 1992.

When office lighting design applies in a London fit-out

Whenever your fit-out moves, adds or replaces a light fitting, the lighting has to be designed again for the new layout. That happens on almost every Cat B fit-out, and on a surprising number of light refurbishments.

Is lighting included in a Cat A fit-out?

Usually, yes. A Cat A floor normally comes with a suspended or exposed ceiling, a general lighting layout designed for open-plan desks, emergency lighting and the landlord’s controls. The catch is that it was designed for an empty open floor. The moment you add meeting rooms, a kitchen or cellular offices, fittings end up straddling walls, sensors end up in the wrong room, and light levels change. The guide to Cat A vs Cat B office fit-out sets out what the landlord’s spec normally covers.

Does Part L apply to office lighting in a fit-out?

For new or replacement fixed lighting, Part L of the Building Regulations 2010 applies, and Approved Document L, Volume 2, is the guidance for buildings other than dwellings. It sets a minimum efficiency for general lighting, measured in luminaire lumens per circuit-watt, and expects controls such as occupancy and daylight sensing. Simply relocating the landlord’s existing fittings is a different case from installing new ones. Ask your building control body early whether your scope needs notifying; do not assume a lighting change is exempt because no walls move.

Do I need landlord consent to change office lighting?

Almost always. Lighting is wired into the landlord’s distribution boards, often linked to the building management system, and sits in a ceiling the landlord may want back at lease end. Your lease will say whether lighting changes need a formal licence to alter for an office fit-out. Expect the landlord to ask for the lighting layout, product data, the emergency lighting design and the electrical test certificates.

What the workplace and screen rules ask for

Regulation 8 of the Workplace (Health, Safety and Welfare) Regulations 1992 requires suitable and sufficient lighting, by natural light so far as is reasonably practicable. The Health and Safety (Display Screen Equipment) Regulations 1992 add that lighting must not cause glare or distracting reflections on screens. You are the employer, so these duties are yours; good design is how you meet them.

How do I keep glare off screens?

Glare is mostly a layout problem. Four rules fix most of it:

  • Run desks at right angles to the windows, so nobody faces the glass or sits with it behind their screen.
  • Keep luminaires out of the reflection zone. A bright fitting directly behind someone’s chair ends up in their colleague’s screen.
  • Choose low-luminance optics — microprismatic diffusers or louvred fittings — over bare panels where ceilings are low.
  • Treat daylight as a light source. Blinds are part of the lighting design, not an afterthought. A south-facing London floor without them is unusable on a bright winter afternoon.

Which lighting controls are worth paying for?

Presence or absence detection. Presence detection switches lights on and off automatically. Absence detection needs a manual switch-on but switches off when the room empties. Absence is often the better choice in meeting rooms and cellular offices, where people expect a switch.
Daylight dimming. Sensors dim the rows nearest the windows when daylight is doing the work. On a deep London floor, the perimeter zone may need little artificial light for much of the day.
Addressable control (DALI). Each fitting has an address, so zones can be regrouped when you move walls — by reprogramming, not rewiring. Worth it on a floor you expect to re-plan during the lease.
Scene setting in meeting rooms. One button for presentation, one for video calls. Faces need light from the front for a camera, not just from above.

Whatever you choose, it has to be commissioned on site. Sensor time-outs and sensitivity left on factory defaults are why lights go off on people sitting still. Ask for a commissioning record showing the settings for each zone.

What about emergency lighting?

Emergency escape lighting is designed to BS 5266-1 and forms part of the fire strategy your Responsible Person maintains under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005. New partitions create new rooms and new escape routes, so the landlord’s emergency layout has to be checked and extended. The new fittings need a test certificate at handover, and someone on your side needs to own the routine monthly and annual tests.

Does lighting affect acoustics and layout?

Yes, more than people expect. Suspended acoustic rafts compete with luminaires for ceiling space, and a fitting that drops through a raft can undo its absorption. Plan lighting and office acoustics design together, on the same reflected ceiling plan, and settle the open-plan vs cellular office layout before either is designed.

What drives the cost of office lighting design

The cost is set by how far your layout departs from the landlord’s, and by the control system you choose. 2VP does not publish a rate for office lighting; every floor starts from a different Cat A base. These are the drivers to ask a contractor to price line by line:

  1. Reuse versus replace. Relocating the landlord’s fittings on a grid ceiling is the lightest scope. New fittings throughout means a new design, Part L compliance for the new installation, and disposal of the old.
  2. Ceiling type. A 600×600 grid makes moves quick. An exposed soffit means suspended fittings, visible containment and more labour at height.
  3. The control system. Standalone sensors cost less to install; addressable control costs more up front and less every time you re-plan.
  4. Emergency lighting. Each new room or route can add fittings, testing and certification.
  5. The landlord’s specification. Some buildings name the luminaire or control manufacturer you must use, which removes competition on price and can add lead time.
  6. Working hours. Ceiling and electrical work in an occupied building may have to be done out of hours, which changes labour cost and programme.
  7. Reinstatement. If the lease says you hand back the Cat A layout, put the cost of putting it back into your budget now. The guide to office fit-out cost in London shows where lighting sits in the wider budget.

Ask for each of these as a separate line in writing. A single “electrical and lighting” sum hides the decisions you are actually paying for.

Frequently asked questions.

How many lux does an office need?

BS EN 12464-1, the European standard used for UK workplace lighting, sets a maintained illuminance of 500 lux for writing, typing, reading and screen work, and for meeting rooms. Reception desks and filing areas are lower, at 300 lux, and corridors 100 lux. Maintained means the level still reached once fittings have aged and gathered dust, so a design should start above the target.

What is UGR 19 in office lighting?

UGR is the unified glare rating, a calculated measure of discomfort glare from light fittings, seen from a seated position. Lower is better. BS EN 12464-1 sets a limit of 19 for most desk work and meeting rooms. Ask for the UGR calculated for your room and layout, not the figure printed on the product sheet, which was tested in a standard room.

Does Part L apply to office lighting in a fit-out?

For new or replacement fixed lighting, yes. Part L of the Building Regulations 2010 applies, and Approved Document L, Volume 2, sets minimum lighting efficiency and expects controls such as occupancy and daylight sensing in buildings other than dwellings. Whether your scope must be notified to building control depends on what you are changing, so ask your building control body early.

Is lighting included in a Cat A fit-out?

Usually. A Cat A floor normally includes the ceiling, a general lighting layout for open-plan desks, emergency lighting and the landlord’s controls. It is designed for an empty floor, so adding meeting rooms, a kitchen or offices in a Cat B fit-out normally means moving fittings, adding sensors and re-checking emergency lighting.

Do I need landlord consent to change office lighting?

Almost always. Lighting is wired into the landlord’s electrical installation, often linked to the building management system, and sits in a ceiling the landlord may want reinstated at lease end. Check your lease for whether a licence to alter is needed, and expect to provide the layout, product data, emergency lighting design and test certificates.

Which lighting controls are worth it in a small office?

Absence detection in meeting rooms and cellular offices, presence detection in toilets and stores, and daylight dimming in the rows nearest the windows. Addressable control such as DALI costs more to install, but lets you regroup zones by reprogramming when you move walls. Whatever you choose, ask for a commissioning record of the sensor settings.
