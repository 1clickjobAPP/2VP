Listed Building Renovation in London — Consent and What Needs It

Listed building renovation in London needs Listed Building Consent for any work affecting the building’s character, and unauthorised work is a criminal offence.

By Vladimir Castravet 29 November 2026 12 min read Updated November 2026
Chester Square · Belgravia SW1W · townhouse Street elevation · 2VP project

Listed building renovation in London runs on a different rulebook from the rest of the city’s housing. A listed house can be renovated, extended and modernised, but almost every change that affects its character needs Listed Building Consent first, and doing the work without it is a crime, not just a planning breach. The consent covers the inside as well as the outside, and the liability can follow the house to its next owner. This guide sets out what listing protects, what typically needs consent, how to apply without losing a season, and how the build itself has to change. 2VP renovates period houses in London, so it has an interest in the subject. Weigh what follows knowing that. It is a summary, not legal advice.

In 60 seconds — the short answer

  • Listing covers the whole building. Inside and out, including fixtures and, often, older structures in the grounds — not just the features named in the list entry.
  • Consent comes first. Any work of demolition, alteration or extension that affects the building’s character needs Listed Building Consent from your local planning authority, under section 7 of the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Act 1990.
  • Unauthorised works are a criminal offence under section 9 of the same Act, for whoever carries them out or causes them. Not knowing the building was listed is not a defence.
  • Talk to the conservation officer early. Pre-application advice saves rounds of redesign.
  • Build with the house, not against it. Lime, timber and breathable finishes, and repair before replacement.
  • Building Regulations still apply, but the energy rules allow for the building’s historic character.

What a listed building actually means

A listed building is one the Secretary of State has added to the statutory list of buildings of special architectural or historic interest, on the advice of Historic England. Historic England maintains the National Heritage List for England, where you can search any address, and advises local authorities on the most important cases.

Does listing cover the inside of a house?

Yes. Listing protects the whole building, interior and exterior, whatever its grade. It also covers objects and structures fixed to it, and generally older structures within its curtilage, such as a garden wall or outbuilding. The list entry describes the building so it can be identified; it is not a complete inventory of what is protected. A staircase that the entry never mentions is still listed.

Who decides?

Your local planning authority decides Listed Building Consent, normally through its conservation officer. Historic England is consulted on applications for the most important buildings, including Grade I and Grade II*. Most London boroughs publish conservation guidance; some publish guidance for particular estates or streets.

When you need Listed Building Consent in London

You need it for any works of demolition, alteration or extension that would affect the building’s character as a building of special interest. That test is about the building’s significance, not the size of the job: a small change to an original feature can need consent when a larger change to a modern rear addition may not. Your conservation officer applies the test, so ask them, in writing.

What work typically needs Listed Building Consent?

Genuine like-for-like repair in matching materials may not need consent, but “like for like” is judged strictly. If you want certainty that proposed works do not need consent, the Act provides for a certificate of lawfulness of proposed works, applied for to the local planning authority.

Kitchens and bathrooms in a listed house

A new kitchen or bathroom does not automatically need consent, but how it is fitted often does. The questions a conservation officer will ask are practical: does the waste pipe cut through an original joist, does the extract duct need a new hole in a historic wall, are units fixed over panelling or a fireplace? Free-standing furniture-style units, services run in existing voids and extract routed through a later wall usually make consent simpler. Put the kitchen or bathroom in a room of lower significance where you can, such as a later rear addition, and keep the principal rooms for living.

Can I replace windows in a listed building?

Only with consent, and replacing original sashes is often refused. Repair is usually preferred: new sash cords, draught-stripping, splicing in new timber where the frame has rotted. Secondary glazing, fitted discreetly on the inside, is often accepted where double glazing is not, though it still needs consent. Where new windows are allowed, they normally have to match the originals in section, glazing bars and opening. Why unauthorised works are a criminal offence

Under section 9 of the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Act 1990, carrying out works to a listed building without the consent that section 7 requires is a criminal offence. That makes it different from most planning breaches, which are not crimes until an enforcement notice is ignored. Four points matter for a homeowner:

  1. It applies to whoever does or causes the work. The owner who instructs it and the contractor who carries it out can both be liable.
  2. Ignorance is no defence. Not knowing the building was listed does not excuse the offence. Check the National Heritage List before you plan anything.
  3. The penalties are serious. Conviction can bring an unlimited fine, imprisonment, or both.
  4. It follows the house. The local authority can serve a listed building enforcement notice requiring the works to be reversed, and can serve it on the current owner even where a previous owner did the work. If you are buying a listed house, ask the seller’s solicitor for the consents behind every alteration.

Buying a listed house: what to ask before you exchange

Because liability can pass to you, the questions are worth asking before you own the problem:

  • Which alterations have been made, and when? Ask for a list, room by room, with dates.
  • Where are the consents? Each alteration since listing should have a Listed Building Consent decision notice, with its approved drawings and evidence that any conditions were discharged.
  • Is anything outstanding? Ask the local authority whether any enforcement action, or complaint, is open on the property.
  • What does the house actually need? A survey by someone who knows historic buildings will tell you which repairs are urgent, and which modern materials, such as cement render or plastic paint, are already causing damp.

If an alteration has no consent, take legal advice before you exchange. It can sometimes be regularised by a retrospective application, but that is the local authority’s decision, not yours, and you should not assume it.

The Act includes a narrow defence for works urgently needed for safety, health or the preservation of the building, limited to the minimum necessary and notified to the authority in writing as soon as reasonably practicable. It is not a route around consent for planned works.

How to get Listed Building Consent, step by step

  1. Confirm the listing. Search the National Heritage List for England and check whether the house is also in a conservation area or under an Article 4 direction. See our conservation area loft conversion guide for how those rules add up.
  2. Survey before you design. A measured survey and an assessment of what is original, what is later and what condition it is in. This is the evidence your application rests on.
  3. Get pre-application advice. Show the conservation officer the survey and the outline proposal. Their early view shapes the design more cheaply than a refusal.
  4. Write the heritage statement. It explains the building’s significance and how each proposed change affects it, with drawings and photographs. Weak statements are a common reason for delay.
  5. Apply. Listed Building Consent has no application fee; planning permission, if also needed, is applied for alongside it. Allow for at least the statutory eight-week determination period, and longer where Historic England is consulted.
  6. Discharge the conditions. Consents usually come with conditions requiring samples, joinery details or method statements to be approved before the relevant work starts. Build that time into the programme.
  7. Build to the consent. Any change on site that is not covered needs to go back to the conservation officer before it is done, not after.

Do Building Regulations apply to listed buildings?

Yes. Listing does not exempt work from the Building Regulations 2010, so structural work, drainage, electrics, fire safety and new thermal elements still need building control. Approved Document L Volume 1 (2021) recognises that energy efficiency measures must not damage a historic building’s character or fabric, and the aim becomes improving efficiency as far as is reasonably practical. Agree any departure with building control and the conservation officer together. The Party Wall etc. Act 1996 applies as usual; see our party wall agreement guide.

Building with the house, not against it

Many London listed houses are solid-walled brick with lime mortar and lime plaster. They manage moisture by letting it evaporate, not by keeping it out. Cement pointing, gypsum plaster, plastic paints and tanking trap that moisture, and the damp ends up in the timber. Our guide to the damp-proof course explains why bridging and blocked evaporation cause most damp in older houses. Movement cracks need the same care: diagnose the cause before anything is filled — see cracks above windows and doors.

What drives the cost of a listed building renovation

A listed renovation costs more in time and specialist work than an unlisted one of the same size. These are the lines that move it, and each should be priced in writing:

  1. Survey and heritage statement. Done before design, by someone who knows historic fabric.
  2. Consent time. Pre-application advice, determination and conditions all sit before the start date.
  3. Repair over replacement. Splicing, piecing-in and conserving original joinery and plaster takes more hours than renewing it.
  4. Traditional materials and trades. Lime work, matched joinery, cast mouldings and hand-made brick.
  5. Services routed with care. Cables and pipes run under lifted boards and through existing voids rather than chased in.
  6. VAT. Approved alterations to listed homes no longer qualify for zero-rating; ask your accountant how VAT applies to your project.

Frequently asked questions.

What work needs Listed Building Consent?

Any work of demolition, alteration or extension that would affect the building’s character as a building of special interest, inside or out. That commonly includes removing walls, altering staircases, fireplaces, cornices or panelling, replacing windows, changing plaster, pointing or roof coverings, and running new services through historic fabric. Your local planning authority’s conservation officer applies the test.

Is it a criminal offence to alter a listed building?

Yes, if the work needed Listed Building Consent and did not have it. Under section 9 of the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Act 1990 it is an offence for whoever carries out or causes the work, and not knowing the building was listed is no defence. Conviction can bring an unlimited fine, imprisonment or both, and the authority can require the work to be reversed.

Does listing cover the inside of a house?

Yes. Listing protects the whole building, interior and exterior, whatever its grade, plus objects and structures fixed to it and generally older structures within its curtilage. The list entry identifies the building; it is not a complete list of what is protected.

Can I replace windows in a listed building?

Only with consent, and replacing original sash windows is often refused. Repair and draught-stripping are usually preferred, and discreet secondary glazing is often accepted where double glazing is not, though it also needs consent. Where new windows are allowed, they normally have to match the originals.

How long does Listed Building Consent take?

The statutory target for a decision is eight weeks from a valid application, and it can take longer where Historic England is consulted. Add time before that for survey, pre-application advice and the heritage statement, and after it for discharging conditions such as samples and joinery details.

Do Building Regulations apply to listed buildings?

Yes. Listing does not exempt work from the Building Regulations 2010. Approved Document L Volume 1 (2021) allows energy efficiency measures to be balanced against the building’s historic character and fabric, so agree the approach with building control and the conservation officer together.
